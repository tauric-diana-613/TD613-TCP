    "description": "<p>Social Infrastructure Theory (SIT) is a systems‑level framework that explains how everyday environments, institutions, and service pathways shape human outcomes through their underlying infrastructural design. Rather than treating social problems as failures of individual behavior, SIT reframes them as predictable outputs of the infrastructures people must navigate. The theory maps how friction, delay, opacity, and resource scarcity accumulate across systems &mdash; healthcare, education, justice, housing, employment &mdash; and produce patterned harm at scale.</p>\n<p>SIT introduces a structural vocabulary for analyzing these environments: load, capacity, exposure, erosion, and systemic drag. These concepts allow researchers and policymakers to identify where infrastructures fail, who absorbs the cost of those failures, and how harm becomes normalized through routine operations. The theory positions infrastructure not as neutral background, but as an active force that distributes opportunity, risk, and exhaustion.</p>\n<p>By shifting analysis from individuals to systems, SIT provides a diagnostic method for understanding why well‑intentioned reforms often fail, why inequities persist despite policy changes, and why frontline workers and service users experience parallel forms of burnout. It offers a blueprint for evaluating institutional design, predicting failure points, and building environments that reduce harm rather than reproduce it.</p>\n<p>Social Infrastructure Theory is foundational to a broader lineage of frameworks &mdash; including Slow Harm Theory, Systemic Erosion Theory, and Infrastructural Exposure &mdash; that collectively map how modern systems shape human life through their operational logic.</p>\n<p>&nbsp;</p>",
