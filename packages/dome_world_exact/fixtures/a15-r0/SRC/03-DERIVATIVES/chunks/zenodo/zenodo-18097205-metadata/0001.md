    "description": "<p>Slow Harm Theory explains how modern systems generate harm not through dramatic failures, but through the steady accumulation of friction, delay, scarcity, and administrative burden over time. Rather than focusing on acute events, the theory maps how small, routine pressures&mdash;missed calls, long wait times, opaque processes, inconsistent communication, shifting requirements&mdash;compound into measurable degradation of health, stability, and capacity.</p>\n<p>Slow Harm Theory reframes harm as a temporal process embedded in the design of infrastructures. It shows how institutions distribute exhaustion unevenly, placing the greatest load on those with the least buffer: people navigating healthcare, housing, justice, disability services, employment systems, and other essential pathways. The theory identifies how these systems externalize their operational costs onto individuals, who absorb them as stress, burnout, financial strain, and reduced life chances.</p>\n<p>By analyzing harm as something produced gradually rather than suddenly, Slow Harm Theory reveals why systemic inequities persist even in the absence of overt crises. It provides a diagnostic method for identifying where harm accumulates, how it becomes normalized, and why reforms that target only acute failures fail to address the deeper structural mechanisms at work.</p>\n<p>Slow Harm Theory forms a core component of a broader lineage of frameworks&mdash;including Social Infrastructure Theory, Systemic Erosion Theory, Infrastructural Exposure Theory, and the STAR Framework&mdash;that collectively map how modern systems shape human outcomes through their operational logic.</p>\n<p>This essay is part of the SignalRupture canon, a body of work examining contemporary systems, infrastructures, and social dynamics through a conceptual and diagnostic lens. Each piece contributes to an ongoing analysis of structural stress, digital environments, governance patterns, and the evolving relationship between individuals and large‑scale systems. The work combines theoretical reflection with infrastructural observation, offering frameworks for understanding systemic erosion, cultural shifts, and emerging forms of social complexity.</p>",
