Infrastructural Exposure Theory: How Systems Generate Harm Through Designed Contact Abstract Infrastructural Exposure Theory explains how systems generate harm by exposing individuals and

communities

to

conditions

that

degrade

health,

stability,

and

agency.

Building

on

Social

Infrastructure

Theory

(SIT),

this

framework

identifies

exposure

as

a

primary

mechanism

through

which

institutions

produce

predictable

human

outcomes.

Infrastructural

exposure

includes

contact

with

instability,

scarcity,

surveillance,

bureaucratic

friction,

and

environmental

or

social

hazards

embedded

within

system

design.

This

theory

also

integrates

the

concept

of

erosion,

demonstrating

how

repeated

exposure

leads

to

cumulative

depletion

of

capacity

and

resilience

over

time.

By

infrastructural

exposure

as

a

distinct

construct,

this

work

provides

a

foundation

for

analyzing

how

systems

shape

lived

experience

through

designed

contact,

and

how

exposure

interacts

with

slow

harm

and

systemic

erosion

to

produce

long ‑ term

injury. Keywords infrastructural exposure; Social Infrastructure Theory; erosion; structural violence; administrative burden;

cumulative

harm;

system

design 1. Introduction Systems do not only harm through scarcity, delay, or bureaucratic friction. They also harm through

exposure—the

conditions

they

place

people

in,

the

risks

they

normalize,

and

the

environments

they

require

individuals

to

navigate.

Infrastructural

exposure

refers

to

the

predictable

contact

individuals

have

with

harmful

or

degrading

conditions

as

a

result

of

system

design. This theory extends Social Infrastructure Theory (SIT) by identifying exposure as a primary mechanism

through

which

systems

shape

human

outcomes.

It

also

introduces

erosion

as

a

secondary

effect:

repeated

exposure

gradually

wears

down

capacity,

stability,

and

resilience,

creating

long ‑ term

injury

that

appears

self ‑ generated

rather

than

system ‑ produced. 2. Literature Gap Existing scholarship addresses fragments of exposure—environmental hazards, administrative burden,

structural

violence—but

no

unified

framework

explains

exposure

as

a

system ‑ level

mechanism

across

environmental,

bureaucratic,

and

social

infrastructures.

Infrastructural

Exposure Theory fills this gap by naming exposure as a distinct, measurable, and intentional output

of

system

design. 3. Defining Infrastructural Exposure Infrastructural exposure is the system ‑ generated contact individuals have with harmful conditions

embedded

in

institutional,

social,

or

environmental

infrastructures.

These

exposures

include: • instability • scarcity • surveillance • bureaucratic friction • environmental hazards Exposure is not incidental. It is a designed feature of systems optimized for efficiency, control, or cost ‑ containment

rather

than

care. 4. A Typology of Infrastructural Exposure 4.1 Material Exposure Contact with physical or environmental hazards (pollution, unsafe housing, degraded infrastructure). 4.2 Administrative Exposure Contact with bureaucratic friction, paperwork, delays, and compliance requirements. 4.3 Temporal Exposure Exposure to waiting, unpredictability, and time ‑ based harm. 4.4 Social Exposure Exposure to stigmatization, policing, surveillance, or exclusion. 4.5 Economic Exposure

Exposure to precarity, resource depletion, and financial instability. This typology provides a structured way to analyze exposure across systems. 5. Theoretical Foundations Infrastructural Exposure Theory synthesizes insights from structural violence (Galtung, 1969), slow

violence

(Nixon,

2011),

administrative

burden

(Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018),

and

(Rodgers

It

extends

these

frameworks

by

identifying

exposure

as

a

primary

mechanism

rather

than

a

secondary

effect

and

by

integrating

erosion

as

a

cumulative

outcome. 6. Exposure as a System Output Under SIT, systems are understood as architectures that produce predictable human outcomes. Exposure

is

one

of

those

outputs. Systems expose individuals to harmful conditions when they are underfunded, understaffed, poorly

designed,

or

optimized

for

efficiency

rather

than

care. Exposure is therefore not a failure of the system. It is the function of the system. 7. Mechanisms of Infrastructural Exposure 7.1 Exposure to Instability Systems often require individuals to navigate unstable conditions—temporary housing, inconsistent

services,

unpredictable

wait

times.

Instability

becomes

a

form

of

harm

when

it

erodes

the

ability

to

plan,

work,

or

maintain

health. 7.2 Exposure to Scarcity Scarcity is frequently framed as natural, but it is often a policy choice. Exposure to scarcity forces

individuals

into

harmful

trade ‑ offs,

accelerating

erosion

of

financial

and

emotional

resources. 7.3 Exposure to Surveillance

Surveillance—through monitoring, reporting, or compliance requirements—creates psychological

strain

and

restricts

autonomy.

Over

time,

this

exposure

erodes

trust,

agency,

and

mental

health. 7.4 Exposure to Bureaucratic Friction Administrative burden exposes individuals to repeated obstacles that consume time, energy, and

resources

(Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018).

This

friction

produces

erosion

by

wearing

down

capacity

and

resilience. 7.5 Exposure to Environmental Hazards Environmental exposures—pollution, unsafe neighborhoods, degraded infrastructure—are disproportionately

distributed

along

lines

of

race,

class,

and

geography

(Nixon,

2011).

These

exposures

accumulate,

producing

long ‑ term

erosion

of

health. 8. Exposure and Erosion: The Cumulative Effect Exposure is the contact; erosion is the consequence. Repeated exposure depletes: • health • stability • resources • resilience • agency This cumulative depletion mirrors Slow Harm Theory but with a distinct emphasis: slow harm describes

the

injury,

while

exposure

describes

the

conditions

that

produce

the

injury. 9. Boundary Conditions Infrastructural exposure does not always produce harm. Key moderating factors include: • access to buffering resources • strong social networks • institutional flexibility • community ‑ based supports

• protective policy environments These conditions determine whether exposure leads to erosion or is mitigated. 10. Causal Model (Text ‑ Based Diagram) Infrastructure Design ↓ Infrastructural Exposure ↓ Slow Harm Processes (delay, scarcity, friction) ↓ Systemic Erosion (capacity depletion) ↓ Long ‑ Term Outcomes (health, stability, agency) 11. Signature Example: Housing Assistance Systems A housing assistance system exposes individuals to: • instability (temporary shelters) • scarcity (limited vouchers) • surveillance (home inspections, reporting) • bureaucratic friction (paperwork, waitlists) • environmental hazards (substandard housing) Over time, these exposures erode: • financial stability • mental health • social ties • long ‑ term resilience This example demonstrates the full causal chain: exposure → slow harm → erosion → outcome.

12. Methodological Invitation Infrastructural Exposure Theory invites researchers to: • measure exposure density • analyze exposure pathways • map exposure architecture • quantify erosion effects • evaluate policy designs that reduce harmful exposures This opens pathways for qualitative, quantitative, and mixed ‑ methods research. 13.
