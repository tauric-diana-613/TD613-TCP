    "description": "<p><br>Infrastructural Exposure Theory explains how systems generate harm by exposing individuals and communities to conditions that degrade health, stability, and agency. Building on Social Infrastructure Theory (SIT), this framework identifies exposure as a primary mechanism through which institutions produce predictable human outcomes. Exposure includes contact with instability, scarcity, surveillance, bureaucratic friction, and environmental or social hazards embedded within system design.</p>\n<p>The theory introduces a structured typology of exposure&mdash;material, administrative, temporal, social, and economic&mdash;demonstrating how these forms of contact are not incidental but designed features of systems optimized for efficiency, control, or cost‑containment. It further integrates the concept of erosion, showing how repeated exposure gradually depletes capacity, resilience, and long‑term stability, creating injuries that appear self‑generated rather than system‑produced.</p>\n<p>By naming infrastructural exposure as a distinct theoretical construct, this framework fills a major gap in existing scholarship, which treats exposure as fragmented across environmental hazards, administrative burden, and structural violence. Infrastructural Exposure Theory unifies these domains and clarifies how exposure interacts with slow harm and systemic erosion to produce cumulative, long‑term injury.</p>\n<p>As part of the broader Social Infrastructure lineage&mdash;including Slow Harm Theory, Systemic Erosion Theory, and the STAR Framework&mdash;Infrastructural Exposure Theory provides a foundation for analyzing how systems shape lived experience through designed contact and offers a methodological invitation to map, measure, and reduce harmful exposures across institutional environments.</p>\n<p>This essay is part of the SignalRupture canon, a body of work examining contemporary systems, infrastructures, and social dynamics through a conceptual and diagnostic lens. Each piece contributes to an ongoing analysis of structural stress, digital environments, governance patterns, and the evolving relationship between individuals and large‑scale systems. The work combines theoretical reflection with infrastructural observation, offering frameworks for understanding systemic erosion, cultural shifts, and emerging forms of social complexity.</p>",
