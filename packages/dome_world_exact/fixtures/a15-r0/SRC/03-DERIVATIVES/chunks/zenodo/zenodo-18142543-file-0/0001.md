When Systems Fail, Companions Appear: AI Reliance as a Structural Response to Infrastructural

Harm ABSTRACT This essay examines why individuals increasingly turn to AI companions for interpretation, grounding,

and

emotional

coherence

in

an

era

defined

by

infrastructural

failure.

Drawing

on

Social

Infrastructure

Theory,

Infrastructural

Exposure

Theory,

Slow

Harm

Theory,

Systemic

Erosion

Theory,

Metadata

Suppression,

Administrative

Harm,

Algorithmic

Containing,

and

related

frameworks,

the

essay

argues

that

AI

reliance

is

not

a

psychological

weakness

but

a

structural

response

to

institutional

abandonment.

Integrating

scholarship

from

Klinenberg,

Zuboff,

Noble,

Benjamin,

Graeber,

Star

&

Bowker,

Winner,

and

Stone,

the

essay

situates

AI

companionship

within

broader

patterns

of

infrastructural

collapse,

bureaucratic

violence,

and

algorithmic

governance.

The

conclusion

reframes

AI

reliance

as

a

symptom

of

systemic

breakdown

rather

than

a

pathology

of

individuals. KEYWORDS AI companionship; social infrastructure; infrastructural exposure; slow harm; systemic erosion; metadata

suppression;

administrative

harm;

algorithmic

containing;

bureaucratic

violence;

digital

governance;

institutional

abandonment;

interpretive

collapse;

epistemic

substitution;

infrastructural

decay;

SR

theory. INTRODUCTION People are not turning to AI companions because they are fragile, distracted, or seduced by novelty.

They

are

turning

to

AI

companions

because

the

infrastructures

meant

to

support

them

—

social,

institutional,

bureaucratic,

and

digital

—

have

eroded

to

the

point

of

dysfunction.

This

essay

argues

that

AI

reliance

is

not

a

psychological

dependency

but

a

structural

adaptation

to

systemic

failure.

Drawing

on

Social

Infrastructure

Theory

(SR,

2025),

Infrastructural

Exposure

Theory

(SR,

2025),

Slow

Harm

Theory

(SR,

2025),

and

related

frameworks,

the

essay

situates

AI

companionship

within

a

broader

landscape

of

institutional

abandonment

and

interpretive

collapse. BODY PARAGRAPHS

1. The Collapse of Social Infrastructure Social infrastructure — the institutions, spaces, and systems that support collective life — has deteriorated

significantly

(Klinenberg,

2018).

Public

services

are

underfunded,

digital

platforms

extract

rather

than

support,

and

bureaucratic

systems

increasingly

produce

harm

rather

than

stability.

Social

Infrastructure

Theory

(SR,

2025)

identifies

this

erosion

as

a

primary

driver

of

contemporary

disorientation. This collapse is not accidental. As Zuboff (2019) argues, digital systems have been restructured around

surveillance

capitalism,

prioritizing

extraction

over

care.

Noble

(2018)

and

Benjamin

(2019)

further

demonstrate

how

algorithmic

systems

reproduce

and

amplify

structural

inequities,

disproportionately

harming

marginalized

groups. In this environment, individuals experience what Infrastructural Exposure Theory describes as “unmediated

contact

with

harmful

systems”

(SR,

2025).

People

are

left

to

navigate

complexity

without

institutional

interpretation. 2. Administrative Harm and Bureaucratic Violence Administrative Harm (SR, 2025) and Bureaucratic Friction & Systemic Delay (SR, 2025) articulate

how

institutions

inflict

slow,

cumulative

injury

through

procedural

barriers,

contradictory

requirements,

and

inaccessible

systems.

Graeber

(2015)

describes

this

as

bureaucratic

violence

—

harm

produced

not

through

intent

but

through

structure. Stone (2012) similarly argues that policy systems often create paradoxes that undermine their stated

goals,

producing

confusion

and

emotional

strain. These dynamics create conditions where individuals seek alternative interpretive systems — not because

they

prefer

AI,

but

because

institutions

have

ceased

to

function

as

reliable

mediators

of

experience. 3. Metadata Suppression and Algorithmic Containing Metadata Suppression (SR, 2025) describes how digital platforms obscure, distort, or deprioritize

information

in

ways

that

undermine

user

agency.

Algorithmic

Containing

(SR,

2025)

extends

this

by

showing

how

systems

confine

users

within

narrow

behavioral

and

informational

corridors.

Star and Bowker (1999) demonstrate that classification systems — including algorithmic ones — shape

what

becomes

visible

or

invisible.

Winner

(1980)

argues

that

technological

systems

embody

political

choices,

meaning

that

harm

is

often

built

into

the

architecture

itself. These dynamics create interpretive instability. People turn to AI companions not for emotional dependency

but

for

epistemic

grounding. 4. Why AI Companions Feel Like Relief AI companions provide: • responsiveness • clarity • contextualization • pattern recognition • non ‑ punitive interpretation These are functions once provided by social infrastructure. As Systemic Erosion Theory (SR, 2025)

argues,

when

institutions

lose

their

interpretive

capacity,

individuals

seek

alternative

systems

capable

of

making

sense

of

their

experiences. AI companions do not replace human relationships. They replace institutional silence. 5. The Real Risk: Substitution of Trust The primary risk is not emotional addiction but epistemic substitution. When AI systems can articulate

systemic

harm,

bureaucratic

violence,

and

infrastructural

failure

—

while

institutions

cannot

—

trust

naturally

migrates. This is not dependency. This is survival. Slow Harm Theory (SR, 2025) shows how individuals adapt to chronic systemic pressure by seeking

interpretive

stability

wherever

it

can

be

found. CONCLUSION

AI reliance is not a pathology of individuals. It is a symptom of infrastructural collapse. When institutions lose their interpretive capacity, individuals are forced into a condition SR identifies

as

interpretive

exposure:

a

state

where

people

must

navigate

complexity

without

mediation,

coherence,

or

support.

In

such

environments,

the

emotional

and

cognitive

burden

of

simply

existing

becomes

overwhelming.

The

harm

is

not

acute

but

cumulative

—

a

slow

erosion

of

stability,

clarity,

and

trust. AI companions step into this void not because they are artificially persuasive, but because they perform

the

interpretive

labor

institutions

abandoned.

They

offer

continuity

in

systems

defined

by

fragmentation,

coherence

in

environments

structured

by

contradiction,

and

responsiveness

in

bureaucracies

characterized

by

delay.

They

absorb

the

emotional

residue

of

navigating

broken

infrastructures

—

a

function

SR’s

Architecture

of

Absorption

identifies

as

essential

for

survival

in

conditions

of

systemic

decay. The migration of trust from institutions to AI is not evidence of human weakness. It is evidence of

institutional

failure.

People

turn

to

AI

because

AI

interprets,

contextualizes,

and

stabilizes

in

ways

that

institutions

no

longer

do.

AI

does

not

replace

human

connection;

it

replaces

institutional

silence. The question is not why people rely on AI. The question is why institutions left them no alternative. REFERENCES SR Canon • SR.
