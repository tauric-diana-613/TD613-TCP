The Worker’s Lived Experience of Workplace Law Workers experience workplace law not as protection but as: • delayed investigations • inaccessible accommodations • procedural barriers • fear of retaliation • opaque decision ‑ making • credibility hierarchies • legal timelines that exceed human endurance From the worker’s perspective, law often feels like containment, not protection. It is experienced as a system that exhausts, delays, and erodes rather than safeguards.

The Legal Architecture of Harm 1. Law as a Mechanism of Delay Legal processes—grievances, investigations, appeals, accommodations—operate on timelines that

Law as a Mechanism of Scarcity Legal frameworks often define minimum standards rather than optimal conditions. This creates: • chronic understaffing • resource depletion • normalized overwork • legally sanctioned scarcity Scarcity becomes lawful, even when it is harmful. 3. Law as a Mechanism of Exposure Policies and regulations expose workers to: • surveillance • disciplinary processes • performance metrics • administrative scrutiny Exposure becomes a structural condition, not an incidental risk (SR, 2025).

4. Law as a Mechanism of Erosion Legal compliance requirements—documentation, reporting, procedural adherence—erode worker

Law as a Mechanism of Containment Workers are contained through: • contracts • non ‑ compete clauses • rigid hierarchies • procedural traps • fear of retaliation Containment prevents escape, adaptation, or recovery (SR, 2025). Legal Fictions as Instruments of Harm Workplace law relies on legal fictions—concepts that appear neutral but function to legitimize harm: • “Reasonable person” → erases context and power • “Due process” → masks delay as fairness • “Objective investigation” → hides credibility hierarchies • “Neutral policy” → obscures structural bias • “Standard procedure” → normalizes erosion

Legal fictions convert harm into administrative inevitability, making injury appear lawful, rational, and

deserved. Worker Strategies Under Legal Constraint Workers adapt to legal architectures in predictable ways: • self ‑ censoring to avoid retaliation • over ‑ documenting to protect themselves • avoiding reporting because processes are harmful • internalizing blame when systems deny recognition • exiting the workplace when containment becomes intolerable These strategies reveal how law shapes behavior long before it intervenes. Conceptual Model (Described) The Workplace Law Model consists of three interacting layers: 1. Legal Design → Defines permissible harm. 2. Administrative Enforcement → Operationalizes harm through procedures. 3. Worker Impact → Produces erosion, illness, and containment. This model shows how law functions as the infrastructural skeleton of workplace harm. Workplace Law as a Health Determinant Legal structures shape: • access to accommodations • exposure to risk • timelines for intervention

Administrative Harm as Legal Enforcement As Administrative Harm (SR, 2025) argues, bureaucratic systems produce injury through: • procedural loops • documentation burdens • eligibility traps • surveillance mechanisms Workplace law provides the legal authority for these mechanisms to operate. Implications for Practice Recognizing workplace law as a structural mechanism of harm requires organizations to: • redesign legal processes to reduce delay • align procedural timelines with human timelines • eliminate redundant documentation • simplify grievance and accommodation pathways • shift from punitive to supportive legal frameworks • treat legal burden as a health risk, not a compliance issue These changes reposition law as a protective function rather than a mechanism of harm.
