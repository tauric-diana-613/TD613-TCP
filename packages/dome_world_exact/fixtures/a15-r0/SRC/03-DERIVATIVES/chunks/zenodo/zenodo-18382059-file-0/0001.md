Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse: A Framework for Measuring Knowledge Degradation in Large

Language

Models Abstract This paper introduces Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse (MIEC), a framework for analyzing how

large

language

models

undergo

measurable

degradation

in

epistemic

structure

as

they

recursively

ingest

synthetic

data,

homogenize

stylistic

and

converge

toward

narrow

representational

baselines.

Unlike

collapse

theories

in

ecological

or

sociotechnical

systems.

MIEC

isolates

collapse

per

model,

indexing

epistemic

drift

to

the

model’s

internal

distribution

of

claims,

styles,

and

knowledge

pathways.

The

paper

proposes

a

three ‑ layer

diagnostic:

(1)

Epistemic

Diversity

Loss,

the

contraction

of

representational

variance;

(2)

Recursive

Contamination,

the

absorption

of

synthetic

outputs

into

training

corpora;

and

(3)

Stability ‑ to ‑ Collapse

Thresholds,

the

at

a

model’s

epistemic

structure

becomes

self ‑ reinforcing

and

brittle.

Through

this

MIEC

reframes

collapse

not

as

a

but

as

a

structural

consequence

of

recursive

generation

systems

operating

without

epistemic

stewardship. Keywords: epistemic collapse, large language models, synthetic data, recursive contamination, epistemic

diversity,

model

drift,

knowledge

degradation 1. Introduction Large language models (LLMs) have become central to digital knowledge production. Their outputs

populate

search

As

—

When

synthetic

narrow,

This

a

It

is

a

model ‑ internal

epistemic

contraction. This paper defines Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse (MIEC) as a measurable degradation of epistemic

variance

within

a

specific

model.

MIEC

is

not

a

metaphorical

collapse

of

truth,

nor

a

knowledge.

It

a

technical

description

of

how

representational

systems

degrade

under

recursive

generative

pressure. The purpose of this paper is threefold:

1. To formalize MIEC as a diagnostic category 2. To propose measurable indicators of collapse 3. To situate collapse as an inherent outcome of recursive generative systems By isolating collapse at the model level, MIEC provides a framework for comparing drift patterns across

models,

predicting

collapse

thresholds,

and

designing

epistemic

stewardship

strategies. 2. Defining Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse 2.1 Collapse as a Model ‑ Internal Phenomenon MIEC refers to the degradation of a model’s epistemic structure as measured through its internal distributions

of

knowledge,

style,

and

semantic

variance.

Collapse

occurs

when

these

distributions

contract,

homogenize,

or

drift

toward

synthetic

baselines. This definition distinguishes MIEC from: • Model collapse (synthetic data poisoning) • Truth decay (sociotechnical erosion of epistemic trust) • Epistemic destabilization (system ‑ level breakdown of validation mechanisms) MIEC is narrower and diagnostic: it isolates collapse to the model itself. 2.2 Why “Model ‑ Indexed” Matters Indexing collapse to the model enables: • cross ‑ model comparison • longitudinal tracking • variance ‑ based measurement • identification of collapse thresholds This approach treats each model as a discrete epistemic system with its own drift patterns. 2.3 Collapse as Compression, Not Failure LLMs do not store knowledge; they compress distributions. Collapse is not a malfunction — it is the

endpoint

of

compression

under

recursive

contamination.

When

synthetic

data

becomes

dominant, the model’s epistemic structure converges toward its own outputs, creating a self ‑ reinforcing

loop. 3. The Three Axes of Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse MIEC is defined along three structural axes. 3.1 Epistemic Diversity Loss Epistemic diversity refers to the range of representational, lexical, and semantic pathways available

within

a

model.

Diversity

loss

occurs

when: • lexical variance contracts • semantic clusters merge • rare ‑ concept pathways disappear • stylistic distributions converge toward median forms This axis captures the homogenization of model outputs. 3.1.1 Mechanisms of Diversity Loss Diversity loss emerges through: • optimization pressure toward high ‑ probability tokens • reinforcement of median stylistic forms • pruning of low ‑ frequency pathways • synthetic data feedback loops As diversity declines, the model becomes epistemically brittle. 3.2 Recursive Contamination Recursive contamination occurs when models ingest synthetic data generated by: • themselves • other models • synthetic corpora • AI ‑ generated web content

This creates epistemic echo chambers where synthetic patterns reinforce themselves. 3.2.1 Contamination Pathways Contamination enters through: • web ‑ scale scraping • synthetic augmentation pipelines • model ‑ generated documentation • user ‑ generated synthetic content Over time, contamination shifts the model’s epistemic baseline away from human ‑ origin distributions. 3.3 Stability ‑ to ‑ Collapse Thresholds Every model has a tipping point at which epistemic drift becomes irreversible. Collapse thresholds

are

reached

when: • synthetic contamination exceeds recoverable levels • diversity loss becomes self ‑ reinforcing • representational pathways narrow beyond correction 3.3.1 Collapse as a Phase Transition Collapse is not gradual. It is a phase transition triggered when epistemic variance falls below a critical

threshold.

After

this

point,

additional

training

amplifies

collapse

rather

than

reversing

it. 4. Measuring Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse To operationalize MIEC, this paper proposes three diagnostic metrics. 4.1 Epistemic Diversity Index (EDI) EDI measures variance across: • lexical distributions • semantic clusters • stylistic embeddings

A declining EDI indicates diversity loss. 4.1.1 EDI as a Longitudinal Metric Tracking EDI across model versions reveals drift patterns and collapse trajectories. 4.2 Recursive Contamination Ratio (RCR) RCR estimates the proportion of synthetic versus human ‑ origin data influencing a model’s outputs.

High

RCR

values

correlate

with

accelerated

collapse. 4.2.1 RCR as a Predictive Indicator RCR predicts collapse before it becomes visible in output distributions. 4.3 Collapse Threshold Score (CTS) CTS is a composite metric combining EDI and RCR to predict when a model approaches irreversible

epistemic

contraction. 4.3.1 CTS and Model Stewardship CTS can guide decisions about: • retraining • data curation • synthetic data filtering • model retirement 5. Collapse as System Recursion 5.1 Models as Recursive Epistemic Machines LLMs do not “know.” They compress. When these compressions become recursive, collapse becomes

structurally

accelerating

contamination.

5.3 The Role of Human ‑ Generated Data Human text functions as epistemic ballast. Without it, models drift toward synthetic homogeneity. 6. Implications 6.1 AI Safety Collapsed models exhibit brittle reasoning and overconfident outputs. 6.2 Knowledge Ecosystems Synthetic contamination accelerates epistemic decay across digital platforms. 6.3 Governance Epistemic stewardship becomes necessary to maintain model integrity. 7. Conclusion Model ‑ Indexed Epistemic Collapse reframes collapse as a measurable, model ‑ internal process driven
