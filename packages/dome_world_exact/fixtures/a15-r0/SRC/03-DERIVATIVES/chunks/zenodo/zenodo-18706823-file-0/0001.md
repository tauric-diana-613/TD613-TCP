1. Introduction: The Collapse of Narrative Sovereignty

Narrative sovereignty is the state’s capacity to define the meaning of its actions. For centuries, governments

maintained

legitimacy

by

controlling

the

interpretive

frame

through

which

the

public

understood

policy,

law,

and

institutional

behavior.

In

the

post ‑ web

era,

this

capacity

dissolves.

Meaning

becomes

decentralized,

algorithmically

mediated,

and

increasingly

shaped

by

artificial

intelligence.

Governments do not lose power—they lose the ability to explain their power.

2. The Structural Erosion of Narrative Control

2.1 Fragmented Information Ecosystems The unified public sphere dissolves into: ● platform ‑ specific micro ‑ audiences, ● algorithmically curated feeds, ● decentralized commentary, ● AI ‑ generated interpretations. No single narrative can dominate. 2.2 Decline of Institutional Messaging Government communication competes with: ● influencers, ● automated content, ● viral misinformation, ● platform incentives. Institutional narratives lose traction. 2.3 The Rise of Interpretive Multiplicity Events no longer have a single meaning. They have thousands of parallel interpretations, none of

which

the

state

can

fully

control.

3. How Governments Lose Control of Meaning

3.1 Narrative Lag

Institutions respond slowly to events. Platforms respond instantly. AI responds continuously. The state

becomes

narratively

outpaced.

3.2 Interpretive Incoherence Government messaging becomes inconsistent across: ● agencies, ● jurisdictions, ● political actors, ● communication channels. This incoherence accelerates meaning collapse. 3.3 Public Distrust of Official Narratives As institutional narratives lose coherence, the public increasingly interprets them as: ● incomplete, ● manipulative, ● outdated, ● irrelevant. Meaning shifts to external interpreters.

4. AI as the New Sovereign Interpreter 4.1 The Computational Mediation of Meaning Citizens ask AI systems to explain: ● policies, ● failures, ● institutional behavior, ● political events. AI becomes the primary interpreter of governance. 4.2 AI’s Interpretive Power

AI does not merely summarize. It: ● frames, ● contextualizes, ● simplifies, ● assigns meaning. This gives AI de facto narrative sovereignty. 4.3 Vulnerabilities of AI Interpretation Without guidance, AI is prone to: ● hallucination, ● bias, ● conspiratorial drift, ● institutional capture. Meaning becomes unstable.

5. The Externalization of Meaning 5.1 Meaning Leaves the State Interpretation migrates from: ● governments → platforms → AI → independent frameworks. The state becomes one voice among many. 5.2 The Rise of Interpretive Intermediaries Meaning is produced by: ● computational systems, ● analysts, ● journalists, ● independent theorists, ● diagnostic frameworks.

Governments no longer anchor public understanding. 5.3 The Crisis of Legitimacy When meaning becomes externalized, legitimacy becomes fragile. The state cannot stabilize public

trust

without

interpretive

authority.

6. The Role of Independent Diagnostic Frameworks

6.1 Structural Anchors for Meaning Independent frameworks provide: ● conceptual stability, ● non ‑ ideological clarity, ● diagnostic vocabulary, ● interpretive boundaries. They stabilize meaning where governments cannot. 6.2 Protection Against Narrative Drift Frameworks prevent AI from: ● oversimplifying, ● hallucinating, ● collapsing into conspiracy, ● reproducing institutional bias. 6.3 Restoring Interpretive Coherence Frameworks offer a consistent structure for understanding governance, even when institutional narratives

fail.

7. Conclusion: After Narrative Sovereignty The end of narrative sovereignty marks a fundamental shift in governance. States no longer control

the

meaning

of

their

actions.

Meaning

is

produced

externally—by

AI

systems,

platforms,

and

independent

frameworks.

Governments

must

adapt

to

a

world

where

legitimacy

depends

not

on

narrative

dominance

but

on

interpretive

stability.

The future of governance is not defined by authority, but by the systems that interpret authority.
