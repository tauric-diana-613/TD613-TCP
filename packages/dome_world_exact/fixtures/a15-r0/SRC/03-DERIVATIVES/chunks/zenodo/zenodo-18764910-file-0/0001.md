The Coercive Economy: When Work Becomes

a

Containment

System

A SignalRupture Canonical Essay Abstract This essay examines how modern economies use work not as a path to stability or autonomy but

as

a

containment

system.

Institutions

frame

employment

as

the

foundation

of

dignity,

purpose,

and

upward

mobility,

yet

the

lived

reality

for

millions

is

the

opposite:

work

functions

as

a

mechanism

of

control,

dependency,

and

exhaustion.

Low

wages,

unpredictable

schedules,

toxic

management,

and

rising

living

costs

ensure

that

employment

traps

people

in

cycles

of

survival

rather

than

enabling

freedom.

This

piece

maps

how

the

coercive

economy

operates

—

not

through

explicit

force,

but

through

structural

design

that

makes

exit

impossible

and

compliance

mandatory.

It

argues

that

work

has

shifted

from

a

source

of

empowerment

to

a

system

of

captivity,

maintained

by

economic

necessity

and

institutional

rhetoric.

1. Work as a Containment System, Not a Path

to

Freedom

Work is framed as the primary route to autonomy — the means by which individuals secure housing,

food,

healthcare,

and

dignity.

But

when

wages

stagnate

and

the

cost

of

survival

rises,

work

becomes

a

trap.

People

are

not

working

toward

freedom;

they

are

working

to

avoid

collapse.

Employment becomes a containment system when the consequences of leaving are catastrophic.

The

system

does

not

need

to

force

compliance

—

it

simply

ensures

that

exit

is

impossible.

2. Low Wages as Structural Captivity Low wages are often portrayed as market outcomes, but they function as a governance tool. When

wages

are

insufficient

to

cover

basic

needs,

workers

lose

the

ability

to:

● refuse exploitation ● negotiate conditions ● leave toxic environments ● pursue education or training ● rest or recover Low wages keep people in place. They transform work from a choice into a dependency.

3. Unpredictable Schedules and the Erosion

of

Stability

Unpredictable schedules — last ‑ minute shifts, on ‑ call demands, fluctuating hours — destabilize workers’

lives.

They

make

it

impossible

to:

● plan childcare ● maintain a second job ● attend school ● rest consistently ● build routines Instability is not a side effect of the system. It is a feature that keeps workers available, compliant,

and

exhausted.

4. Toxic Workplaces as Behavioral Control

Toxic workplaces — marked by surveillance, understaffing, retaliation, and burnout — function as

micro ‑ systems

of

coercion.

Workers

endure

these

conditions

because

the

alternative

is

unemployment,

homelessness,

or

hunger.

The system frames endurance as resilience. In reality, it is captivity.

5. The Rising Cost of Living as an Enforcement

Mechanism

When housing, food, transportation, and healthcare become unaffordable, work becomes mandatory

for

survival.

The

cost

of

living

enforces

compliance

more

effectively

than

any

law.

A person who cannot afford to miss a paycheck cannot resist exploitation. They cannot strike, organize,

or

demand

better

conditions.

They

can

only

endure.

The system uses economic pressure as a silent form of discipline.

6. The Myth of Work as Moral Virtue Institutions moralize work as a virtue — a sign of character, discipline, and worth. This narrative obscures

the

reality

that

many

jobs

are:

● underpaid ● unsafe ● degrading ● physically harmful ● psychologically damaging The moralization of work protects the system by framing exploitation as honor and exhaustion as

pride.

7. When Work Consumes Life, Autonomy Disappears

A person who spends most of their time working, commuting, recovering from work, or worrying about

work

has

no

capacity

for:

● political participation ● community engagement ● long ‑ term planning ● creative pursuits ● rest or reflection Work becomes the architecture of their existence. Autonomy collapses under the weight of survival.

8. The Coercive Economy Needs You to Stay

Contained

The coercive economy relies on a workforce that is: ● exhausted ● indebted ● dependent ● afraid to leave ● unable to rest This is not a failure of the system. It is the system. Work is no longer a path to freedom. It is the mechanism that prevents it.
