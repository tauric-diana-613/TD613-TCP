Systems

1. Introduction: Rights Without Infrastructure

Modern societies often celebrate progress through the language of rights: women’s rights, civil rights,

disability

rights,

LGBTQ+

rights,

migrant

protections.

Yet

these

rights

operate

atop

infrastructures

built

in

eras

where

only

a

narrow

subset

of

the

population—typically

men,

typically

white,

typically

property ‑ owning—were

recognized

as

full

legal

subjects.

The result is a paradox: Marginalized groups have rights on paper but encounter systems that were never rebuilt to

support

them.

This is why progress feels uneven, fragile, or performative. The architecture beneath the rights remains

unchanged.

2. Historical Defaults: Who the System Was

Built

For

For centuries, institutional design reflected a single dominant subject: ● Men were the only legal persons. ● Men controlled property and inheritance. ● Men held political power. ● Men defined harm, legitimacy, and authority. ● Men shaped the rules of speech, mobility, and participation. These defaults became embedded in: ● legal codes ● bureaucratic processes ● workplace norms ● educational structures ● medical systems ● digital platforms Even as laws changed, the infrastructure remained anchored to its original assumptions.

3. Mirage Policies: Symbolic Inclusion Without

Structural

Change

Many institutions adopt policies that appear progressive: ● diversity statements ● gender equity pledges ● anti ‑ racism commitments ● accessibility guidelines ● inclusive language protocols

But these policies often function as mirage rights —symbolic gestures that do not alter the underlying

architecture.

They

create

the

appearance

of

fairness

while

leaving

the

structural

barriers

intact.

Examples include: ● workplaces with parental leave policies but no childcare infrastructure ● universities with anti ‑ harassment rules but opaque reporting systems ● governments with disability rights but inaccessible public services ● companies with diversity goals but biased hiring algorithms The gap between policy and lived experience is not accidental. It is infrastructural.

4. Engineered Containment as a Gendered and

Racialized

Mechanism

Engineered containment—the practice of exhausting individuals through loops, dead ends, and automated

funnels—disproportionately

affects

marginalized

groups.

Why? Because these groups are already positioned at the edges of institutional legibility. Women, racialized people, disabled people, queer and trans communities, and migrants often face:

● systems that do not recognize their situations ● forms that do not match their lived realities ● chatbots that cannot interpret their needs ● bureaucracies that treat their problems as anomalies Containment becomes a way of managing difference without addressing it. Example: Women and Care Work A woman trying to resolve a childcare subsidy issue encounters: ● a portal with no category for her specific situation ● a phone tree that disconnects after long waits ● a chatbot that loops her back to the same menu

Her exhaustion is not incidental. It is engineered. Example: Racialized Communities and Policing Systems A Black or Indigenous person attempting to file a complaint about discriminatory policing faces: ● opaque procedures ● missing escalation pathways ● contradictory instructions ● institutional silence The system protects itself by exhausting the complainant. Example: Disabled People and Accessibility Bureaucracies A disabled person applying for benefits encounters: ● inaccessible forms ● repeated documentation requests ● inconsistent eligibility criteria ● automated denials The burden of proof is shifted onto the individual. Example: Migrants Navigating Immigration Systems A migrant seeking status clarification faces: ● contradictory information ● long delays ● automated rejections ● no access to a human caseworker Containment becomes a gatekeeping mechanism.

5. The Digital Layer: How AI Reproduces Historical

Bias

Digital systems inherit the biases of the infrastructures they replace. AI systems often:

● treat male experiences as defaults ● misinterpret non ‑ Western names or accents ● fail to recognize gender ‑ diverse identities ● misclassify racialized individuals ● ignore disability ‑ specific needs When these systems become the first point of contact—customer service, healthcare triage, benefits

portals—they

amplify

historical

inequities.

The result is a new form of exclusion: Automated systems that reproduce old hierarchies at scale.

6. Why Rights Alone Cannot Fix Structural Harm

Rights operate at the level of principle. Infrastructure operates at the level of practice. Without rebuilding the infrastructure, rights remain: ● unenforced ● symbolic ● fragile ● easily undermined ● dependent on individual navigation This is why marginalized groups continue to struggle even when laws appear protective. The system was not designed for them.

7. Rebuilding the Infrastructure: What Real Change

Requires

Structural change requires more than policy updates. It requires: ● redesigning bureaucratic pathways ● creating escalation mechanisms

● ensuring human oversight ● building accessible systems ● embedding intersectional analysis into institutional design ● replacing containment with care This is not reform. It is reconstruction.
