1. Introduction: The Accountability Paradox

In modern societies, the most consequential harms are infrastructural: ● medication shortages ● algorithmic denials ● supply ‑ chain collapse ● administrative overload

● financial erosion ● digital cognitive harm Yet when these harms occur, the legal system asks: ● Who made the mistake? ● Who acted negligently? ● Who failed to follow procedure? This framing is incompatible with the reality of infrastructural harm. Infrastructures do not "act." They produce . They generate harm through: ● design ● defaults ● incentives ● interdependencies ● opacity ● acceleration But because the law is built on individual responsibility , it cannot assign blame to an infrastructure.

This is the accountability paradox.

2. Why the Law Cannot See Infrastructures The legal system is built on three assumptions: 1. Harm is caused by a person or organization. 2. Causation is linear and traceable. 3. Responsibility requires intention or negligence. Infrastructures violate all three. 2.1 No Single Actor Controls the Outcome Medication shortages, for example, involve: ● PBM rebate structures

● formulary exclusions ● manufacturer consolidation ● global supply fragility ● AI allocation systems ● pharmacy inventory algorithms No single point of failure exists. 2.2 Causation Is Distributed Harm emerges from the interaction of multiple systems, none of which individually "cause" the outcome.

2.3 Harm Is Produced by Design, Not Error Scarcity, denial, and erosion are not mistakes—they are outputs of infrastructural logic. The law cannot process this because it expects mistakes , not mechanisms .

3. The SR Framework: Harm as an Infrastructural

Output

SignalRupture reframes harm as: the predictable output of infrastructural architecture, not the failure of individual actors. 3.1 Infrastructure Determinism Systems produce what they are designed to produce. ● PBMs produce scarcity. ● Healthcare produces overload. ● Media produces volatility. ● AI produces misclassification. ● Banking produces erosion. These are not failures—they are outputs.

3.2 The Blind System Infrastructures are designed to: ● not see harm ● not integrate ● not coordinate ● not self ‑ correct Ignorance is structural, not accidental. 3.3 Post ‑ Institutional Power PBMs, algorithms, and platforms govern without appearing to govern. They shape: ● access ● visibility ● possibility ● survival But they evade responsibility because they do not fit the legal model of an "actor."

4.2 What the Law Sees ● no negligence ● no intent ● no single responsible party ● no direct causation 4.3 Outcome No one is accountable. This is not because the harm is ambiguous. It is because the legal framework is obsolete.
