1. Introduction Institutions assume that governance requires: ● visibility ● comprehension ● traceability ● stable categories ● interpretable signals But modern infrastructures violate all of these assumptions. They produce environments where: ● data exceeds interpretive capacity ● systems update faster than oversight ● categories drift ● signals distort ● complexity outpaces cognition This essay maps how legibility collapses as systems evolve beyond human ‑ scale understanding.

2. The End of Interpretability Traditional governance relies on the belief that systems can be: ● measured ● modeled ● audited ● predicted ● corrected But contemporary infrastructures are: ● too large to model ● too dynamic to predict ● too opaque to audit ● too interdependent to isolate ● too automated to intervene in Interpretability becomes a nostalgic assumption.

3. Complexity as Obscurity Complexity is not a byproduct — it is a shield. Systems become illegible because they are: ● distributed ● multi ‑ layered ● probabilistic ● self ‑ modifying ● globally entangled Illegibility is not a failure of documentation. It is a structural feature of scale.

4. The Drift of Categories Institutions rely on stable categories: ● citizen ● worker

● transaction ● risk ● identity But algorithmic systems operate on: ● behavioral clusters ● probabilistic segments ● dynamic risk scores ● inferred identities ● shifting classifications The categories drift faster than institutions can update their definitions.

5. The Failure of Oversight Oversight assumes: ● transparency ● auditability ● accountability ● human decision ‑ making But modern systems are governed by: ● automated processes ● opaque models ● proprietary architectures ● real ‑ time optimization Oversight collapses because it cannot see what it is meant to oversee.

6. The Politics of Illegibility Illegibility is not neutral. It produces: ● unaccountable power ● unresolvable errors ● untraceable harms

● ungovernable infrastructures ● unintentional governance When systems cannot be seen, they cannot be contested.

7. Toward Legibility ‑ Aware Governance To address legibility collapse, institutions must shift from: ● demanding transparency → mapping opacity ● regulating outcomes → regulating architectures ● assuming comprehension → acknowledging limits ● relying on categories → tracking drift ● governing systems → governing conditions Legibility must be treated as a structural variable, not an informational problem.
