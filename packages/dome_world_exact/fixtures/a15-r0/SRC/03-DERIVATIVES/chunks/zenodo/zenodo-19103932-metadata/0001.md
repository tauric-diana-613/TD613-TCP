    "description": "<p>Narrative interventions&mdash;documentaries, public campaigns, feminist critiques, and policy narratives&mdash;are widely assumed to reduce structural harms such as objectification and incarceration. This research finds the opposite. Across media‑effects studies, sexualization research, and long‑term incarceration statistics, the evidence shows that objectification increased during the same decades that feminist critiques became mainstream, and incarceration rates remained stable or rose despite policy reforms.</p>\n<p>The analysis identifies four infrastructures that produce these patterns:<br>(1) media‑economic infrastructures that reward sexualization and comparison;<br>(2) platform algorithmic infrastructures that amplify objectifying and aggressive content;<br>(3) institutional‑carceral infrastructures that maintain stable imprisonment regardless of policy intention; and<br>(4) policy‑implementation infrastructures that dilute reforms through bureaucratic drift and administrative inertia.</p>\n<p>Together, these findings demonstrate that infrastructures&mdash;not narratives&mdash;govern population‑level outcomes. Awareness campaigns operate at the symbolic layer, while objectification and carceral systems operate at the infrastructural layer. The result is a cross‑domain pattern in which harms persist or intensify even when interventions attempt to reduce them. This paper contributes a structural explanation for why narrative‑level change consistently fails to alter material outcomes.</p>",
