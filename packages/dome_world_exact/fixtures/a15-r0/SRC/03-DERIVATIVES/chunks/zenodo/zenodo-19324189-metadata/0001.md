    "description": "<p>The Global Institutional Genealogy: From Industrial Displacement to the Modern Surplus Population traces the structural architecture through which industrial societies generate surplus populations and the institutions that arise to manage the instability that surplus produces. Through a comparative analysis of Britain, the United States, and Canada, the paper identifies a repeating sequence&mdash;displacement, surplus formation, density‑driven instability, and institutional stabilization&mdash;that recurs across national contexts despite differing historical mechanisms. Britain establishes the prototype through enclosure and early industrialization; the United States amplifies the model through racialized and territorial displacement; Canada adapts it through settler‑colonial governance and resource extraction.</p>\n<p>The paper extends this genealogy into the contemporary era, examining how automation, deindustrialization, financialization, housing markets, immigration regimes, and algorithmic labour platforms generate a modern surplus population that is no longer a historical residue but a central operating condition of global systems. It introduces the concept of externalized displacement to explain how wealthy nations produce surplus populations abroad through economic restructuring, resource extraction, and geopolitical intervention. Across all variants, the analysis demonstrates that institutions such as public health, hospitals, criminal law, and administrative governance emerge as retroactive stabilizers of the instability industrial displacement creates.</p>",
