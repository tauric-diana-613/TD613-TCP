The Coercive Economy: When Work Becomes

a

Containment

System

Abstract This essay develops the concept of the coercive economy , a structural condition in which economic

systems

function

not

as

mechanisms

of

opportunity

but

as

containment

infrastructures

that

restrict

agency,

mobility,

and

autonomy.

Drawing

on

research

in

labor

studies,

organizational

sociology,

and

political

economy,

the

essay

argues

that

contemporary

work

structures

are

shaped

by

incentives

that

prioritize

compliance,

predictability,

and

extraction

over

human

development.

The

coercive

economy

emerges

through

wage

stagnation,

precarity,

algorithmic

management,

and

the

erosion

of

institutional

support

systems.

These

forces

create

a

system

in

which

individuals

are

compelled

to

remain

within

economic

structures

that

no

longer

provide

stability

or

upward

mobility.

SignalRupture

positions

the

coercive

economy

as

a

central

mechanism

of

modern

governance,

revealing

how

economic

infrastructures

shape

behavior,

constrain

choice,

and

limit

the

capacity

for

structural

resistance.

Keywords Coercive economy; precarity; labor extraction; algorithmic management; structural coercion; economic

containment;

SignalRupture.

1. Introduction: Work as a Containment System Work is traditionally framed as a pathway to stability, identity, and social mobility. In contemporary

economic

systems,

however,

work

increasingly

functions

as

a

containment

system —a

structure

that

restricts

agency

rather

than

expanding

it.

The coercive economy is not defined by overt force. It is defined by structural dependence . People remain in jobs not because they offer opportunity, but because exit is economically impossible.

SignalRupture treats the coercive economy as a structural condition that shapes behavior, identity,

and

cognition.

2. The Rise of Precarity

Precarity is the foundation of the coercive economy. Labor research shows that precarity is not a temporary

condition

but

a

systemic

design

feature

of

modern

economies.

Precarity emerges through: ● unstable contracts ● unpredictable hours ● gig-based labor ● algorithmic scheduling ● wage stagnation ● erosion of benefits These conditions create a system in which workers cannot plan, save, or exit. Precarity is not failure. Precarity is infrastructure . 3. Wage Stagnation and the Collapse of Mobility Economic research shows that wages have stagnated relative to productivity for decades, creating

a

structural

gap

between

labor

value

and

compensation.

This

gap

forces

individuals

to

remain

in

jobs

that

no

longer

provide

upward

mobility.

Wage stagnation produces: ● long-term dependence ● reduced bargaining power ● increased debt ● delayed life transitions ● structural immobility The coercive economy thrives on immobility. 4. Algorithmic Management and Behavioral Control Digital infrastructures have transformed management into a system of algorithmic control. Noble (2018)

and

Bayer

et

al.

(2020)

show

that

algorithmic

systems

shape

behavior

by

structuring

visibility,

reward,

and

access.

In the workplace, algorithmic management: ● monitors workers continuously ● automates discipline ● predicts behavior ● enforces compliance ● reduces autonomy

Workers are not managed by people. Workers are managed by infrastructures . This is a new form of coercion: invisible, automated, and continuous. 5. The Erosion of Institutional Support Systems The coercive economy is reinforced by the collapse of support systems that once provided buffers

against

economic

dependence.

These include: ● affordable housing ● accessible healthcare ● public education ● unemployment protections ● social safety nets As these systems erode, individuals become more dependent on employment for survival. Dependence is the core mechanism of coercion. 6. The Coercive Economy as Governance The coercive economy is not merely an economic condition. It is a governance system . Governance research shows that institutions increasingly rely on economic structures to regulate

behavior

(Kharas

&

Dooley,

2023).

The

coercive

economy

functions

as

a

form

of

soft

governance

by:

● limiting exit ● shaping identity ● controlling time ● constraining mobility ● reducing resistance Economic coercion replaces political coercion. 7. Containment as a Structural Logic Containment is the defining logic of the coercive economy. It operates through: ● debt ● precarity ● surveillance

● algorithmic control ● institutional erosion Containment is not punishment. Containment is design . SignalRupture identifies containment as a structural mechanism that shapes agency and limits the

capacity

for

systemic

change.
