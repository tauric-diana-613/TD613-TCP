2. The SARS Warning Window (2003–2019) Scientific literature between 2003 and 2019 documented: ● bat reservoirs containing SARS ‑ like coronaviruses (Ge et al., 2013) ● ACE2 ‑ binding viral strains capable of infecting human cells (Menachery et al., 2015) ● ecological conditions for zoonotic spillover (Daszak, 2008) ● the absence of global coronavirus surveillance (Osterholm, 2017) Despite this, institutional focus remained on influenza, a virus with predictable seasonality and established

vaccine

markets

(Osterholm,

2017).

SARS ‑ like

viruses

were

treated

as

anomalies

rather

than

structural

threats.

This mismatch between knowledge and action is the first indicator of structurally intentional negligence.

3. Why Institutions Ignore Predictable Risks 3.1 Prevention is invisible Successful prevention produces no visible political reward (Hood, 2011). 3.2 Prevention is expensive Long ‑ term surveillance and preparedness require sustained investment with no immediate payoff

(Katz,

2019).

3.3 Prevention requires political courage Acting before a crisis can be framed as alarmist or economically disruptive (Kingdon, 1995). 3.4 Crisis response is incentivized Crises justify emergency powers, centralized authority, and rapid policy shifts (Boin et al., 2005). Thus, institutions are structurally designed to wait for failure. 3.5 Why Influenza Receives Continuous Updates While SARS ‑ Like Viruses Are

Overlooked

4. Structurally Intentional Negligence: A Diagnostic Model SignalRupture defines structurally intentional negligence as: A system designed to ignore predictable risks due to misaligned incentives, then capitalize on the

This model has three components: 4.1 Predictable risk SARS ‑ like spillover was a documented inevitability (Daszak, 2008; Menachery et al., 2015). 4.2 Incentivized inaction Institutions lacked incentives to act on the risk (Hood, 2011). 4.3 Opportunistic response Once the crisis emerged, institutions rapidly expanded authority, implemented compliance measures,

This pattern is not intentional in the conspiratorial sense; it is intentional in the structural sense. 5. Crisis Governance and Compliance Measurement COVID ‑ 19 revealed how crises function as governance accelerators. During the pandemic, governments

7. How SR’s Prevention Model Intervenes at the Governance

Layer

SignalRupture does not attempt to prevent pathogens. It prevents systemic failure by intervening

at

SR’s prevention model focuses on: 7.1 Epistemic integrity Ensuring scientific warnings are not lost in bureaucratic noise (Jasanoff, 2004). 7.2 Incentive realignment Structurally rewarding prevention rather than crisis exploitation (Wildavsky, 1988). 7.3 Institutional transparency

Reducing the opacity that allows negligence to persist (Oreskes, 2019). 7.4 Governance ‑ layer diagnostics Identifying failure modes before they cascade (Weick, 1995). 7.5 Crisis ‑ resilient architecture Designing systems that do not rely on emergency powers to function (Lakoff, 2017). COVID ‑ 19 becomes a case study for how SR can model and prevent governance ‑ layer collapse.
