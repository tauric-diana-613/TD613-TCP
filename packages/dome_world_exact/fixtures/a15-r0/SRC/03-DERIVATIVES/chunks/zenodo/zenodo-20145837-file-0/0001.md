Raising Wages as Structural Policy: Re-Aligning

Incentives

and

Restoring

Public

Trust

Abstract Housing affordability crises across advanced economies are frequently framed as multi-causal phenomena

involving

supply

shortages,

demographic

pressures,

financial

conditions,

and

regulatory

constraints.

While

these

factors

influence

housing

markets,

this

article

argues

that

wage

stagnation

relative

to

the

cost

of

living

constitutes

the

foundational

structural

driver

of

declining

homeownership

and

rising

economic

precarity

across

OECD

countries

(OECD,

2023).

Drawing

on

labour

economics,

political

economy,

housing

studies,

and

institutional

theory,

the

paper

demonstrates

that

inadequate

wage

growth

reduces

household

resilience

to

broader

market

pressures,

intensifies

dependence

on

debt,

and

amplifies

the

social

effects

of

housing

financialization.

The

article

further

examines

the

institutional

incentives

that

discourage

wage

reform,

analyzes

how

complexity

framing

can

diffuse

and

explores

the

relationship

between

insecurity,

institutional

trust,

and

the

emergence

of

conspiratorial

interpretations

of

economic

governance.

The

paper

concludes

by

outlining

a

policy

framework

grounded

in

wage

growth,

institutional

labour

1. Introduction Economic precarity has become a defining feature of life across advanced economies. Households

increasingly

face

stagnant

real

and

declining

in

(OECD,

Policymakers

typically

frame

these

pressures

as

the

product

of

multiple

interacting

forces,

including

housing

shortages,

demographic

concentration,

interest

rates,

and

market

volatility.

While

these

factors

are

significant,

this

paper

argues

that

the

of

wage

relative

to

costs

constitutes

the

foundational

structural

driver

of

contemporary

affordability

crises.

The central argument advanced here is not that wages are the sole determinant of affordability outcomes,

but

that

wage

stagnation

determines

the

extent

to

which

households

can

withstand

broader

economic

pressures.

Housing

supply

constraints,

financialization,

and

speculative

investment become socially destabilizing primarily when labour income no longer supports stable

asset

acquisition,

rent

burdens,

or

long-term

household

security.

This article further argues that the persistence of wage stagnation reflects institutional incentives rather

than

economic

inevitability.

Governments

wage

reform

because

it

disrupts

existing

political

and

economic

arrangements

tied

to

asset

inflation,

capital

accumulation,

and

electoral

incentives.

Instead,

policymakers

often

rely

on

symbolic

or

incremental

interventions

that

manage

political

pressure

without

altering

underlying

income

dynamics.

2. Wage Stagnation as Structural Imbalance

Across OECD economies, productivity growth has substantially outpaced real wage growth over recent

decades

(OECD,

2023).

This

divergence

represents

a

structural

transformation

in

the

relationship

between

labour

and

capital.

Whereas

post-war

economic

systems

broadly

linked

productivity

gains

to

rising

household

incomes,

contemporary

economies

increasingly

concentrate

gains

among

asset

owners

and

high-income

groups

(Piketty,

2014).

Mishel and Bivens (2021) attribute this divergence to institutional and policy changes, including weakened

collective

bargaining

systems,

labour

market

deregulation,

declining

union

density,

and

increased

capital

mobility.

These

developments

reduced

labour’s

bargaining

power

while

strengthening

the

political

and

economic

position

of

capital

holders.

Housing affordability pressures emerge most severely where wage growth fails to keep pace with

essential

expenditures.

Rising

housing

prices

alone

do

not

necessarily

produce

widespread

precarity

if

incomes

rise

proportionally.

However,

when

housing

costs

increase

while

wages

stagnate,

households

become

increasingly

dependent

on

debt,

precarious

employment

arrangements,

delayed

family

formation,

and

intergenerational

transfers.

Comparative OECD evidence suggests that countries with stronger wage growth and stronger labour

institutions

frequently

exhibit

lower

levels

of

housing

precarity,

even

where

supply

constraints exist. This indicates that wage dynamics significantly mediate the social effects of broader

housing

market

pressures.

2.1 Comparative Affordability Dynamics Recent OECD data demonstrates that housing prices in many advanced economies have risen substantially

faster

than

median

wages

over

the

past

two

decades

(OECD,

2023).

In

several

metropolitan

regions,

home

price-to-income

ratios

have

doubled

or

tripled

relative

to

historical

norms.

Simultaneously,

labour’s

share

of

national

income

has

declined

across

much

of

the

OECD.

These trends have produced several observable outcomes: ● declining rates of first-time homeownership, ● increasing household debt burdens, ● higher rent-to-income ratios, ● delayed household formation, ● and declining intergenerational mobility. The interaction between wage stagnation and housing inflation is therefore central to understanding

contemporary

affordability

crises.

3. Institutional Incentives and Policy Drift Governments frequently avoid direct wage intervention because wage growth disrupts established

institutional

incentives.

Hacker’s

(2004)

concept

of

policy

drift

helps

explain

how

political

systems

maintain

outdated

policy

frameworks

despite

changing

economic

realities.

Drift

occurs

not

necessarily

through

explicit

retrenchment,

but

through

institutional

inaction

that

allows

structural

imbalances

to

intensify

over

time.

Several incentives contribute to wage policy avoidance: ● corporate resistance to labour cost increases, ● concerns regarding inflationary perception, ● fiscal dependence on asset appreciation, ● electoral incentives tied to property ownership, ● and bureaucratic resistance to disruptive structural reform. Importantly, these incentives do not require centralized coordination to produce consistent outcomes.

Political

and

economic

actors

operating

within

similar

institutional

constraints

may

independently

reproduce

policies

that

favour

asset

inflation

and

wage

restraint.

Governments therefore often prefer policies that preserve macroeconomic stability and asset growth

while

minimizing

conflict

with

concentrated

economic

interests.

Incremental

affordability

measures—such

as

tax

credits,

savings

incentives,

or

targeted

subsidies—can

alleviate

political

pressure

without

fundamentally

altering

labour

income

dynamics.

4. Complexity Framing as Governance Mechanism

Contemporary governments frequently describe affordability crises as highly complex policy problems

requiring

multi-stakeholder

coordination,

long-term

planning,

and

incremental

adjustment.

While

housing

systems

are

undeniably

complex,

complexity

framing

can

also

function

institutionally

by

diffusing

political

accountability

across

multiple

interacting

variables.

Complexity framing serves several governance functions: ● distributing causality across numerous factors, ● justifying incremental rather than structural interventions, ● reducing expectations for immediate policy outcomes, ● and reinforcing technocratic approaches to governance. This does not necessarily imply intentional deception by policymakers. Rather, institutional systems

often

favour

policy

narratives

that

minimize

political

disruption

and

preserve

existing

arrangements.

Housing policy provides a clear example. Governments commonly prioritize supply-side interventions

such

as

zoning

reform,

development

incentives,

and

tax

adjustments.

Although

such

policies

can

improve

housing

availability

over

time,

their

effects

on

affordability

remain

limited

where

household

income

growth

substantially

lags

behind

housing

costs.

The issue is therefore not that housing supply is irrelevant, but that supply-side approaches alone

cannot

restore

affordability

in

environments

characterized

by

prolonged

wage

stagnation.

5. Wages as the Primary Affordability Lever

Wage growth functions as a foundational affordability mechanism because it directly affects household

purchasing

power,

debt

dependency,

savings

capacity,

and

resilience

to

market

fluctuations.

Housing

affordability

is

not

determined

solely

by

prices;

it

is

determined

by

the

relationship

between

prices

and

incomes.

5.1 Real Wages and Purchasing Power Nominal wage growth alone is insufficient if inflation outpaces compensation. What matters structurally

is

real

wage

growth

relative

to

essential

expenditures

such

as

housing,

food,

transportation,

and

healthcare.

When real wages stagnate: ● debt burdens increase, ● rent burdens rise, ● savings rates decline, ● and homeownership becomes increasingly inaccessible. Conversely, sustained real wage growth improves household resilience even in high-cost housing

environments.

5.2 Wage Growth and Inflation A common argument against wage increases is that they generate inflationary pressure. However,

the

relationship

between

wages

and

inflation

is

more

complex

than

conventional

narratives

suggest.

In

highly

concentrated

sectors,

firms

may

maintain

elevated

prices

independently

of

labour

costs,

while

weak

labour

bargaining

power

can

suppress

demand

without

significantly

reducing

structural

inflation.

Moreover, wage growth tied to productivity increases differs from purely demand-driven inflationary

expansion.

Historically,

periods

of

strong

middle-class

growth

frequently

coincided

with

rising

wages,

expanding

consumption,

and

broad

economic

stability.

5.3 Labour Market Concentration and Bargaining Power Recent labour economics research increasingly emphasizes monopsony dynamics in labour markets,

where

employers

possess

disproportionate

bargaining

power

over

workers.

Weak

collective

bargaining

systems,

labour

fragmentation,

and

declining

unionization

reduce

workers’

capacity

to

negotiate

compensation

aligned

with

productivity

growth.

As bargaining power declines, labour income becomes increasingly disconnected from broader economic

growth.

This

contributes

not

only

to

inequality

but

also

to

long-term

affordability

deterioration.
