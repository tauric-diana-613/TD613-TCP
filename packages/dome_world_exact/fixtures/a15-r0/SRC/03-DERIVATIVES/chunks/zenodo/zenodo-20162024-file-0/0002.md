9. Conclusion The Outcome Drift Differential (ODD) framework demonstrates persistent divergence between institutional

targets

and

observed

outcomes

across

ODSP,

OW,

Canadian

housing

policy,

and

U.S.

housing

policy.

Infrastructure

determinism

provides

a

robust

framework

for

understanding

why

policy–outcome

divergence

persists

even

when

institutional

goals

are

explicit,

publicly

supported,

and

formally

resourced.

The persistence of patterned drift across multiple institutional domains suggests that implementation

infrastructures

should

be

treated

not

as

secondary

administrative

mechanisms

but

as

primary

determinants

of

policy

performance.

10. Limitations 1. Data Availability and Temporal Lag

Institutional datasets are often released with delays, limiting real-time accuracy (FAO, n.d.; CMHC,

n.d.).

2. Variability in Measurement Standards Different institutions use different methodologies, complicating comparability (HUD, n.d.; CMHC, n.d.).

3. Structural Opacity of Administrative Systems Many infrastructural mechanisms are not publicly documented, limiting causal mapping. 4. Policy Intent Ambiguity Targets may be aspirational rather than operationalized. 5. Heterogeneity Across Jurisdictions Findings may not generalize across regions. 6. CERB as a Low ‑ Drift Outlier CERB operated under exceptional conditions (Government of Canada, n.d.; CRA, n.d.). 7. ODD as a First ‑ Generation Metric ODD does not capture deeper structural dynamics. Institutional methodologies for estimating non ‑ take ‑ up, vacancy rates, and homelessness counts vary

across

agencies

and

are

not

always

fully

transparent,

introducing

additional

uncertainty

into

drift

calculations.
