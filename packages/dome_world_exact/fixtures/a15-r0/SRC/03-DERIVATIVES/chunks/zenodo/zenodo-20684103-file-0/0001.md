2. Theoretical Foundations Institutional Theory Institutions influence behavior through rules, incentives, and repeated procedural environments. Behavioral Economics Scarcity reallocates attention and reduces available cognitive bandwidth. Political Psychology Identity shapes political interpretation and information processing. Sociology Social environments structure habitus and interpretive tendencies. Cognitive Science Reasoning adapts to environmental constraints and available cognitive resources.

4. Theorem 4.1 The Cognitive Inequality Theorem (Invariant Statement)

For a population P , unequal distributions of institutional conditioning, scarcity, informational complexity,

Cognitive inequality exists when: [ \text{Var}(A_i) > \tau ] where τ is a defined inequality threshold (Section 4.2). This theorem is invariant: it specifies the conditions under which cognitive inequality emerges , independent

4.2 Defining the Threshold τ The threshold τ may be defined in one of four ways: 1. Normative threshold — a theoretically justified level of inequality beyond which democratic

agency

is

impaired. 2. Empirical percentile threshold — e.g., τ = 75th percentile of cross ‑ national variance. 3. Cross ‑ country benchmark — τ defined relative to stable high ‑ capacity democracies.

4. Statistically estimated threshold — τ derived from structural break tests, clustering, or inequality

indices.

Each empirical application must justify its definition of τ.

4.3 Mediation and Moderation Structure

The conceptual logic of the CIT implies a mediated and moderated structure: Primary Mediation [ S_i \rightarrow I_i \rightarrow A_i ] Scarcity increases reliance on institutions, which shapes cognitive tendencies. Moderation by Drift Institutional drift D moderates both pathways: ● Drift amplifies the effect of scarcity on conditioning.

● Drift amplifies the effect of conditioning on cognitive tendencies. This structure more accurately reflects the theory than a purely additive model.

6. Corollaries ● Corollary 1: Political choices increasingly reflect identity dependence under rising scarcity. ● Corollary 2: Institutional drift amplifies cognitive divergence. ● Corollary 3: Complexity exposure moderates inequality effects. ● Corollary 4: Formal political equality does not guarantee equal practical agency.

7. Empirical Identification Strategy The theorem becomes empirically observable if: 1. Institutional exposure predicts measurable cognitive differences. 2. Scarcity predicts reduced adaptive updating. 3. Institutional drift predicts widening variance. 4. Competing explanations cannot fully account for observed effects.

8. Hypotheses ● H1: Greater scarcity predicts lower adaptive cognitive tendency. ● H2: Greater institutional conditioning predicts stronger identity-based reasoning. ● H3: Institutional drift mediates scarcity effects. ● H4: Complexity exposure partially offsets inequality formation. ● H5: The combined model explains more variance than isolated factors.

12. Null Theorem

Null Theorem (H ₀ ) Institutional and scarcity variables do not systematically predict variance in adaptive cognitive tendencies.

Formally: [ \beta_1 = \beta_2 = \beta_3 = \beta_4 = 0 ] and [ \text{Var}(A_i) = \tau_0 ] Rejecting the null supports—but does not prove—the CIT.

13. Theoretical Expectations If the theorem holds under empirical testing:

● cognitive conditions will distribute unevenly across institutional environments ● scarcity will increase reliance on simplified reasoning ● institutional conditioning will shape interpretive tendencies ● drift will amplify inequality ● complexity exposure will moderate inequality formation These are theoretical expectations, not empirical results.

14. Competing Theories The CIT differs from existing frameworks: ● Rational choice: assumes stable preferences; CIT models preference formation. ● Bounded rationality: focuses on cognitive limits; CIT explains structural origins of those limits. ● Motivated reasoning: explains identity ‑ driven processing; CIT explains why identity reliance

varies. ● Cultural cognition: emphasizes group norms; CIT integrates institutional and scarcity effects. ● Scarcity theory: explains bandwidth loss; CIT embeds scarcity in a broader institutional system. ● Institutional path dependence: explains drift; CIT links drift to cognitive outcomes. CIT is a unifying framework across these literatures.

15. Policy Implications ● Reduce scarcity to increase adaptive reasoning capacity. ● Increase complexity exposure through civic education and institutional transparency. ● Reduce drift through oversight, simplification, and accountability. ● Design institutions that reward reflection rather than compliance. ● Strengthen informational environments to reduce identity ‑ based reasoning.

16. Ethical Considerations

● CIT does not imply cognitive superiority or inferiority. ● It rejects deterministic interpretations of cognition. ● It avoids stigmatizing individuals or groups. ● It requires careful handling of sensitive cognitive and institutional data. ● It must not be used to justify exclusion or disenfranchisement.
