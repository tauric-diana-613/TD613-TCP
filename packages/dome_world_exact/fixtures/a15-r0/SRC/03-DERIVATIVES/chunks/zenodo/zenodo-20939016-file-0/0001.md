Contemporary scholarship on extractivism and the energy transition builds on these foundations.

Cerioli

The Extractivism.de (2026) synthesis reinforces this interpretation, noting that “green windows” of opportunity can either empower or constrain Global South governments depending on their political

Complementing this perspective, Maconachie et al. (2026) show that mining governance and green

While these literatures provide essential insights, they leave a conceptual gap: How

This is where SignalRupture (SR) contributes.

SR synthesizes the insights of Dependency Theory, World ‑ Systems Theory, and contemporary extractivism

SR introduces three conceptual innovations: 1. Managed Fragility — instability is not accidental but functional within asymmetric dependency

3. CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK: THE P.E.S.T.

MODEL

The SR P.E.S.T. model integrates four domains into a single conceptual architecture.

3.1 P — Political Logic: Asymmetric Geoeconomic Pressure

High ‑ capacity states use: ● sanctions ● conditional loans ● security agreements ● regulatory leverage ● supply ‑ chain capture These tools do not aim to topple regimes but to maintain predictable extraction environments .

Instability

The transition requires unprecedented quantities of: ● lithium ● cobalt ● nickel ● copper ● rare earth elements These minerals are disproportionately located in states with low institutional capacity , creating structural

3.3 S — Structural Logic: Managed Fragility Instability is not accidental. It

Mechanisms include: ● debt structures ● supply ‑ chain dependency ● corporate extraction ● security assistance ● fragmented governance These mechanisms keep states: ● too weak to renegotiate , ● too important to collapse , ● too dependent to resist . 3.4 T — Transitional Logic: Externalizing Instability The transition decade compresses multiple pressures: ● institutional drift ● demographic strain ● energy volatility ● economic slowdown ● political polarization High ‑ capacity states externalize instability because they cannot absorb additional internal shocks.

3.5

Scope

Conditions

Analysis

The SR framework applies to a specific analytical domain: state–resource–transition interactions .

The

● state institutions, ● resource endowments, ● external actors, and ● transition ‑ era pressures. Within this framework, high ‑ capacity states refer to configurations of: ● institutional coherence, ● industrial systems with strategic depth, ● financial and monetary influence, and ● supply ‑ chain coordination capabilities. SR does not assume intentional destabilization. Instability

may

emerge

from:

● incentive structures, ● institutional constraints, ● competitive adaptation, or ● structural pressures generated by the transition. Conditions where SR applies ● transition ‑ critical resource dependence, ● asymmetric bargaining capacity,

● reliance on external capital or technology, ● exposure to geoeconomic leverage. Conditions where SR does not apply ● diversified economies with strong buffers, ● high institutional capacity, ● low exposure to strategic minerals, ● limited integration into global supply chains. SR therefore proposes a conditional structural mechanism rather than a universal geopolitical

law.

4. THE SR MECHANISM MODEL 4.1 — Falsifiable Propositions To convert the SR framework into a testable theory, the following hypotheses specify observable relationships:

H1 — Geoeconomic Influence Hypothesis States with greater dependence on external critical mineral supply exhibit greater use of geoeconomic

influence

mechanisms

(sanctions,

conditional

finance,

regulatory

H2 — Fragility Amplification Hypothesis Resource ‑ dependent states with lower institutional capacity experience greater governance volatility

during

transition

H3 — Dependency Persistence Hypothesis Higher external dependence predicts lower domestic value capture from green industrialization.

H4 — Compression Hypothesis Periods of simultaneous transition pressure increase the probability of policy narrowing in extractivist

states.
