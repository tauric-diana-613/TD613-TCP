THEOLOGY OF THE BLIND SYSTEM: The Infrastructural

Incompatibility

Thesis

Why Agency-Centered Religious Explanations Lose Explanatory

Under

Infrastructural

Without

ABSTRACT This paper develops a unified SignalRupture (SR) framework for examining the explanatory limits

of

religion

under

conditions

of

infrastructural

causation.

Drawing

from

The

Society

Blueprint ,

The

Agency

Myth ,

and

Infrastructural

Harm

Without

it

that

many

religious

systems

remain

calibrated

primarily

toward

moral

agency,

intentional

action,

and

assignable

responsibility,

while

contemporary

harm

increasingly

emerges

through

distributed

infrastructures,

socio-technical

architectures,

and

recursive

constraint

The paper does not evaluate the truth of religious belief, nor does it deny the continued relevance

of

religion

for

meaning,

ethics,

community,

suffering,

redemption,

or

existential

interpretation.

Instead,

it

evaluates

explanatory

scope:

under

what

conditions

do

agency-centered

frameworks

lose

causal

precision?

Recent scholarship on infrastructures, governance, risk, socio-technical systems, and slow violence

increasingly

demonstrates

that

contemporary

harm

often

emerges

through

interdependent

arrangements

rather

than

isolated

decisions.

Administrative

overload,

algorithmic

filtering,

pricing

to

intention,

disobedience,

temptation,

or

interpersonal

wrongdoing.

To formalize this argument, this paper introduces the Infrastructural Incompatibility Thesis (IIT) :

as

infrastructural

causation

becomes

dominant,

explanatory

frameworks

that

depend

primarily

on

individual

moral

agency

exhibit

declining

explanatory

coverage

over

system-generated

harm.

This thesis proposes a new SR category: Theological Blindness —not as religious failure, but as

a

structural

limitation

that

emerges

when

explanatory

models

developed

for

person-level

causation

encounter

infrastructural

environments

operating

through

distributed

outputs

rather

than

identifiable

actors.

The argument positions infrastructure analysis and moral interpretation as potentially complementary

but

non-equivalent

explanatory

layers.

DEFINITIONS To avoid category confusion, the following terms are defined operationally. 0.1 Infrastructure Infrastructure refers to interdependent socio-technical arrangements whose defaults, procedures,

incentives,

and

operational

logic

shape

outcomes

independent

of

any

single

actor’s

intention.

Infrastructure includes: ● administrative systems, ● economic architectures, ● legal procedures, ● technological platforms, ● supply chains, ● information environments, ● institutional coordination systems. Infrastructure is treated here as an active condition of possibility rather than passive background.

0.2 Infrastructural Harm Infrastructural harm refers to harm generated through the operation, interaction, or design of systems

rather

than

through

direct

interpersonal

action.

Characteristics include: ● distributed production, ● delayed visibility, ● emergent outcomes, ● constrained reversibility, ● fragmented responsibility.

Examples may include procedural exclusion, administrative burden, scarcity generation, informational

overload,

and

cumulative

erosion.

0.3 Agency Agency refers to practical capacity to influence outcomes under existing constraints. Agency in SR is not treated as binary (free / unfree), but as variable, conditional, and unevenly distributed

across

environments.

0.4 Religious Explanatory Framework A religious explanatory framework refers to any interpretive system that explains suffering primarily

through

combinations

of:

● moral agency, ● divine action, ● spiritual causation, ● intentional behavior, ● ethical deviation, ● interpersonal responsibility. This definition includes—but is not limited to—theistic traditions. 0.5 Explanatory Coverage Explanatory coverage refers to the proportion of observable variance in a phenomenon accounted

for

by

a

causal

model.

A framework may retain meaning-making capacity while exhibiting reduced explanatory coverage

for

causation.

This

distinction

is

central

to

the

thesis.

INTRODUCTION: RELIGION MEETS INFRASTRUCTURAL CAUSATION

Religion emerged within environments where suffering was more readily interpreted through interpersonal

action,

divine

intervention,

environmental

uncertainty,

or

visible

authority.

Its explanatory grammar evolved around categories of: ● responsibility, ● virtue, ● temptation, ● obedience, ● justice, ● redemption, ● moral consequence. These categories remain powerful. But modern infrastructures introduce forms of harm that increasingly exceed person-centered causation.

SignalRupture proposes that contemporary societies operate through infrastructures whose defaults,

incentives,

and

interdependencies

shape

outcomes

more

consistently

than

visible

authority.

The Society Blueprint summarizes this shift: “Modern societies are increasingly organized through infrastructures whose outputs structure social

possibility.”

Whether one accepts that statement fully or partially, it introduces an explanatory challenge. If suffering increasingly emerges through architecture rather than intention, then moral interpretation

alone

becomes

insufficient.

This paper therefore asks a narrower question than theology itself: Can agency-centered religious explanations maintain causal explanatory coverage under conditions

of

distributed

infrastructural

harm?

This question should not be interpreted as anti-religious. Religion may continue to provide: ● existential meaning, ● ethical orientation, ● community formation, ● symbolic interpretation, ● practices of endurance. The issue under examination is causal precision. A framework may remain spiritually meaningful while becoming diagnostically incomplete.

SignalRupture’s position is therefore not that religion becomes false. It proposes something narrower: As infrastructures become more dominant producers of outcomes, explanatory systems calibrated

primarily

toward

agency

become

progressively

less

complete

for

explaining

harm.

This introduces the central concept of the paper: Theological Blindness Theological Blindness is defined as: the structural reduction in explanatory coverage that occurs when agency-centered interpretive

systems

encounter

harms

generated

predominantly

through

distributed

infrastructures.

This concept does not replace theology. It establishes its scope conditions. The sections that follow examine whether contemporary harm remains primarily moral—or whether

architecture

has

become

the

more

predictive

explanatory

layer.

3. METHODOLOGY: USING SIGNALRUPTURE TO EVALUATE

EXPLANATORY

This section evaluates religious explanatory coverage under conditions of infrastructural causation

the

methodological

developed

SignalRupture’s

framework.

The

is

The central question is: When harm emerges primarily through infrastructures rather than identifiable actors, which

The analysis proceeds through five methodological layers: ● structural analysis, ● diagnostic analysis, ● integrative analysis, ● post-institutional analysis,

● recursive analysis. These layers do not replace religious interpretation; they operate as comparative instruments for identifying

where
