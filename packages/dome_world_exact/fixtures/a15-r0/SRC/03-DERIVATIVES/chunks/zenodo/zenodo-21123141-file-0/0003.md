7. ACCOUNTABILITY UNDER DISTRIBUTED CAUSATION Traditional accountability models assume four conditions: ● identifiable actor, ● visible action, ● observable consequence, ● assignable responsibility. Infrastructural environments increasingly violate these assumptions. No single participant may: ● control the outcome, ● understand the system, ● possess corrective authority. Yet harm still occurs. This creates a paradox: Responsibility exists. Control fragments. SR describes this as Distributed Responsibility . Distributed Responsibility does not eliminate ethics. It expands the object of responsibility. Questions

shift

from:

Who caused this? toward: What arrangement repeatedly produces this? This shift changes intervention. Moral repair often emphasizes: ● repentance, ● accountability, ● behavior change. Structural repair emphasizes: ● redesign, ● simplification, ● resilience, ● transparency, ● incentive correction.

The approaches are complementary but not interchangeable.

8. CASE ANALYSIS: MEDICATION SCARCITY Consider a patient unable to obtain prescribed treatment. The question is not: Who failed? The question is: What produced the shortage? A person-centered model may emphasize:

● responsibility, ● compliance, ● individual decision, ● provider behavior. These may matter, but they rarely explain the full event. An infrastructural model asks: ● Were reimbursement structures distorted? ● Did procurement incentives change? ● Were administrative barriers introduced? ● Did supply dependencies accumulate? ● Were coordination mechanisms fragile? Each answer alters intervention. The harm may emerge despite: ● competent clinicians, ● responsible patients, ● ethical intentions. No actor intends deprivation. Yet deprivation appears. The system produces scarcity. This distinction demonstrates the paper’s central claim: causal precision increasingly requires

infrastructural

analysis .

9. CONDITIONS OF FALSIFICATION The Infrastructural Incompatibility Thesis should not be treated as unfalsifiable. Its explanatory value

weakens

if

consistently

● agency-centered religious frameworks reliably predict infrastructural outcomes, ● moral interventions reduce system-generated harms without architectural change, ● complex distributed harms consistently reduce to identifiable actors, ● practical agency remains largely unaffected by infrastructural load, ● system redesign contributes less explanatory improvement than moral intervention. If these conditions were demonstrated broadly, the thesis would require revision. This paper therefore

proposes

a

conditional

rather

than

a

closed

doctrine.

11. CONCLUSION: FROM MORAL SUFFERING TO ARCHITECTURAL

SUFFERING

The central argument of this paper is not that religion becomes obsolete. It is that explanatory environments

change.

Religion

emerged

within

conditions

where

suffering

could

be

interpreted

through

visible

action,

moral

choice,

and

legible

Under

those

historical

religious

systems—including

Anglicanism—often

functioned

as

infrastructures

of

social

order,

producing

stability

through

moral

regulation,

identity

governance

(Thompson,

1963;

Asad,

1996).

Modern infrastructures operate differently. Contemporary harm increasingly emerges through distributed

causation,

recursive

procedural

accumulation,

and

interdependence.

These

environments

generate

that

exceed

individual

resist

Under

agency ‑ centered

becomes

less

Religion continues to provide meaning, hope, ethics, and endurance. These functions remain vital.

But

infrastructure

analysis

increasingly

explains

mechanism,

prediction,

distribution,

and

the reproduction of harm. These explanatory layers need not compete, but they should not be confused.

Modern suffering increasingly exceeds categories centered on sin, intention, or personal deviation.

Understanding

contemporary

architecture

persons.

This

is

the

Infrastructural

Incompatibility

Thesis:

as

becomes

dominant,

frameworks

calibrated

agency

lose

causal

coverage

over

system ‑ generated
