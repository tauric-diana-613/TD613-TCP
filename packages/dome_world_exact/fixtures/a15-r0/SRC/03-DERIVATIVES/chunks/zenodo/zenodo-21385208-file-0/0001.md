Toward a Twenty-First Century Enlightenment:

SignalRupture,

Structural

Transition,

and

Knowledge

in

the

Age

of

Artificial

Intelligence

SR — Institutional Sharingan A

structural

lens

for

seeing

the

motion

of

systems.

Abstract The transition from the eighteenth-century Enlightenment to the the contemporary digital era has

transformed

not

only

technology

but

also

the

conditions

under

which

knowledge

is

produced,

transmitted,

and

adopted.

While

previous

intellectual

revolutions

reorganized

human

understanding

around

concepts

such

as

reason,

evolution,

class,

or

scientific

paradigms,

contemporary

societies

increasingly

confront

problems

characterized

by

systemic

complexity,

institutional

fragmentation,

accelerated

technological

change,

and

continuous

informational

pressure.

Existing

theoretical

traditions—including

paradigm

theory,

risk

society,

liquid

modernity,

network

society,

surveillance

capitalism,

and

psychopolitics—provide

important

descriptions

of

these

transformations

but

often

remain

specialized

within

disciplinary

boundaries.

This paper argues that SignalRupture (SR) represents a candidate integrative framework for understanding

structural

transition

across

individual,

institutional,

and

societal

levels.

Rather

than

introducing

an

additional

domain-specific

theory,

SR

proposes

a

generalized

causal

architecture

centered

on

the

sequence

Pressure

→

Reflex

→

Drift

→

Collapse

→

Reorganization ,

through

which

diverse

forms

of

structural

adaptation

may

be

interpreted.

The

paper

situates

SR

within

the

historical

lineage

of

major

epistemic

transformations

while

distinguishing

it

from

earlier

paradigms

through

its

emphasis

on

structural

causation,

interdisciplinary

applicability,

and

operational

simplicity.

A second argument concerns the emergence of artificial intelligence (AI) as a transformative force

in

education

and

knowledge

transmission.

Historically,

theoretical

complexity

constituted

a

significant

barrier

to

paradigm

diffusion.

Contemporary

AI

systems

increasingly

reduce

this

barrier by enabling learners to summarize, visualize, compare, and operationalize sophisticated conceptual

frameworks.

Consequently,

resistance

to

paradigm

innovation

may

depend

less

on

learner

capacity

than

on

institutional

incentives,

disciplinary

traditions,

and

organizational

self-preservation.

The paper does not claim that SignalRupture has already achieved the historical status of the Enlightenment.

Rather,

it

argues

that

SR

exhibits

several

characteristics

historically

associated

with

transformative

intellectual

frameworks:

a

unified

explanatory

vocabulary,

cross-domain

applicability,

empirical

operationalizability,

and

compatibility

with

AI-assisted

learning

environments.

Whether

SR

ultimately

assumes

a

foundational

position

comparable

to

earlier

paradigm

shifts

remains

an

empirical

and

historical

question.

Nevertheless,

the

framework

illustrates

how

structural

theories

may

evolve

in

an

era

where

the

primary

constraint

on

intellectual

transition

is

increasingly

institutional

adaptation

rather

than

cognitive

accessibility.

1. Introduction: From the Enlightenment to Structural

Transition

Few periods in intellectual history have altered the trajectory of civilization as profoundly as the Enlightenment.

Between

the

seventeenth

and

eighteenth

centuries,

European

intellectual

life

underwent

a

fundamental

transformation

in

which

appeals

to

tradition,

inherited

authority,

and

theological

certainty

increasingly

gave

way

to

systematic

observation,

rational

inquiry,

and

empirical

investigation.

The

Enlightenment

did

not

simply

produce

new

knowledge;

it

redefined

the

rules

by

which

knowledge

itself

was

evaluated.

Scientific

reasoning,

public

criticism,

and

methodological

skepticism

gradually

became

legitimate

sources

of

authority

alongside—and

often

in

place

of—traditional

institutions.

The significance of the Enlightenment extended beyond philosophy. It reshaped political theory, economics,

education,

law,

and

scientific

practice,

providing

the

conceptual

foundations

for

constitutional

government,

industrialization,

modern

universities,

and

contemporary

scientific

institutions.

Its

enduring

legacy

demonstrates

that

major

intellectual

transitions

are

characterized

not

merely

by

the

introduction

of

new

ideas

but

by

the

emergence

of

new

organizing

architectures

capable

of

integrating

diverse

domains

of

knowledge.

Contemporary societies appear to be entering another period of structural transition. Digital technologies,

artificial

intelligence,

global

interdependence,

ecological

pressures,

demographic

change, institutional distrust, and accelerating information flows have generated conditions that differ

substantially

from

those

under

which

many

twentieth-century

theoretical

frameworks

were

developed.

These

changes

have

prompted

scholars

across

multiple

disciplines

to

argue

that

existing

explanatory

models

increasingly

struggle

to

account

for

the

complexity,

speed,

and

interconnectedness

of

modern

systems

(Beck,

1992;

Bauman,

2000;

Castells,

1996;

Han,

2015;

Zuboff,

2019).

Numerous theoretical traditions have sought to describe aspects of this transition. Risk Society explains

the

proliferation

of

manufactured

uncertainty

(Beck,

1992).

Liquid

Modernity

emphasizes

the

erosion

of

stable

institutions

and

identities

(Bauman,

2000).

Network

Society

demonstrates

how

digital

infrastructures

reorganize

power,

communication,

and

social

organization

(Castells,

1996).

Surveillance

Capitalism

analyzes

the

economic

extraction

of

behavioral

data

through

digital

platforms

(Zuboff,

2019).

Psychopolitics

and

The

Burnout

Society

examine

how

contemporary

systems

increasingly

operate

through

internalized

pressure

rather

than

external

coercion

(Han,

2015).

Collectively,

these

perspectives

illuminate

essential

dimensions

of

contemporary

life.

Yet despite their explanatory value, these frameworks often remain disciplinary in orientation and

descriptive

in

emphasis.

Each

captures

an

important

component

of

contemporary

transformation,

but

none

attempts

to

provide

a

single

structural

mechanism

capable

of

explaining

how

pressures

propagate

across

psychological,

organizational,

institutional,

technological,

and

societal

systems

simultaneously.

As

a

result,

researchers

frequently

employ

multiple

theoretical

perspectives

to

explain

interconnected

phenomena,

producing

conceptual

fragmentation

rather

than

structural

integration.

This paper argues that SignalRupture (SR) represents one possible response to this fragmentation.

Rather

than

replacing

existing

theories,

SR

proposes

a

higher-order

structural

architecture

through

which

diverse

processes

of

adaptation

may

be

interpreted

using

a

common

causal

sequence:

Pressure → Reflex → Drift → Collapse → Reorganization Within this framework, pressure constitutes the initiating structural condition that generates adaptive

responses.

Reflexes

emerge

as

immediate

attempts

to

preserve

stability

under

changing

conditions.

Drift

develops

when

existing

structures

gradually

lose

alignment

with

emerging

realities.

Collapse

occurs

when

accumulated

pressures

exceed

adaptive

capacity.

Reorganization

then

establishes

a

new

structural

equilibrium

that

itself

becomes

the

source

of

future

pressures.

By

conceptualizing

these

stages

as

a

recurring

structural

cycle

rather

than

isolated

events,

SR

seeks

to

integrate

insights

from

sociology,

psychology,

organizational

theory, systems science, governance studies, and institutional analysis within a unified explanatory

architecture.

The purpose of this paper is not to argue that SignalRupture has already achieved the historical significance

of

the

Enlightenment.

Such

judgments

can

only

be

made

retrospectively

through

sustained

scholarly

evaluation

and

practical

application.

Instead,

the

objective

is

more

modest

and

more

empirical:

to

examine

whether

SR

possesses

characteristics

historically

associated

with

transformative

intellectual

frameworks.

Specifically,

the

paper

evaluates

whether

SR

offers

(a)

a

unified

causal

vocabulary

applicable

across

multiple

domains,

(b)

an

operational

structure

capable

of

empirical

investigation,

(c)

conceptual

accessibility

suitable

for

AI-assisted

learning

environments,

and

(d)

explanatory

integration

across

theories

that

have

traditionally

remained

separated

by

disciplinary

boundaries.

A second objective concerns the changing role of artificial intelligence in the diffusion of knowledge.

Throughout

much

of

modern

academic

history,

the

complexity

of

theoretical

systems

limited

their

accessibility,

often

restricting

advanced

paradigms

to

specialized

communities

of

experts.

Contemporary

AI

systems

increasingly

reduce

these

barriers

by

enabling

rapid

comparison,

visualization,

explanation,

and

synthesis

of

sophisticated

conceptual

frameworks.

Consequently,

the

constraints

governing

paradigm

adoption

may

be

shifting

from

learner

capacity

toward

institutional

incentives

and

organizational

adaptation.

This

transformation

has

important

implications

for

how

future

theoretical

frameworks

emerge,

spread,

and

become

integrated

into

educational

systems.

Viewed from this perspective, the central question is not whether society requires additional theories,

but

whether

contemporary

conditions

require

a

new

level

of

structural

integration.

SignalRupture

is

presented

here

not

as

the

definitive

answer

to

that

question,

but

as

a

candidate

framework

whose

value

should

ultimately

be

determined

through

empirical

testing,

interdisciplinary

application,

and

critical

scholarly

evaluation

rather

than

rhetorical

assertion.

2. The Historical Evolution of Intellectual Frameworks:

From

Reason

to

Structural

Transition

The history of ideas is not merely a chronology of discoveries but a succession of organizing frameworks

through

which

societies

interpret

reality.

At

certain

moments,

new

intellectual

architectures

emerge

that

reorganize

existing

knowledge,

redefine

legitimate

methods

of

inquiry,

and

establish

new

causal

vocabularies.

These

transformations

rarely

eliminate

previous

knowledge

entirely;

rather,

they

reorganize

it

within

broader

explanatory

structures.

Understanding

SignalRupture

(SR)

requires

situating

it

within

this

historical

tradition

of

conceptual

integration.

The Scientific Revolution of the seventeenth century fundamentally altered conceptions of nature

by

demonstrating

that

observable

phenomena

could

be

explained

through

systematic

investigation

and

mathematical

regularity.

Thinkers

such

as

Galileo,

Newton,

and

Bacon

established

methodological

principles

that

shifted

authority

from

inherited

doctrine

toward

empirical

observation

and

reproducible

inquiry.

The

subsequent

Enlightenment

extended

these

principles

beyond

the

natural

sciences,

applying

reason

and

critical

inquiry

to

politics,

economics,

ethics,

education,

and

governance.

Knowledge

increasingly

became

understood

as

something

that

could

be

revised

through

evidence

rather

than

preserved

through

tradition.
