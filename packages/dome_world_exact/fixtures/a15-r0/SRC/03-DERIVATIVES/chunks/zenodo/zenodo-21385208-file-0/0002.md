Throughout the nineteenth century, additional organizing frameworks reshaped intellectual life. Darwin’s

extending

(Freud,

1900).

Although

differed

previously

disconnected

The twentieth century witnessed another important transition as attention shifted from isolated phenomena

toward

structures,

and

Thomas

Kuhn

(1962)

through

that

until

generate

periods

of

crisis

and

eventual

transformation.

Kuhn’s

attention

scientific

alone

Knowledge

Subsequent scholarship expanded this systemic perspective across multiple domains. Ulrich Beck

(1992)

Zygmunt

Manuel

(1996)

Shoshana

Zuboff

(2019)

transform

human

behavior

economic

Byung-Chul

Collectively, these frameworks reveal a gradual evolution in the focus of social theory. Earlier intellectual

Contemporary

increasingly

Rather

increasingly

ask

evolve

under

Despite these advances, contemporary scholarship remains highly specialized. Risk theory, institutional

sociology,

As

Researchers

SignalRupture emerges within this intellectual landscape not as a replacement for existing theories

Rather

3. Why Existing Frameworks Become Insufficient:

Complexity,

Fragmentation,

and

the

Search

for

Structural

Integration

Scientific and social theories rarely disappear because they are entirely incorrect. More often, they

become

progressively

less

sufficient

as

the

environments

they

were

designed

to

explain

increase

in

complexity.

Throughout

the

history

of

science,

intellectual

transitions

have

generally

occurred

when

existing

conceptual

frameworks

continued

to

explain

portions

of

reality

while

proving

increasingly

unable

to

integrate

newly

emerging

phenomena.

Thomas

Kuhn

(1962)

described

this

process

as

the

accumulation

of

anomalies

leading

to

periods

of

conceptual

crisis

and

eventual

paradigm

transformation.

The

transition

is

therefore

not

a

rejection

of

previous

knowledge

but

an

expansion

of

explanatory

architecture.

The contemporary world exhibits many characteristics associated with such periods of structural transition.

Digital

infrastructures

connect

billions

of

individuals

instantaneously;

artificial

intelligence

increasingly

participates

in

knowledge

production;

governance

systems

confront

simultaneous

economic,

ecological,

demographic,

technological,

and

geopolitical

pressures;

and

institutions

operate

within

environments

characterized

by

unprecedented

informational

velocity.

Problems

that

once

appeared

isolated

now

propagate

rapidly

across

interconnected

systems,

producing

cascading

effects

that

frequently

cross

disciplinary

boundaries.

This growing interdependence has exposed an important limitation within modern scholarship: the

fragmentation

of

explanatory

frameworks.

Sociology,

psychology,

economics,

organizational

studies,

systems

science,

political

science,

public

administration,

and

information

science

often

analyze

different

dimensions

of

the

same

structural

phenomenon

while

employing

independent

conceptual

vocabularies.

As

a

result,

researchers

may

identify

similar

adaptive

dynamics

without

recognizing

their

underlying

structural

relationships.

For example, organizational scholars describe defensive routines that emerge when institutions encounter

threatening

information

(Argyris,

1990).

Psychologists

analyze

identity

and

cognitive

responses

to

disruption

(Breakwell,

1986).

Governance

researchers

examine

drift

and

Complexity

investigate

nonlinear

transitions

and

emergent

Systems

theorists

describe

while

researchers

examine

adaptive

cycles

(Holling,

1973).

Each

framework

contributes

valuable

yet

they

often

remain

conceptually

isolated

despite

addressing

comparable

processes

of

structural

adaptation.

This disciplinary specialization has generated considerable empirical knowledge but comparatively

limited

theoretical

integration.

Rather

than

producing

a

unified

structural

language,

contemporary

scholarship

frequently

develops

increasingly

sophisticated

descriptions

within

narrower

domains.

The

consequence

is

not

necessarily

theoretical

weakness

but

epistemic

compartmentalization,

where

explanations

become

difficult

to

compare

across

fields

despite

addressing

similar

forms

of

systemic

change.

SignalRupture proposes that many of these apparently distinct phenomena can be interpreted through

a

common

structural

sequence.

Instead

of

beginning

analysis

with

the

particular

domain—organization,

institution,

individual,

technology,

or

begins

with

the

dynamics

of

structural

adaptation

itself.

Within

this

perspective,

pressure

functions

as

the

initiating

condition

that

generates

attempts

Reflexes

emerge

as

immediate

adaptive responses intended to preserve existing structures. Drift develops when these responses

no

longer

maintain

alignment

with

changing

conditions.

Collapse

represents

the

failure

of

accumulated

adaptive

strategies,

while

reorganization

establishes

a

revised

structural

equilibrium

capable

of

responding

to

newly

emerging

pressures.

Importantly, SR does not claim that all systems behave identically or that every form of change follows

a

Rather,

proposes

The

of

to

between

fields

that

have

traditionally

remained

separated.
