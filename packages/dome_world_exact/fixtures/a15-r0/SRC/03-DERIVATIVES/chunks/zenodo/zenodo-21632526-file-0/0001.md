COLLAPSE CRIMINOLOGY: THE OFFENDER INSIDE QUIET GOVERNANCE

An Empirical Field Manuscript on Offending, Victimization, Enforcement, Institutional

Friction,

and

Structural

Erosion

SignalRupture (SR) · 2026

Abstract Collapse Criminology is defined here as the study of offending, victimization, enforcement,

and

justice-system

response

under

persistent

deterioration

in

institutional

capacity,

accessibility,

coherence,

legitimacy,

or

social

reproduction.

Its

distinct

object

is

not

'crime

during

collapse'

in

a

deterministic

sense.

It

is

the

changing

relationship

between

structural

conditions

and

crime-related

outcomes

when

formal

systems

remain

present

but

the

environments

in

which

people

act,

seek

protection,

or

encounter

justice

become

uneven,

burdensome,

or

degraded. The field is tested against current Canadian evidence. National police-reported crime

in

2024

provides

an

important

null:

the

Crime

Severity

Index

declined

4%,

demonstrating

that

structural

pressure

cannot

be

assumed

to

generate

uniform

increases

in

crime.

Offense

categories

diverged

sharply,

however,

with

shoplifting

under

$5,000

rising

14%

while

breaking

and

entering

fell

11%

and

motor-vehicle

theft

fell

17%.

Rural

areas

showed

a

different

trajectory:

their

2024

crime

rate

was

42%

above

the

urban

rate

and

their

violent

Crime

Severity

Index

was

46%

higher.

Self-reported

victimization

data

also

show

strong

structural

exposure

gradients:

people

who

had

experienced

homelessness

reported

violent

victimization

at

three

times

the

rate

of

those

who

had

never

been

homeless,

and

recent

homelessness

was

associated

with

a

rate

nearly

five

times

as

high.

In

2023/2024,

58,666

adult

criminal

court

cases

took

more

than

one

year

to

complete,

about

one

quarter

of

cases

with

measured

elapsed

time. These results support context sensitivity, structural exposure in victimization, administrative-delay

burden,

and

the

need

to

model

offense

types

separately.

They do not establish that structural erosion causes crime generally, that offenders

lack

agency,

that

algorithmic

systems

are

a

dominant

cause

of

offending,

or

that

crime

is

a

reliable

leading

indicator

of

societal

collapse.

Collapse

Criminology

is

therefore

presented

as

an

empirically

grounded

emerging

field

with

partial

mechanism

support

and

substantial

causal

work

still

required. Keywords: collapse criminology; structural erosion; strain; victimization; administrative

burden;

rural

crime;

court

delay;

algorithmic

mediation;

quiet

governance;

reconstruction

Preface Collapse Criminology begins from a narrower empirical problem: offending, victimization,

enforcement,

and

justice-system

contact

occur

inside

changing

institutional,

economic,

technological,

and

social

environments.

The

field

asks

whether

persistent

deterioration

in

accessibility,

capacity,

coherence,

legitimacy,

or

opportunity

changes

the

distribution

and

form

of

crime-related

outcomes.

Contemporary offending can be shaped by structural forces including strain, institutional

accessibility,

administrative

friction,

dependency,

technological

classification,

neighbourhood

conditions,

opportunity,

and

social

support.

Existing

criminology

already

studies

many

of

these

mechanisms.

Collapse

Criminology

contributes

by

organizing

them

around

a

distinct

problem:

crime-related

outcomes

under

persistent

deterioration

of

institutional

capacity,

accessibility,

coherence,

and

legitimacy.

Criminology has not yet absorbed the psychological collapse that precedes the

criminal

act.

It

continues

to

analyze

crime

as

deviation

rather

than

adaptation,

as

pathology

rather

than

response,

as

choice

rather

than

erosion.

It

continues

to

treat

offenders

as

autonomous

actors

navigating

coherent

systems, even as those systems drift, fragment, and govern through design rather

than

law.

Collapse Criminology is proposed as an emerging criminological research field. Its

status

depends

on

whether

its

distinct

constructs

and

models

add

explanatory

value

beyond

established

criminological

theories.

It provides society with a discipline built for the world as it is — unstable, algorithmic,

administratively

mediated,

narratively

fragmented

—

and

for

the

offender

who

must

navigate

it.

Collapse

Criminology

begins

where

traditional

criminology

ends,

offering

a

framework

for

understanding

crime

not

as

an

isolated

act

but

as

the

behavioral

expression

of

erosion.

Lived and street-level experience can generate hypotheses and reveal mechanisms,

but

it

is

not

a

substitute

for

sampling,

comparison,

measurement,

or

causal

identification.

Institutional

models

are

likewise

hypotheses

that

must

be

tested

against

observed

conditions.

Collapse Criminology is the architecture for the post ‑ collapse offender, the foundation

of

reconstruction,

and

the

declaration

that

a

potential

contribution

to

criminology

begins

not

with

deviance

or

but

with

the

deliberate

rebuilding

of

agency,

identity,

and

meaning

inside

environments

where

erosion

is

not

an

event

but

a

condition.

Literature Review Collapse Criminology is presented here as a new SR research program, not as an

already

established

consensus

within

criminology.

Its

central

claim

is

that

contemporary

offending

and

victimization

may

be

shaped

by

cumulative

erosion across administrative, infrastructural, algorithmic, emotional, and institutional

environments.

Existing

criminology

does

not

use

the

complete

Collapse

Criminology

vocabulary,

but

several

mature

literatures

provide

testable

foundations

for

parts

of

the

architecture.

The

purpose

of

this

review

is

therefore

not

to

claim

prior

validation

of

SR

constructs.

It

is

to

identify

where

established

evidence

supports

component

mechanisms,

where

Collapse

Criminology

extends

them,

and

where

new

empirical

testing

is

required.

1. Strain, blocked goals, and adaptation under pressure General strain theory provides the clearest established criminological bridge to

the

SR

proposition

that

persistent

adverse

conditions

can

alter

behavioral

options.

Agnew

(1992)

formalized

strain

around

failure

to

achieve

positively

valued

goals,

removal

of

positive

stimuli,

and

exposure

to

negative

stimuli,

while

later

work

extended

strain

analysis

to

community-level

differences

in

crime

rates

(Agnew,

1999).

This

literature

supports

the

narrower

proposition

that

accumulated

pressure

can

increase

criminogenic

risk

under

specified

conditions.

Collapse

Criminology

extends

that

logic

by

asking

whether

administrative

friction,

infrastructural

dependency,

algorithmic

exclusion,

and

repeated

access

denial

can

be

operationalized

as

contemporary

sources

of

strain.

It

does

not

follow

from

general

strain

theory

that

such

conditions

inevitably

produce

crime;

the

SR

claim

is

probabilistic

and

requires

measurement

of

mediating

factors,

coping

resources,

social

support,

opportunity,

and

individual

heterogeneity.

2. Administrative burden, discretion, and friction

Lipsky (1980) demonstrated that frontline public-service systems operate through

discretion

under

conditions

of

limited

resources,

ambiguous

goals,

and

high

caseloads.

Herd

and

Moynihan

(2018)

subsequently

developed

administrative

burden

as

a

framework

for

learning,

compliance,

and

psychological

costs

imposed

through

citizens'

interactions

with

the

state.

These

literatures

do

not

establish

“Quiet

Governance”

as

a

criminogenic

mechanism,

but

they

make

its

proposed

components

empirically

tractable.

Friction

can

be

decomposed

into

waiting

time,

documentation

requirements,

procedural

complexity,

inconsistent

decisions,

repeated

applications,

appeal

costs,

and

uncertainty.

Collapse

Criminology

therefore

treats

friction

not

as

an

assumed

cause

of

offending

but

as

a

measurable

exposure

whose

relationship

to

rule-breaking,

disengagement,

informal

workarounds,

and

survival

behavior

can

be

tested.

3. Structural inequality, social control, and institutional context Criminology has long rejected purely individual explanations of crime. Strain,

social

disorganization,

life-course,

critical,

and

institutional

perspectives

all

locate

behavior

within

social

structure.

Collapse

Criminology

enters

this

lineage

while

shifting

the

unit

of

attention

toward

cumulative

erosion

across

systems.

Its

distinctive

hypothesis

is

that

criminogenic

exposure

may

arise

not

only

from

neighborhood

disadvantage

or

interpersonal

strain,

but

also

from

cross-system

interaction:

housing

instability

combined

with

administrative

burden;

economic

precarity

combined

with

access

denial;

surveillance

combined

with

classification;

or

institutional

delay

combined

with

emotional

saturation.

The

empirical

task

is

to determine whether these combined exposures explain variance beyond established

criminological

predictors

rather

than

simply

renaming

them.

4. Datafication, surveillance, and algorithmic criminal justice Contemporary research on data-intensive policing provides a direct basis for examining

algorithmic

mediation.

Brayne

(2020)

documents

how

large-scale

data

systems

alter

police

surveillance,

discretion,

and

the

production

of

suspicion.

Related

on

automated

decision

systems

has

shown

that

classification

technologies

can

distribute

opportunity,

scrutiny,

and

administrative

burden

unevenly.

Collapse

Criminology

builds

from

this

literature

by

treating

algorithmic

mediation

as

part

of

the

environment

in

which

offenders,

victims,

police,

courts,

and

institutions

act.

The

defensible

claim

is

not

that

algorithms

automatically

cause

crime.

It

is

that

algorithmic

systems

can

change

access,

visibility,

surveillance,

classification,

and

decision

pathways,

and

these

changes

may

interact

with

established

criminogenic

mechanisms.

5. Para-crime and the changing location of illegality Vigh and Sausdal (2025) provide an especially important contemporary point of

contact.

Their

concept

describes

of

that

Their

“grey

does

Collapse

Criminology

as

but

the

proposition

that

boundary

becomes

harder

to

analyze

when

harmful

or

is

entangled

systems.

This

directly

to

SR

categories

such as access-crime, infrastructure-mediated crime, and governance-embedded

offending,

which

should

be

treated

as

hypotheses

requiring

clear

coding

rules

and

empirical

discrimination

from

existing

offence
