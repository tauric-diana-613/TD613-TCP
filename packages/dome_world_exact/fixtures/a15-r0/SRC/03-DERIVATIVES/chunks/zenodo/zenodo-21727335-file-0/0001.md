COLLAPSE EPISTEMOLOGY: THE ARCHITECTURE OF ERODED KNOWLEDGE

An Empirical Field Manuscript on the Structural Conditions of Usable Knowledge SignalRupture (SR) · 2026 Field Definition, Prior-Art Audit, Formal Architecture, Empirical Tests, Falsification, and

Recovery

Preface Collapse Epistemology begins with a blunt truth: knowledge can erode before institutions

visibly

fail.

The

field

studies

the

conditions

under

which

truth-tracking,

meaning,

narrative

continuity,

interpretation,

and

epistemic

authority

become

unstable

while

the

institutional

shell

remains

operational.

Traditional epistemology asks what knowledge is, what justifies belief, and what

distinguishes

knowledge

from

error.

Collapse

Epistemology

asks

a

different

question:

what

happens

when

the

environments

that

make

knowledge

usable

become

structurally

unstable?

It

treats

epistemic

instability

not

as

an

exceptional

crisis

but

as

a

condition

that

can

be

produced

by

acceleration,

platform

mediation,

informational

saturation,

institutional

distrust,

recursive

synthetic

information,

and

algorithmic

classification.

•

truth that becomes unstable •

meaning that becomes metricized •

narratives that dissolve into fragments •

interpretation increasingly mediated by algorithms

•

legitimacy reorganized through visibility •

knowledge converted into affective and classificatory signals •

authority performed without equivalent epistemic trust •

understanding displaced by prediction and categorization This is not an epistemology of dissolution because truth literally disappears. It

is

an

epistemology

of

dissolution

because

the

social

and

technical

conditions

that

allow

knowledge

to

remain

anchored,

interpretable,

contestable,

and

institutionally

actionable

can

erode.

Collapse Epistemology is proposed here as an emerging interdisciplinary epistemic

field.

Its

strongest

claims

are

framed

as

SR

propositions

to

be

operationalized

and

tested,

not

as

conclusions

already

established

by

the

cited

literature.

Field

status

rests

on

a

distinct

longitudinal

research

object

and

cumulative

empirical

program,

not

on

the

novelty

of

every

component

mechanism.

Author’s Note: On Structural Inevitability SR retains a structural orientation, but the language of inevitability requires precision.

Structures

can

create

path

dependence,

lock-in,

threshold

effects,

and

strongly

constrained

trajectories

without

making

every

outcome

logically

unavoidable.

Accordingly,

this

manuscript

distinguishes

structural

pressure

from

deterministic

certainty.

The SR claim is not that the future can be known in advance. The claim is that

recurrent

mechanisms

can

make

some

trajectories

increasingly

probable

when countervailing mechanisms weaken. Collapse Epistemology therefore treats

irreversibility

as

an

empirical

question:

a

trajectory

is

irreversible

only

when

recovery

capacity,

substitution,

institutional

correction,

or

epistemic

anchoring

falls

below

a

demonstrable

recovery

threshold.

The canonical SR formulation remains: collapse is a process before it is an event.

The

research

task

is

to

identify

the

mechanisms,

indicators,

thresholds,

and

counterexamples

that

determine

whether

that

process

accelerates,

stabilizes,

or

reverses.

Literature Review Collapse Epistemology draws from epistemology, sociology, media theory, science

and

technology

studies,

political

theory,

cognitive

psychology,

and

platform

studies.

The

literature

does

not

independently

establish

the

SR

architecture.

Rather,

it

documents

mechanisms

that

motivate

the

field:

post-truth

dynamics,

institutional

trust,

algorithmic

mediation,

classification,

surveillance,

acceleration,

information

overload,

simulation,

1. Epistemic Instability and Post-Truth Conditions Bauman’s account of liquid modernity supplies a vocabulary for institutional and

social

fluidity,

but

it

should

not

be

treated

as

a

direct

empirical

demonstration

that

truth

itself

becomes

fluid

(Bauman,

2000).

McIntyre (2018) directly addresses post-truth conditions and the political devaluation

of

factual

truth.

Frankfurt

(2005)

analyzes

indifference

to

truth

through the concept of bullshit. Together, these works support a narrower SR proposition:

epistemic

norms

can

weaken

even

where

information

remains

abundant.

Collapse Epistemology extends this literature by defining epistemic drift as change

in

the

stability,

provenance,

coherence,

or

actionability

of

knowledge

claims

over

time.

2. Algorithmic Mediation and Machine-Produced Knowledge Zuboff (2019) examines behavioral prediction and surveillance capitalism; Noble

(2018)

demonstrates

how

search

and

classification

can

reproduce

social

hierarchies;

Gillespie

(2018)

shows

how

platform

moderation

and

visibility

decisions

shape

public

discourse.

These sources do not establish that algorithms literally replace all human judgment.

They

support

the

more

defensible

proposition

that

computational

systems

increasingly

mediate

discovery,

ranking,

classification,

and

visibility.

Recent

work

on

AI-mediated

knowledge

environments

also

describes

epistemic

harms

as

conditional

socio-technical

effects

rather

than

inevitable

outcomes.

SR names the stronger mechanism algorithmic substitution: the migration of an

epistemic

function

from

human

or

institutional

interpretation

to

computational

classification,

prediction,

ranking,

or

synthesis.

3. Narrative, Simulation, and Meaning

Baudrillard’s theory of simulation, Fisher’s account of capitalist realism, and Han’s

critique

of

transparency

provide

conceptual

resources

for

studying

symbolic

saturation,

constrained

imagination,

and

visibility

(Baudrillard,

1994;

Fisher,

2009;

Han,

2015a).

Collapse Epistemology does not treat these authors as empirical proof of a universal

narrative

collapse.

It

uses

to

formulate

testable

questions

about

narrative

fragmentation,

platform

sequencing,

attention,

and

the

replacement

of

contextual

interpretation

by

metricized

signals.

4. Epistemic Authority and Institutional Legitimacy Habermas (1989) analyzes transformations of the public sphere; Luhmann (1979)

theorizes

trust

as

a

mechanism

for

reducing

social

complexity;

Brown

(2015)

analyzes

neoliberal

transformations

of

democratic

SR synthesizes these lines into a hypothesis about epistemic authority: institutions

may

retain

formal

authority

while

losing

their

capacity

to

stabilize

shared

interpretations,

trusted

procedures,

and

credible

knowledge

claims.

5. Saturation, Acceleration, and Cognitive Load Rosa (2013) theorizes social acceleration; Han (2015b) examines exhaustion and

achievement

society;

Kahneman

(2011)

synthesizes

research

on

judgment

and

cognitive

processing.

The manuscript therefore replaces categorical statements such as 'overload makes

interpretation

impossible'

with

a

graded

proposition:

increasing

informational

load,

time

pressure,

affective

activation,

and

source

conflict

can

reduce the depth, consistency, or reliability of interpretation under specified conditions.

6. Science and Technology Studies: Knowledge as Socio-Technical

Production

Latour (1993), Haraway (1991), and Hacking (1999) each challenge simple separations

between

knowledge,

categories,

institutions,

technologies,

and

social

organization.

These works support a central methodological commitment of Collapse Epistemology:

epistemic

outcomes

should

be

studied

as

products

of

interacting

human,

institutional,

classificatory,

and

technical

systems

rather

than

as

properties

of

isolated

individuals.
