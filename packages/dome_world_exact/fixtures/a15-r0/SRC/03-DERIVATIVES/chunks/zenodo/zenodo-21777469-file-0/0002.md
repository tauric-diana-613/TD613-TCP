Chapter 3 — Classical Theories and the Limits

of

Institutional

Agency

Classical theories of the state were built for an era in which governments operated primarily through

institutions

they

owned,

staffed,

and

controlled.

Bureaucratic

capacity,

administrative

coherence,

and

formal

authority

were

treated

as

the

core

determinants

of

state

agency.

This

chapter

examines

those

traditions,

identifies

their

analytical

boundaries,

and

explains

why

cannot

fully

account

for

the

infrastructural

conditions

that

define

contemporary

governance.

1. Bureaucratic Capacity and Administrative Autonomy Max Weber’s model of bureaucratic authority positioned the state as an organization whose agency

derived

from

hierarchy,

professionalization,

and

procedural

rationality.

In

this

tradition,

the

state

acts

through:

● Formal authority — legally defined powers ● Administrative coherence — internal coordination ● Professional bureaucracy — trained civil servants These components assume that the state’s operational systems are internal. The model presumes

that

the

infrastructures

through

which

the

state

acts—communications,

logistics,

information

systems—are

part

of

the

state’s

own

administrative

machinery.

In the modern infrastructural environment, this assumption no longer holds. 2. State Capacity and Institutional Strength Contemporary political economy expanded Weberian theory by emphasizing state capacity , defined

through:

● Extraction capacity — ability to raise revenue ● Coercive capacity — ability to enforce rules ● Implementation capacity — ability to deliver programs These models treat capacity as an internal property of the state. They assume that the state’s ability

to

act

depends

on

its

own

institutions,

not

on

external

infrastructures.

However, modern governments rely on privately governed systems for taxation, enforcement, and

implementation.

Payment

networks,

cloud

platforms,

and

logistics

chains

mediate

core

state

functions.

Capacity

is

no

longer

purely

institutional;

it

is

infrastructural.

3. Autonomy and Embeddedness Theories of state autonomy —from Skocpol to Evans—argue that states possess independent preferences

and

can

act

against

societal

pressures.

They

distinguish

between:

● Autonomous states — capable of independent action ● Embedded states — integrated with societal actors These frameworks focus on interest groups, political coalitions, and institutional design. They do not

account

for

infrastructural

operators

whose

power

derives

not

from

political

mobilization

but

from

ownership

and

control

of

essential

systems.

Infrastructural operators can constrain state action even without political conflict. Their power is structural,

not

political.

4. Why Classical Theories Fall Short Classical theories share three assumptions that no longer hold: ● The state owns its operational systems. Modern

states

rely

on

external

infrastructures. ● Institutional capacity determines agency. Infrastructural

dependency

can

override

institutional

strength. ● Power is political or bureaucratic. Infrastructural

power

is

neither;

it

is

operational.

These assumptions limit the ability of classical theories to explain how modern states act within privatized

infrastructural

environments.

They

cannot

account

for

situations

in

which:

● A telecommunications operator can shape emergency response. ● A cloud provider can determine the continuity of government services. ● A logistics chain can constrain national distribution capacity. ● A wage ‑ setting mechanism can influence public ‑ sector stability. The GAUID/GAI framework emerges from these limitations. It provides a structural model that incorporates

infrastructural

dependency,

substitution

capacity,

regulatory

and

structural

power—dimensions

Chapter 4 — Conceptual Architecture of the

GAUID/GAI

Framework

The GAUID/GAI framework provides the conceptual foundation for analyzing government agency

under

infrastructure

dependency .

This

defines

components

the

explains

their

and

establishes

that

modern

act

within

infrastructural

The framework is built on four interdependent concepts: ● Infrastructure Dependency ● Substitution Capacity ● Regulatory Leverage ● Structural Power These components form the conceptual architecture of GAUID/GAI. They define the operational boundaries

of

state

agency

and

reveal

how

infrastructural

conditions

shape

the

ability

of

governments

to

Infrastructure Dependency (ID) Infrastructure Dependency refers to the degree to which government operations rely on infrastructures

owned

or

controlled

by

external

operators.

Dependency

is

not

merely

technical;

it

is

operational,

institutional,

and

strategic.

A

government

is

dependent

when:

● essential services require external infrastructures ● continuity of operations relies on private systems ● crisis response is mediated by operator cooperation ● substitution is costly, slow, or infeasible High dependency reduces agency because the state cannot act without the cooperation or stability

of

external

systems.

Substitution Capacity (SC) Substitution Capacity measures the ability of a government to replace, bypass, or reconfigure infrastructures

when

operators

fail,

resist,

or

impose

constraints.

Substitution

can

take

multiple

forms:

● technical substitution (alternative networks or platforms) ● operational substitution (manual or parallel systems) ● institutional substitution (public alternatives or emergency powers) Low substitution capacity amplifies dependency. High substitution capacity mitigates it.

Regulatory Leverage (RL) Regulatory Leverage captures the degree of influence government can exert over infrastructural operators

through:

● legislation ● oversight ● compliance mechanisms ● enforcement powers ● licensing and sectoral regulation Regulatory leverage is not equivalent to ownership. A government may regulate an operator without

being

able

to

compel

operational

cooperation

during

crisis

or

conflict.

Leverage

reduces

dependency

but

does

not

eliminate

it.

Structural Power (SP) Structural Power refers to the ability of infrastructural operators to shape, constrain, or condition state

action

through

their

control

of

essential

systems.

Structural

power

is

not

political

lobbying

or

negotiation;

it

is

operational.

Operators

possess

structural

power

when:

● government cannot act without their systems ● substitution is infeasible ● regulatory leverage is insufficient ● dependency is high Structural power is the inverse of agency. As structural power increases, government agency decreases.

Interdependence of the Four Components The four components interact to determine the GAI (Government Agency Index). Their relationships

are

non ‑ linear

and

context ‑ dependent:

● High ID + Low SC → severe constraint ● High ID + High RL → moderated constraint ● High SP + Low RL → operator dominance ● High SC + High RL → strong agency These interactions form the conceptual logic of the GAUID/GAI framework. They explain why governments

with

strong

institutions

may

still

experience

infrastructural

constraint

and

why

infrastructural

operators

can

possess

operational

veto

power

even

without

political

conflict.

1. Core Variables Let: ● ID ∈ [0,1] = Infrastructure Dependency ● SC ∈ [0,1] = Substitution Capacity ● RL ∈ [0,1] = Regulatory Leverage ● SP ∈ [0,1] = Structural Power ● GAI = f(ID, SC, RL, SP) Agency is represented as: [ GAI = f(ID, SC, RL, SP) ] The function ( f ) is non ‑ linear because infrastructural constraint is non ‑ linear.

2. Base Interaction Structure The foundational relationship is: GAI = (1 − ID) + SC + RL − SP This expresses the intuitive logic: ● Higher ID reduces agency ● Higher SC increases agency ● Higher RL increases agency ● Higher SP reduces agency But this linear form is insufficient.
