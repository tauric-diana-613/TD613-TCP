10. Discussion: Private-Sector Structural Power and the Limits of Government

Agency

10.1 Formal authority versus operational agency The principal theoretical contribution of GAUID is to distinguish legal authority from operational

ability.

A

may

the

authority

to

regulate

an

infrastructure

operator

while

lacking

a

credible

substitute

if

the

operator

refuses,

fails,

or

becomes

unavailable.

This

creates

a

form

of

dependency

that

is

difficult

to

observe

through

constitutional

or

statutory

analysis

alone

(Evans,

1995;

Farrell

&

Newman,

2019).

10.2 Market concentration as an agency mechanism Market concentration matters to GAUID because fewer providers can increase switching

costs

and

reduce

credible

alternatives.

Dal

Bó

(2006)

and

Vogel

(2021)

provide

foundations

for

understanding

how

market

structure

and

regulatory

arrangements

can

generate

political

leverage.

GAUID

adds

a

distinct

operational

dimension:

concentration

can

also

reduce

the

state's

ability

to

substitute

away

from

a

provider.

10.3 Outsourcing and public service provision Research on public-private partnerships and outsourcing suggests that the effects of private

provision

depend

on

institutional

design,

contractual

incentives,

monitoring,

and

accountability

mechanisms

(Vining

&

Boardman,

2008).

GAUID

does

not

assume

that

necessarily

reduces

agency;

it

predicts

that

the

effect

depends

on

the

availability

of

alternatives,

contractual

design,

monitoring,

and

regulatory

leverage.

10.4 Private governance Uribe (2026), together with DeNardis (2020) and Farrell and Newman (2019), is relevant

to

the

proposition

that

private

actors

can

participate

in

governance

beyond

influence

becomes

an

operational

constraint

rather

of

10.5 Theoretical contribution GAUID therefore makes four claims that should remain analytically distinct: dependency

11. Policy Implications 11.1 Rebuild credible substitutes •

public or hybrid digital infrastructure •

interoperable telecommunications and emergency communications •

public or diversified payment and clearing alternatives •

strategic logistics redundancy •

energy and grid redundancy 11.2 Increase substitution capacity •

11.3 Strengthen regulatory leverage •

11.6 Continuous agency monitoring •

cross-national benchmarking 12. Limitations •

The current Canadian coefficients are provisional parameterizations, not regression-estimated

national

weights.

•

Infrastructure boundaries differ across sectors and countries, requiring transparent coding

rules.

•

Structural Power is latent and requires multiple observable indicators for identification.

•

Regulatory Leverage cannot be inferred from enforcement counts alone. •

Wage-setting should be treated as a functional subsystem only where empirical evidence

establishes

operational

relevance.

•

Cross-national comparisons require harmonization of definitions, time periods, and sectoral

coverage.

•

Causal inference requires outcomes independent of the GAI itself; otherwise validation

risks

circularity.

•

Threshold values should be estimated from observed disruption or implementation outcomes

rather

than

imposed

universally.

All

and

weights,

regulatory

The framework also specifies nonlinear interactions, threshold effects, crisis coding, sectoral

declines
