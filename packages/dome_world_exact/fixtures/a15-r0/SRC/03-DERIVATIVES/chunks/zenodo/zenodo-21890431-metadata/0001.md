    "description": "<p>The SR Collapse Path is introduced as a <strong>structural field theory</strong> that explains how drift emerges, propagates, amplifies, and becomes institutionally consequential across interconnected sociotechnical systems. The manuscript positions SR as an <strong>infrastructure‑oriented analytical geometry</strong> rather than an outage taxonomy or a deterministic failure sequence. Its central contribution is the articulation of a <strong>six‑stage diagnostic path&mdash;Node Drift &rarr; Model Drift &rarr; Substrate Drift &rarr; Hyperscale Drift &rarr; Human‑Capacity Collapse &rarr; Institutional Failure</strong>&mdash;that makes the progression from local deviation to institutional consequence <strong>empirically legible</strong> and <strong>theoretically measurable</strong>.</p>\n<p>SR contributes a set of <strong>formal constructs</strong> that allow institutions to treat stability as an <strong>empirical object rather than a metaphor</strong>. These include <strong>drift load</strong>, <strong>amplification gain</strong>, <strong>dependency concentration</strong>, <strong>cross‑layer consistency</strong>, <strong>human compensatory margin</strong>, <strong>governance recovery capacity</strong>, and <strong>collapse pressure</strong>. The manuscript emphasizes that <strong>modern institutional stability is often produced not by perfect infrastructure, but by layers of human compensation, procedural adaptation, redundancy, and recovery</strong> (SR Collapse Path, Preface). This insight reframes institutional failure from an <strong>event‑centered problem</strong> to a <strong>substrate‑centered problem</strong>.</p>\n<p>The SR Collapse Path also contributes a <strong>falsifiable state‑transition architecture</strong>. Each stage is analytically separable but empirically overlapping, allowing systems to move backward through repair or bypass stages when coupling is direct. The manuscript explicitly distinguishes SR from deterministic failure models, noting that <strong>&ldquo;the geometry is therefore best understood as a state‑transition architecture rather than a claim of mechanical inevitability&rdquo;</strong> (SR Collapse Path, Section 2). This provides a foundation for <strong>empirical testing across sectors and infrastructures</strong>.</p>\n<p>A further contribution is the integration of <strong>sociotechnical research</strong>, <strong>infrastructure engineering</strong>, and <strong>cross‑sector ICT failure analysis</strong>. The manuscript draws on evidence from digital‑health risk research and major ICT incidents to demonstrate that <strong>configuration, automation, centralized dependencies, recovery processes, and human‑machine interaction can interact to produce cross‑system consequences</strong> (Jabin, 2026; Adel et al., 2025). By situating these interactions within a <strong>propagation geometry</strong>, SR provides a <strong>unified analytical vocabulary</strong> for understanding how drift becomes structurally consequential.</p>\n<p>The SR Collapse Path advances the concept of <strong>infrastructure debt</strong>, defined as <strong>&ldquo;the accumulated structural burden created by unresolved drift, deferred maintenance, brittle dependencies, temporary workarounds, outdated interfaces, insufficient redundancy, and repeated recovery without substrate correction&rdquo;</strong> (SR Collapse Path, Section 11). This formulation makes infrastructure debt <strong>measurable</strong> and connects it directly to <strong>collapse pressure</strong>, providing institutions with a diagnostic mechanism for assessing <strong>long‑term vulnerability</strong>.</p>\n<p>Finally, SR contributes a <strong>repair geometry</strong> that distinguishes <strong>recovery</strong> from <strong>structural repair</strong>. Recovery restores function; repair reduces the conditions that produced the failure. As the manuscript states, <strong>&ldquo;a system can recover while infrastructure debt continues to rise&rdquo;</strong> (SR Collapse Path, Section 15). This distinction enables institutions to evaluate whether post‑incident actions <strong>reduce future propagation potential</strong> or merely restore <strong>temporary operational states</strong>.</p>\n<p>In summary, the SR Collapse Path contributes:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A measurable geometry of drift propagation and institutional collapse.</strong></p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A set of formal constructs for quantifying drift, coupling, amplification, compensatory capacity, and collapse pressure.</strong></p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A falsifiable state‑transition architecture applicable across sectors.</strong></p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A unified sociotechnical and infrastructure‑theory framework.</strong></p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A measurable definition of infrastructure debt and its relationship to collapse risk.</strong></p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>A repair geometry that distinguishes structural repair from operational recovery.</strong></p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Together, these contributions establish SR as a <strong>field of infrastructure theory</strong> concerned with the <strong>substrate conditions </strong>through which institutions maintain <strong>coherence</strong>, <strong>coordinated action</strong>, and <strong>recoverable operational states</strong>.</p>",
