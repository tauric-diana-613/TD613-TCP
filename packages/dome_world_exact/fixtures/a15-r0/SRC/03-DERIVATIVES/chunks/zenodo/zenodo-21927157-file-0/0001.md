SR ANTHROPOLOGY — THE HUMAN UNDER STRUCTURAL EROSION SR Anthropology The Human Under Structural Erosion An Empirically Grounded Field Monograph in the SR Canon SignalRupture (SR) 2026 Preface SR Anthropology emerges from a simple observation: the human has changed

faster

than

many

of

the

conceptual

frameworks

used

to

study

contemporary

human

life.

Classical anthropology was never a discipline of simple stability; it has long

studied

mobility,

colonialism,

rupture,

exchange,

migration,

conflict,

hybridity,

and

social

transformation.

The

problem

addressed

here

is

therefore

not

that

anthropology

cannot

study

change.

The

problem

is

narrower

and

more

consequential:

contemporary

human

experience

is

increasingly

organized

through

infrastructures,

platforms,

data

systems,

administrative

architectures,

and

algorithmic

classifications

that

cross

the

conventional

boundaries

of

locality,

institution,

and

culture.

SR

Anthropology

proposes

a

vocabulary

for

studying

the

human

when

those

systems

become

persistent

conditions

of

identity,

belonging,

agency,

and

meaning.

The field is oriented toward collapse-era conditions characterized by:

•

infrastructural dependency •

algorithmic mediation •

institutional thinning and capacity loss •

drift-normalized expectations •

narrative fragmentation and meaning scarcity •

global platform dependence •

erosion of durable social anchors SR Anthropology does not replace classical anthropology. It extends anthropological

inquiry

into

environments

where

culture

remains

powerful

but

is

increasingly

co-produced

with

infrastructures.

Its

central

object

is

the

human

under

structural

erosion:

a

human

whose

social

world

is

simultaneously

cultural,

institutional,

infrastructural,

and

algorithmic.

It provides a conceptual vocabulary for identity fragmentation, meaning scarcity,

infrastructural

belonging,

algorithmic

selfhood,

drift-adapted

behavior,

hollow

community,

and

cross-context

variation.

These

are

proposed

SR

constructs,

not

settled

empirical

facts.

Their

value

depends

on

whether

they

can

be

operationalized,

distinguished

from

neighboring

concepts,

and

supported

or

falsified

through

fieldwork.

Author’s Note This manuscript was written in response to a recurring observation: modern life

can

feel

unstable,

fragmented,

and

increasingly

mediated

by

systems

that

operate

beyond

any

single

person’s

intention.

Across

households,

workplaces,

institutions,

neighborhoods,

cities,

and

digital

environments,

familiar

structures may remain visibly present while their capacity to provide continuity,

intelligibility,

or

dependable

action

changes.

The chapters that follow draw on anthropology, sociology, infrastructure studies,

media

studies,

political

theory,

and

the

existing

SR

canon.

They

also

formalize

a

set

of

SR

propositions—erosion,

drift,

hollowness,

infrastructural

dependency,

and

algorithmic

mediation—as

hypotheses

be

studied

rather

than

conclusions

to

be

presumed.

This work does not use collapse to mean apocalypse or inevitable terminal breakdown.

Collapse

is

used

analytically

to

describe

patterned

deterioration,

loss

of

functional

capacity,

weakening

of

coordination,

or

degradation

of

meaning

and

institutional

That

The book is offered as a map, not a verdict: a framework for observing how human

is

reorganized

when

and

information

consequential

to

everyday

experience.

— SR Abstract SR Anthropology is an empirically grounded emerging anthropological

research

field

examining

human

subject

formation

under

cumulative

infrastructural,

institutional,

administrative,

platform,

and

algorithmic

mediation.

Its

distinctive

object

is

not

infrastructure,

algorithms,

precarity,

or

cultural

change

separately;

anthropology

and

adjacent disciplines already study all of them. Its object is the changing

human

configuration

produced

when

these

systems

jointly

condition

agency,

identity,

belonging,

visibility,

access,

and

life

trajectories

across

settings

and

over

time.

The

field

retains

five

analytical

dimensions

-

Eroded,

Infrastructural,

Algorithmic,

Drift,

and

Hollow

-

but

treats

them

as

measurable

dimensions

rather

than

fixed

human

types.

The monograph confronts prior art in globalization, multi-sited ethnography,

infrastructure

anthropology,

digital

anthropology,

algorithmic

management,

datafication,

precarity,

acceleration,

bureaucracy,

identity,

and

social-media

research.

It

then

subjects

SR

Anthropology

to

current

empirical

tests.

Evidence

from

randomized

social-media

deactivation,

platform-work

surveys,

algorithmic-management

ethnography,

digital-identification

studies,

biometric-welfare

experiments,

online-identity

experiments,

platform

outages,

and

large-scale

feed-ranking

studies

supports

several

bounded

propositions:

infrastructures

and

can

materially

condition

access,

autonomy,

exposure,

self-presentation,

and

well-being;

effects

are

strongly

moderated

by

dependency,

alternatives,

and

context;

and

mediation

can

help,

harm,

or

produce

downstream

change.

The

field

therefore

survives

as

an

emerging

field,

while

its

integrated

predictive

superiority

over

established

anthropological

and

social-science

models

remains

unproven.

Introduction — Anthropology After Stability Anthropology has always studied humans in social and cultural worlds, and the

discipline

has

repeatedly

revised

its

concepts

when

those

worlds

changed.

Globalization,

migration,

media

circulation,

colonialism,

capitalism,

technology,

and

transnational

networks

have

already

pushed

anthropology

beyond

the

image

of

a

sealed

local

culture.

Appadurai

(1996)

analyzed

global

cultural

flows

and

electronic

mediation;

Marcus

(1995)

formalized

multi-sited

ethnography

for

objects

that

exceed

a

single

locality;

Augé

(1995)

theorized

non-places;

and

Larkin

(2013)

showed

that

infrastructures

are

not

merely

technical

supports

but

material

and

aesthetic

forms

that

organize

circulation

and

experience.

SR Anthropology begins from this transformed disciplinary terrain rather than

from

a

claim

that

anthropology

stopped

at

bounded

culture.

Its

intervention

is

to

integrate

five

pressures

that

are

often

studied

separately:

erosion,

drift,

hollowness,

infrastructural

dependency,

and

algorithmic

mediation.

The

claim

is

not

that

culture

disappears.

It

is

that

culture

increasingly

operates

through

and

alongside

technical

systems

whose

classifications,

affordances,

outages,

metrics,

and

access

rules

can

shape

everyday

life.

The collapse-era environment is therefore treated as a layered environment: •

cultural and symbolic •

institutional and administrative •

material and infrastructural

•

digital and platform-mediated •

algorithmic and classificatory SR Anthropology treats erosion as a potential structural process rather than a

rhetorical

diagnosis;

drift

as

directional

instability

rather

than

mere

change;

fragmentation

as

an

empirical

pattern

rather

than

a

universal

condition;

and

collapse

as

a

regime

that

must

be

demonstrated

with

indicators.

These

distinctions

make

the

field

stronger

because

they

create

conditions

under

which

SR

claims

can

be

wrong.

Literature Review — Theoretical Foundations for Studying

the

Human

Under

Structural

Erosion

The literature does not already contain SR Anthropology. It contains adjacent

findings

and

concepts

from

which

SR

develops

its

own

synthesis.

That

distinction

is

essential:

established

authors

are

used

here

as

theoretical

anchors,

not

as

retroactive

validators

of

SR

terminology.

1. Cultural Instability, Liquidity, and Non-Places Bauman’s Liquid Modernity (2000) describes social forms that no longer retain

their

shape

long

enough

to

provide

the

durability

associated

with

earlier

modern

institutions.

Augé’s

Non-Places

(1995)

analyzes

spaces

of

transit,

circulation,

and

consumption

that

do

not

reproduce

the

relational,

historical,

and

identity-bearing

qualities

of

anthropological

place.

Neither

author

establishes

SR’s

'Hollow

Human,'

but

both

provide

conceptual

resources

for

examining

thin,

transient,

or

unstable

forms

of

belonging.

2. Beyond Bounded Culture Appadurai (1996) analyzes globalization through disjunctive flows of people, media,

technologies,

finance,

and

ideas,

undermining

any

simple

equation

of

culture

with

bounded

territory.

Marcus

(1995)

does

not

claim

that

the

local

coherent

cultural

world

has

disappeared;

rather,

he

argues

for

ethnography

that

follows

complex

objects

across

multiple

sites

and

across

the

local/global

and

lifeworld/system

divide.

SR

Anthropology

adopts

this

methodological

lesson

while

avoiding

the

stronger

and

inaccurate

claim

that

classical

anthropology

presupposes

universally

bounded

cultures.

3. Infrastructural Subjectivity Larkin (2013) defines infrastructures as material forms that enable exchange over

space

and

shows

how

they

organize

politics,

aesthetics,

sensation,

and

promise.

This

supports

treating

infrastructure

as

an

anthropologically

meaningful

environment.

SR

extends

that

insight

into

the

proposed

construct

of

the

Infrastructural

Human:

a

subject

whose

options,

rhythms,

and

dependencies

are

substantially

conditioned

by

infrastructures.

4. Algorithms, Datafication, and Classification Beer (2017) calls attention to the social power of algorithms and the need to study

how

algorithmic

systems

participate

in

ordering

social

life.

Couldry

and

Mejias

(2019)

theorize

data

colonialism

as

the

appropriation

of

human

life

through

pervasive

datafication.

These

works

support

investigation

of

algorithmically

mediated

subject

formation,

but

SR

avoids

claiming

that

algorithms

literally

construct

the

whole

person.

The

stronger,

defensible

formulation is that algorithms can condition visibility, classification, exposure,

opportunity,

and

the

informational

environments

through

which

selfhood

is

performed.

5. Governance, Institutions, and Mediatization Hajer (2009) analyzes authoritative governance under mediatization and changing

institutional

conditions.

Herzfeld’s

Cultural

Intimacy

(2016)

examines

tensions

between

official

representations

and

socially

recognized

features

of

collective

identity.

These

works

do

not

directly

establish

a

theory

of

'functionally

hollow

institutions.'

SR

therefore

treats

institutional

hollowness

as

its

own

construct

and

requires

to

be

measured

through

discrepancies

between

formal

mandate,

procedural

activity,

service

capacity,

and

outcomes.

6. Meaning, Bureaucracy, and Moral Fragmentation Graeber (2015) critiques bureaucratic structures and the violence, stupidity, and

technological

fantasies

that

can

accompany

them.

MacIntyre

(1981/2007)

diagnoses

fragmentation

in

modern

moral

discourse

and

the

loss

of

shared

teleological

frameworks.

Frankfurt’s

On

Bullshit

(2005)

analyzes

indifference

to

truth

of

truth.

draws

from
