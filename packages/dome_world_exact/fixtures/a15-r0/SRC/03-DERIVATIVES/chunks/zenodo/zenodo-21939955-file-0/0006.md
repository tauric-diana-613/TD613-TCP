17.3 Empirical Finding II — Administrative Architecture Changes Realized Access Administrative-burden research demonstrates that institutional design changes who successfully reaches

public

programs.

Herd,

DeLeire,

Harvey,

and

Moynihan

used

a

mixed-method

study

of

Medicaid

in

Wisconsin

and

showed

that

process

changes

that

shifted

burden

away

from

citizens

increased

program

take-up

while

maintaining

program

integrity.

More

recent

field

and

experimental

evidence

likewise

shows

that

documentation

requirements,

expected

effort,

learning

costs,

and

simplified

enrollment

architecture

influence

willingness

to

apply

and

realized

participation.

This finding matters to SR-IGF because the population visible to an institution can be partly produced

by

the

institution’s

own

access

architecture.

If

high-friction

procedures

disproportionately

screen

out

eligible

people,

administrative

records

may

describe

the

population

that

successfully

traversed

the

process

rather

than

the

full

population

structurally

entitled

to

or

in

need

of

the

service.

The resulting correspondence pathway is:

Eligible Population → Administrative Friction → Observed Applicant Population → Institutional Dataset

→

Policy

Representation

Thus:

Observed Caseload ≠ Eligible or Affected Population

unless access and non-take-up processes are explicitly modeled.

This provides an empirical mechanism for representational selection rather than merely an abstract

claim

about

incomplete

information.

It

also

connects

citizen-state

burden

to

upstream

institutional

perception:

burden

can

alter

data

from

which

the

institution

subsequently

learns.

17.4 Empirical Finding III — Longitudinal Federal Service Data Reveal Capacity Gaps and Measurement-Frame

Change

Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat reporting provides a direct longitudinal example. For high-volume

Government

of

Canada

services

meeting

service

standards,

reported

performance

was

46%

in

2021–22,

40%

in

2022–23,

55%

in

2023–24,

and

52%

in

2024–25.

For

Government

of

Canada

business

applications

assessed

as

healthy,

reported

values

were

37%

in

2021–22,

38%

in

2022–23,

35%

in

2023–24,

and

38%

in

2024–25.

These trajectories matter for two reasons. First, they demonstrate that formal administrative capacity

contains

persistent

measurable

gaps

rather

than

a

simple

monotonic

modernization

trajectory.

Second,

the

2026–27

Treasury

Board

plan

changed

the

service-performance

indicator

from

the

percentage

of

high-volume

services

meeting

standards

to

the

percentage

of

service

standards

met

for

all

Government

of

Canada

services.

The

newer

report

notes

that

earlier-year

values

shown

under

the

new

measure

differ

from

previously

reported

values

because

the

measurement

population

changed.

This is an unusually clean example of why SR-IGF separates structural motion from representational

motion.

When

an

indicator

definition

changes,

a

time

series

can

change

even

if

the

underlying

service

system

does

not

change

by

the

same

amount.

The

appropriate

diagnostic

question

is

therefore

not

simply

whether

the

reported

percentage

increased

or

decreased,

but

whether

the

measurement

frame,

denominator,

service

population,

and

underlying

condition

remain

comparable.

Let R_t denote the institutional indicator and M_t the measurement architecture. Then:

R_t = g(X_t, M_t)

and therefore:

Δ R_t = g( Δ X_t, Δ M_t).

When Δ M_t ≠ 0, observed change in R_t cannot be attributed entirely to structural change in X_t.

This

makes

measurement-frame

stability

an

explicit

requirement

for

estimating

Institutional

Drift.

17.5 Empirical Finding IV — Phoenix Demonstrates the Difference Between Formal Authority, Operational

Capacity,

and

Recalibration

The federal Phoenix pay system and the transition to Dayforce provide a strong empirical test of the

authority–agency

distinction.

The

Auditor

Canada

reported

in

2026

that,

backlog

The

Dayforce

The case demonstrates that legal authority and organizational mandate are insufficient measures

of

realized

governance

The

Government

of

Canada

possessed

authority

throughout

Yet

backlogs,

rule

In SR-IGF notation:

A^f_t > 0

while:

A^e_t < A^f_t

for the specific objective of reliably resolving pay transactions within the intended service window.

The case also makes recalibration empirically testable. Replacing Phoenix with Dayforce is not itself

The

General

errors

SR-IGF

therefore

post-transition

17.6 Empirical Finding V — Oversight Can Produce Visibility Before It Produces Correction The Phoenix case also supplies evidence for the distinction between oversight and correction. Independent

audits,

parliamentary

scrutiny,

departmental

reporting,

and

formal

modernization

initiatives

made

the

problem

highly

visible,

yet

a

substantial

transaction

backlog

persisted

years

after

the

original

deployment.

In

2026

the

Auditor

General

stated

that

a

central

lesson

from

Phoenix

was

the

need

to

simplify

and

standardize

pay

rules

before

launching

a

replacement

system,

while

also

finding

that

progress

on

simplification

had

been

slow.

The empirical point is not that oversight failed absolutely. Oversight generated visibility, identified

risks,

documented

lessons,

and

influenced

the

design

of

the

replacement

project.

The

stronger

finding

is

that:

Observation → Recommendation

is not equivalent to:

Observation → Structural Correction.

This supports treating correction efficiency as a distinct variable rather than inferring it from audit activity,

committee

activity,

or

the

number

of

recommendations

issued.

17.7 Cross-Domain Empirical Convergence

The cases above come from different institutional environments: English health-care performance

management,

North

American

social-program

access,

Canadian

federal

digital-service

reporting,

and

federal

payroll

modernization.

Their

substantive

domains

differ,

but

they

share

a

common

transformation

problem.

In each case, a structural condition is filtered through an institutional architecture before becoming

a

decision-relevant

representation:

Reality → Access / Observation → Metric / Category → Decision → Action → Feedback.

The empirical convergence is therefore not that all institutions fail in the same way. It is that several

independently

established

literatures

document

measurable

losses

or

constraints

at

different

transitions

in

the

same

general

governance

chain.

This generates the field-level empirical proposition:

If SR-IGF is valid, then measures of correspondence loss at different transitions should explain or

predict

governance

error

better

than

domain-specific

outcome

indicators

alone

in

at

least

some

classes

of

institutional

problems.

That proposition is falsifiable. If representational fidelity, visibility lag, agency realization, dependency

exposure,

and

correction

efficiency

add

no

predictive

or

diagnostic

value

beyond

established

models,

the

integrated

field

claim

should

contract.
