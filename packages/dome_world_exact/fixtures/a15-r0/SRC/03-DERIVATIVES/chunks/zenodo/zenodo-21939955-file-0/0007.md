CHAPTER 17A — FORMAL PRESENCE, EFFECTIVE ABSENCE, AND COMPENSATORY

GOVERNANCE

17A.1 Formal Presence Is Not Effective Accessibility A government institution can remain legally present, funded, staffed, and formally responsible while

the

population

experiences

only

partial

practical

access

to

the

function

that

institution

is

supposed

to

deliver.

This

distinction

is

empirical

rather

than

rhetorical.

In

Canadian

primary

care,

82.6%

of

adults

reported

access

to

a

regular

health

provider

in

2024,

while

only

26.8%

reported

same-day

or

next-day

access

for

a

non-urgent

primary-care

need.

These

indicators

measure

different

constructs

and

therefore

should

not

be

mechanically

subtracted.

Their

coexistence

nevertheless

demonstrates

a

central

SR-IGF

proposition:

formal

attachment

and

timely

usable

access

are

not

equivalent.

The relevant institutional sequence is:

Formal Presence → Nominal Access → Effective Access → Realized Function.

SR-IGF therefore treats effective absence as a relational condition rather than literal institutional disappearance.

The

institution

remains

present

in

law,

organization,

and

representation,

while

part

of

the

function

becomes

inaccessible,

delayed,

externally

compensated,

or

transferred

to

another

layer

of

the

system.

17A.2 Compensated Public Continuity Hospital staffing provides a direct example of visible institutional continuity supported by extraordinary

compensatory

capacity.

Canadian

hospitals

used

more

than

7.8

million

purchased

staffing

hours

in

2023–2024,

equivalent

to

approximately

4,010

full-time

positions

and

126%

more

than

in

2019–2020.

In

the

same

year,

hospitals

recorded

almost

32

million

overtime

hours,

equivalent

to

approximately

16,397

full-time

positions

and

100%

more

than

in

2019–2020.

Purchased

staffing

represented

roughly

1%

of

hospital

worked

hours

and

overtime

approximately

5%,

with

heavier

reliance

in

rural

and

remote

settings.

The empirical significance is not that overtime or purchased staffing automatically constitute institutional

failure.

Surge

labour,

contracting,

and

overtime

can

be

legitimate

operational

tools.

The

stronger

finding

is

that

observable

public

function

can

remain

continuous

while

the

internal

capacity

mix

changes

substantially

and

extraordinary

labour

inputs

increase.

Thus:

Q_t > D_t,

C_{E,t} > 0,

F_t \approx \text{continuous},

where Q_t is effective service requirement, D_t durable endogenous capacity, C_{E,t} extraordinary

compensatory

capacity,

and

F_t

observable

function.

This is the governance condition SR-IGF needs to distinguish from simple institutional absence. The

institution

is

visible.

The

function

remains

observable.

But

part

of

the

capacity

required

to

sustain

that

function

is

being

supplied

through

compensatory

channels.

17A.3 Unpaid Care as an External Capacity Substrate Statistics Canada reported that in 2022, 42% of Canadians aged 15 and older—13.4 million people—provided

unpaid

care

to

children

or

care-dependent

adults.

Approximately

1.8

were

sandwich

providing

care

This

establishes

that

a

very

large

volume

of

socially

necessary

capacity

exists

outside

formal

paid

institutions.

Unpaid care is not itself evidence of governance failure. Family care can be chosen, culturally valued,

reciprocal,

and

beneficial.

It

becomes

relevant

to

SR-IGF

when

unpaid

capacity

is

required

to

compensate

for

delayed,

withdrawn,

inaccessible,

or

insufficient

formal

provision.

The

field

therefore

distinguishes:

Ordinary Social Reproduction ≠ Compensatory Governance Load.

The empirical task is to identify the point at which voluntary or ordinary care becomes required substitution

for

a

measurable

institutional

capacity

gap.

A second indicator demonstrates why visible compliance is an incomplete measure of stability. Bank

Persistent

The relevant SR-IGF insight is not that credit-card use proves governance failure. It is that private

A

This supports the distinction:

Observed Compliance ≠ Residual Capacity.

And more broadly:

Visible Continuity ≠ Durable Capacity.

17A.5 Formal Stability Can Be Privately Produced Taken together, the healthcare staffing, access, unpaid-care, and household-finance findings identify

a

common

empirical

architecture.

Public

institutions

can

remain

operational

while

some

portion

of

the

effective

burden

required

to

preserve

function

is

carried

by

overtime,

purchased

labour,

unpaid

caregivers,

household

coordination,

private

spending,

credit,

savings,

or

other

external

buffers.

The resulting sequence is:

Institutional Requirement → Durable Public Capacity → Capacity Gap → External Compensation → Preserved Visible Function → Hidden Buffer Erosion → Later Distress if Compensation Fails.

This is not a universal law. It is a testable governance pathway.

The decisive empirical distinction is between ordinary complementarity and compensatory substitution.

Private

participation,

family

care,

contractors,

and

credit

are

not

pathological

merely

because

they

exist.

They

become

evidence

of

a

governance-capacity

problem

only

where

an

independently

measurable

public

requirement-capacity

gap

exists

and

the

external

input

is

materially

necessary

to

preserve

the

function.

17A.6 Governance Absence as an Agency-Correspondence Problem These findings sharpen the SR-IGF concept of effective absence. Government absence should not

be

operationalized

as

whether

an

institution

physically

or

legally

exists.

The

stronger

variable

is

the

distance

between

formal

responsibility

and

realized

function.

Let A_i^f denote formal institutional responsibility or authority over function i, and A_i^e denote realized

effective

agency.

Then:

ARR_i = A_i^e / A_i^f.

A low Agency Realization Ratio can coexist with high formal presence. Likewise, let E_i denote effective

accessibility

to

the

public

function.

A

system

can

have

high

nominal

institutional

presence

P_i

while

E_i

remains

low:

P_i \uparrow \not\Rightarrow E_i \uparrow.

The resulting condition is not institutional disappearance but functional absence inside formal continuity.

SR-IGF therefore distinguishes three states:

1. Formal absence: no institution or mandate exists. 2. Formal presence with effective capacity: responsibility and realized function remain closely coupled.

3. Formal presence with effective absence: the institution remains present, but access, capacity, or

realization

is

insufficient

and

unmet

function

is

delayed,

transferred,

or

compensated

elsewhere.
