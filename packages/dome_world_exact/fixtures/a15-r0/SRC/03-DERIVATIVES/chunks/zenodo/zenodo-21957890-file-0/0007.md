29C.1 Why Boundary Operators Belong Inside SR-RIF The Knowledge-Selection Substrate does not begin only when a grant is submitted. Before funding,

publication,

indexing,

or

citation

can

select

knowledge,

research

must

first

define

what

is

inside

the

analytical

object.

Two

SignalRupture

operators

are

therefore

incorporated

into

SR-RIF

as

measurable

boundary

operators:

Exposure-Silo

Bias

(ESB)

and

Systemic

Substrate

Omission

(SSO).

ESB asks whether structurally co-occurring exposures are separated analytically and then interpreted

beyond

the

boundary

justified

by

the

model.

SSO

asks

whether

a

causally

reproductive

substrate

remains

outside

the

operative

research,

measurement,

policy,

or

governance

boundary.

Their

functions

are

distinct:

ESB → Is the subject/exposure boundary adequate? SSO → Is the causal/system boundary adequate?

Together they provide SR-RIF with empirical tests of knowledge loss before conventional publication-selection

mechanisms

begin.

29C.2 Exposure-Silo Bias: Empirical Status ESB has an operational form rather than remaining a rhetorical claim. For an evaluated model M and

system

S,

the

omitted

exposure

surface

is:

𝒪 _ESB = ℰ _S \ ℰ _M

A diagnosis requires more than omission. ESB is present only where analytical separation, consequential

omission,

and

interpretive

boundary

expansion

occur

together:

ESB = 1 when A ∧ O ∧ I.

Where comparable nested models exist, Exposure-Silo Distortion is measurable as:

ESD_j = |β_ j,S − β_ j,C| / max(| β_ j,S|, ε)

where β_ j,S is the focal coefficient in the silo specification and β_ j,C is the corresponding coefficient

after

theoretically

justified

co-exposures

are

restored.

An empirical PISA 2022 application provides a bounded test. OECD reporting places multiple performance-relevant

exposures

inside

the

same

student

population:

digital

distraction,

bullying,

food

insecurity,

socioeconomic

disadvantage,

classroom

disorder,

anxiety,

and

unequal

support.

Published

marginal

prevalences

of

30%

for

digital

distraction,

20%

for

bullying,

and

8%

for

food

insecurity

imply,

without

assuming

independence,

the

Fréchet

bound:

0.30 ≤ P(D ∪ B ∪ F) ≤ 0.58.

The result establishes that the same population contains multiple prevalent exposures while the precise

overlap

remains

unidentified

from

the

published

marginals.

It

does

not

establish

their

joint

causal

effect.

The

empirical

contribution

is

narrower

and

stronger:

a

single-exposure

estimate

can

be

valid

while

remaining

insufficient

as

a

whole-subject

explanation.

ESB

is

therefore empirically anchored and directly testable through nested specifications, overlap measurement,

interactions,

mediation,

out-of-sample

prediction,

and

intervention

heterogeneity.

29C.3 Systemic Substrate Omission: Empirical Status SSO is tested by boundary expansion. Let C(Y) be the empirically supported causal/reproductive chain

for

outcome

Y

and

B

the

operative

boundary.

The

omitted

substrate

is:

O = C(Y) \ B.

For comparative work, the Substrate Omission Ratio is:

SOR = [Σ w_i I(c_i ∉ B)] / Σ w_i.

Omission becomes consequential only when restoring the omitted substrate materially changes the

structural

conclusion.

Boundary

Sensitivity

is

therefore:

BST = |Y_B+ − Y_B|.

With categorical boundary sensitivity, the empirical operator becomes:

SSO* = SOR × BST.

Cross-domain tests already demonstrate the measurable signature. A narrow Canadian aggregate-emissions

boundary

records

national

improvement

while

sector

expansion

reveals

a

different

oil-and-gas

trajectory

and

expanded

oil-export

capacity.

A

recycling-rate

boundary

records

improvement

while

OECD

baseline

projections

show

absolute

plastics

use

and

waste

rising

sharply.

A

carbon-only

electrification

boundary

omits

concentrated

critical-mineral

and

refining

dependencies.

Per-task

or

facility-level

AI

efficiency

can

coexist

with

rapid

growth

in

total

electricity

demand

and

indirect

water

use.

A

Canadian

electricity

negative

control

is

critical:

when

coal-generating

capacity

is

actually

retired

or

transformed,

boundary

expansion

reinforces

rather

than

destabilizes

the

original

conclusion.

The empirical signature is therefore:

Local Validity + Material Boundary Sensitivity → SSO.

This negative-control structure prevents SSO from becoming a universal accusation of incompleteness.

If

adding

the

alleged

substrate

does

not

materially

change

interpretation,

the

operator

returns

a

negative

result.

29C.4 Placement in the Knowledge-Selection Grammar The resulting research grammar is now:

External Reality → Possible Inquiry → Question Formation → Exposure Boundary [ESB] → Causal/System Boundary [SSO] → Institutional Legibility → Funding → Research Production → Publication → Retrieval

→ Citation/Amplification → Institutional Knowledge → Future Inquiry.

SR-RIF therefore measures not only which completed studies become visible, but also which portions

of

reality

become

representable

as

research

objects

before

selection

occurs.

CHAPTER 29D — EMPIRICAL TEST 6: ENDOGENOUS INQUIRY SELECTION

29D.1 The Previously Missing Arrow The strongest unresolved SR-RIF proposition is that research-selection environments can become

inputs

into

future

question

formation.

This

claim

is

stronger

than

publication

bias.

It

requires

evidence

that

funding

opportunities,

review

outcomes,

institutional

position,

or

prior

knowledge

states

alter

what

researchers

subsequently

propose

or

study.

The testable equation is:

Q_{d,t+1} = β_0 + β_ R R_{d,t} + β_ K K_{d,t} + β_ F F_{d,t} + β_ I I_{d,t} + β_ O O_{d,t} + ε_{ d,t},

where R represents external real-world conditions, K the existing knowledge state, F prior funding

conditions,

I

institutional

position

or

priorities,

and

O

scientific

opportunity/tractability

controls.

The endogenous-inquiry hypothesis receives support when lagged research-system variables retain

predictive

power

after

external

reality

and

justified

opportunity

controls

are

included.

29D.2 Targeted Funding Can Alter Research Direction Myers (2020) uses NIH data to estimate how scientists respond to targeted funding opportunities. The

study

directly

identifies

willingness

to

change

research

direction

in

exchange

for

resources

and

finds

that

scientists

can

be

induced

to

move

toward

marginally

different

topics,

although

switching

costs

are

substantial.

This

is

direct

evidence

for

a

key

SR-RIF

arrow:

Funding Opportunity_t → Research Direction_{t+1}.

The result does not imply that funding determines inquiry. It establishes non-zero elasticity of research

direction

with

respect

to

targeted

resources.

A 2026 study of government funding and academic energy research extends the same question to Department

of

Energy

and

National

Science

Foundation

grant

environments,

explicitly

testing

whether

targeted

government

funding

opportunities

change

scientists'

publication

trajectories

and

research

focus.

This

provides

a

contemporary

independent

design

for

estimating

the

funding-to-direction

transition.

29D.3 Topic Choice Predicts Funding Survival Hoppe et al. (2019) analyzed 157,549 NIH R01 applications from fiscal years 2011–2015 and showed

that

application

topic

was

associated

with

funding

outcomes.

Topic

choice

contributed

to

observed

racial

funding

disparities

even

after

accounting

for

other

application

characteristics.

For

SR-RIF,

the

critical

mechanism

is

not

the

demographic

interpretation

alone.

It

is

that:

P(Funded | Topic = a) ≠ P(Funded | Topic = b).

If topic categories face different survival probabilities, repeated exposure to the funding surface can

alter

the

expected

return

to

future

topic

choice.

This

supplies

an

empirical

bridge

between

selection

conditionality

and

endogenous

inquiry

selection.

29D.4 Review Outcomes Can Affect Future Research Behaviour Research-selection feedback can influence what happens after an unsuccessful application. Experimental

work

among

NIH

K-awardees

found

that

the

interpretation

of

negative

grant

feedback

affected

motivation

to

reapply

and

intent

to

change

research

focus.

The

effects

varied

across

participant

groups,

so

the

result

cannot

be

universalized.

It

nevertheless

demonstrates

that

review

outcomes

can

alter

future

research

behaviour

rather

than

merely

classify

a

static

proposal.

The relevant transition is:

Review Outcome_t → Reapplication / Research-Focus Decision_{t+1}.

This mechanism is consistent with longitudinal SSHRC evidence in which non-reapplication after

an

unsuccessful

proposal

varies

by

institutional

position.

Non-reapplication

is

not

proof

that

a

question

disappears,

but

it

is

observable

attrition

from

the

formal

inquiry

pipeline.

29D.5 Institutional Position and Recursive Selection The SSHRC application denominator supplies another critical empirical element. Larregue and Pavie

analyze

56,680

successful

and

unsuccessful

applications

and

find

that

institutional

affiliation and prior funding predict subsequent funding success after adjustment. Prior funding is

therefore

not

merely

an

outcome;

it

becomes

a

state

variable

associated

with

future

survival.

A recursive empirical model is:

P(F_{i,t+1}=1) = logit^{-1}( α + β_1 F_{i,t} + β_2 Prestige_i + β_3 Topic_{i,t+1} + β_4 Score_{i,t+1}

+

X_i γ).

The scientific question is whether prior selection changes later selection after proposal-quality and

field

controls.

If

β_1

remains

positive

across

designs

and

systems,

cumulative

advantage

becomes

part

of

the

knowledge-selection

substrate

rather

than

a

descriptive

afterthought.

29D.6 What Is Now Empirically Supported The empirical status of the recursive architecture can now be separated into levels:

SUPPORTED MECHANISM 1 — Funding opportunities can alter research direction. SUPPORTED MECHANISM 2 — Topic choice can alter funding survival.

SUPPORTED MECHANISM 3 — Review feedback can alter reapplication and intended research

focus.

SUPPORTED MECHANISM 4 — Prior funding and institutional position can predict later funding

survival.

SUPPORTED MECHANISM 5 — Publication and citation stages contain additional non-random

survival

and

amplification.

UNRESOLVED SYSTEM CLAIM — The complete closed loop from prior funding and knowledge

to

future

inquiry,

future

knowledge,

and

subsequent

funding

has

not

yet

been

identified

in

one

linked

longitudinal

dataset.

The full loop therefore remains:

F_t → Q_{t+1} → K_{t+1} → F_{t+2}.

But the first arrows are no longer unsupported speculation. They have empirical mechanisms beneath

them.

CHAPTER 29E — SR-RIF FULL EMPIRICAL VALIDATION STANDARD

SR-RIF is fully empirically specified when every field-level proposition maps to an observable estimand,

a

dataset

class,

and

a

falsifier.

It

is

not

necessary

that

every

proposition

already

be

confirmed.

What

matters

is

that

no

central

claim

survives

only

because

it

cannot

be

tested.

The validation architecture is:

1. Inquiry formation — estimate how reality signals, prior knowledge, funding priorities, disciplinary

fit,

and

institutional

position

predict

question/proposal

composition.

2. Boundary construction — test ESB through nested exposure models and SSO through preregistered

boundary

expansion

and

negative

controls.

3. Funding selection — estimate stage-specific survival probabilities using submitted and rejected

proposals,

not

funded

projects

alone.
