CHAPTER 29F — EMPIRICAL TEST 7: CUMULATIVE SELECTION AND KNOWLEDGE-STATE

ATTRITION

29F.1 Why Stage Effects Must Be Linked Before They Are Multiplied

The Knowledge-Selection Substrate contains multiple survival transitions. If the same bounded cohort

can

be

followed

from

one

stage

to

the

next,

cumulative

survival

is

directly

estimable:

R_{0→n} = ∏_{j=0}^{n-1} r_j,

where r_j = N_{j+1}/N_j for the same cohort and the same unit of analysis.

This identity is descriptive, not causal. It becomes scientifically informative when transition probabilities

differ

systematically

by

a

characteristic

X

and

when

X

is

measured

consistently

across

the

linked

stages.

For group g:

P(K_vis | g) = ∏_j P(S_j=1 | S_{j-1}=1, g).

The cumulative selection contrast between groups a and b is then:

CSR_{a,b} = P(K_vis | a) / P(K_vis | b).

A CSR different from one means that stage-specific differences compound across the observed chain.

It

does

not

establish

why

they

differ.

Causal

interpretation

requires

stage-specific

controls

or

designs.

SR-RIF explicitly prohibits constructing CSR from unrelated cohorts. NIH annual application success,

a

historical

clinical-trial

publication

cohort,

and

a

citation

meta-analysis

cannot

be

multiplied

into

one

empirical

survival

rate

because

they

do

not

follow

the

same

projects.

The

correct

target

is

a

linked

proposal-level

or

study-level

cohort.

29F.2 Cumulative Distortion as a Testable Prediction

A weak effect at one stage can become consequential when the same directional asymmetry appears

repeatedly.

Conversely,

effects

can

cancel

if

later

stages

favor

previously

disadvantaged

material.

Therefore

SR-RIF

does

not

assume

monotonic

accumulation.

Let δ_ j be the log selection contrast at stage j:

δ_ j = log SR_j(x).

Then cumulative log selection in a linked cohort is:

Δ_ cum = Σ_ j δ_ j.

Three empirical regimes are possible:

1. Reinforcement: stage effects have the same sign and |Δ_ cum| grows. 2. Compensation: later stages partially offset earlier selection. 3. Cancellation: net cumulative contrast approaches zero despite stage-specific selection.

This gives the field a falsifiable cumulative claim. If linked cohorts repeatedly show compensation

or

cancellation

rather

than

reinforcement,

generalized

claims

of

compounding

distortion

must

weaken.

29F.3 Knowledge-Selection Loss

For a bounded cohort, SR-RIF defines descriptive Knowledge-Selection Loss between stages a and

b

as:

KSL_{a→b} = 1 − R_{a→b}.

KSL measures attrition, not epistemic error. High attrition can be appropriate where quality control

is

strong

and

resources

are

scarce.

To

study

distortion,

attrition

must

be

decomposed

by

the

characteristics

that

predict

survival

and

by

whether

those

characteristics

are

normatively

and

scientifically

justified

within

the

declared

selection

objective.

The empirical question is therefore not “How much disappears?” alone. It is:

What disappears, at which stage, under which rule, relative to which declared criterion, and with what

downstream

consequence?

CHAPTER 29G — EMPIRICAL TEST 8: RETRIEVAL, INDEXING, LANGUAGE, AND VISIBILITY

29G.1 Retrieval Is an Empirical Selection Surface

Scientific visibility does not end at publication. Evidence must be indexed, searchable, linguistically

accessible,

and

retrieved

to

enter

reviews,

guidelines,

and

subsequent

research.

Database

coverage

provides

direct

denominators

for

this

stage.

Heintz et al. (2023) examined 4,709 included references across 274 systematic reviews from the Norwegian

Institute

of

Public

Health.

MEDLINE

indexed

86.6%

of

included

references

and

Embase

88.2%;

combining

both

databases

increased

coverage

to

90.2%.

Consequently,

9.8%

of

references

used

by

the

completed

reviews

were

indexed

in

neither

database.

Coverage

also

varied

strongly

by

subject,

from

97.4%

in

physical-health

treatment

reviews

to

58.9%

in

welfare

reviews.

This establishes:

P(Indexed | relevant study, database, field) < 1

and, more importantly,

P(Indexed | relevant study, field=a) ≠ P(Indexed | relevant study, field=b).

Retrieval visibility is therefore not merely conceptual. It can be measured against a known set of studies

already

judged

relevant

to

a

review.

29G.2 Language as a Resource-Dependent Visibility Boundary

Rasmussen and Montgomery (2018) examined 123 Campbell systematic reviews. Of 108 reviews

that

did

not

explicitly

exclude

non-English

studies,

only

17

included

non-English

studies.

Inclusion

was

positively

associated

with

the

number

of

countries

represented

on

the

review

team,

while

authors

identified

time,

funding,

and

language

resources

as

major

barriers.

The relevant SR-RIF mechanism is not that English-language review is necessarily biased in effect

size.

A

separate

systematic

review

of

empirical

studies

found

no

evidence

of

a

systematic

treatment-effect

bias

from

English-only

restrictions

in

conventional

medicine,

although

precision

could

improve

when

non-English

trials

were

included.

This

is

an

important

negative

control:

incomplete

retrieval

does

not

automatically

produce

a

materially

different

scientific

conclusion.

The field therefore distinguishes:

Retrieval Omission ≠ Inferential Distortion.

Distortion requires a second condition:

Retrieval Omission + Material Result Sensitivity.

This mirrors the logic of SSO. An omitted study matters to a system-level conclusion only when its

inclusion

materially

changes

the

evidence

state

or

the

uncertainty

relevant

to

the

question.

29G.3 Database Boundary Sensitivity

For a defined review question q, let E_q be the set of studies ultimately judged eligible after a broad

search

and

E_{q,D}

the

subset

discoverable

through

database

set

D.

Retrieval

coverage

is:

RC_{q,D} = |E_{q,D}| / |E_q|.

Database Boundary Sensitivity can then be evaluated by comparing the synthesis under a constrained

source

set

with

the

synthesis

under

the

expanded

source

set:

DBS_q = d( θ̂_{ q,D}, θ̂_{ q,D+}).

A low RC with low DBS indicates visibility loss without major inferential change for that question.

A

low

RC

with

high

DBS

indicates

retrieval-dependent

inference.

This

converts

indexing

into

an

empirically

falsifiable

SR-RIF

transition

rather

than

a

generalized

complaint

about

databases.

CHAPTER 29H — EMPIRICAL TEST 9: CORRECTION, REGISTERED REPORTS, AND

NEGATIVE

CONTROLS

29H.1 A Field About Selection Must Also Measure Successful Correction

SR-RIF would be invalid if it coded every selection mechanism as distortion and every correction

as

ineffective.

A

mature

field

requires

environments

in

which

institutional

design

measurably

reduces

the

selection

effect

under

investigation.

Registered Reports provide such a quasi-control for result-dependent publication because in-principle

acceptance

occurs

before

outcomes

are

known.

Scheel,

Schijen,

and

Lakens

(2021)

compared

71

Registered

Reports

with

152

standard

psychology

reports.

The

first

tested

hypothesis

was

supported

in

43.66%

of

Registered

Reports

and

96.05%

of

standard

reports,

a

difference

of

52.39

percentage

points

(p

<

.001).

Restricting

the

comparison

to

original

rather

than

replication

studies

still

produced

a

large

difference.

The result does not prove that every difference is publication bias; design, sampling, and study-type

differences

may

contribute.

But

it

shows

that

changing

the

publication-selection

architecture

is

associated

with

a

radically

different

distribution

of

visible

results.

This is a direct institutional-design prediction of SR-RIF:

Change Selection Rule → Change Visible Knowledge Distribution.

Soderberg et al. (2021) provide an additional negative-control dimension. In blinded assessments by

353

researchers,

29

Registered

Reports

and

57

comparison

papers

were

evaluated

on

19

criteria.

Registered

Reports

were

statistically

indistinguishable

from

comparison

papers

in

novelty

and

creativity

while

showing

sizeable

advantages

in

methodological

and

analytical

rigor

and

higher

overall

quality.

Thus

a

mechanism

designed

to

weaken

result-contingent

selection

did

not

show

the

predicted

tradeoff

of

substantially

lower

novelty

in

this

sample.

29H.2 Scientific Correction Is Real but Incomplete

Correction after publication also produces measurable effects. Evidence is mixed, which is theoretically

valuable.

A study of 228 psychology papers that failed to replicate found that publication of the failed replication

predicted

an

average

14%

decline

in

citations

to

the

original

over

a

fourteen-year

postpublication

period.

This

supports

a

real

correction

channel.

Other studies find weaker adjustment. Analyses of 95–98 studies linked to large replication projects

found

little

evidence

that

citation

trajectories

strongly

distinguished

successful

from

failed replications; in one study failed replications were associated with only 5%–9% lower citations

on

average,

differences

that

were

not

statistically

distinguishable
