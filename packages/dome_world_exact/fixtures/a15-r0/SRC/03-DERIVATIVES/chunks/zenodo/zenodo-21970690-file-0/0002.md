1. The Institutional Claim: “Our Research Is Neutral” Neutrality functions as a legitimacy signal. It communicates trustworthiness, objectivity, methodological

rigor,

independence,

and

fairness.

Neutrality

is

that

to

•

“Our findings reflect reality.” •

“Our methods eliminate bias.” •

“Our conclusions are objective.” But neutrality is not a natural property of research. It is an ideal under constraint, continually

destabilized

by

the

systems

within

which

institutions

operate.

2. Structural Truth: Why Neutrality Is Structurally Unstable Neutrality is often treated as if it were a natural property of scientific work—something that

emerges

automatically

from

proper

methods,

careful

design,

and

institutional

safeguards.

But

neutrality

is

better

as

an

asymptotic

ideal:

something

institutions

aim

toward

but

cannot

fully

achieve

under

real ‑ world

conditions.

Structural truth reframes neutrality as structurally unstable, not because researchers are biased,

but

because

research

is

embedded

in

systems

shaped

by:

•

incentives •

infrastructures

•

access constraints •

methodological limits •

temporal drift These forces do not eliminate the value of research. They simply mean that neutrality is contingent,

not

inherent.

Why neutrality destabilizes in practice Neutrality becomes unstable when: •

incentives shape which questions get asked •

infrastructures shape which data can exist •

access constraints shape who is included or excluded •

methods must adapt to what is measurable, not what is ideal •

drift changes the system faster than institutions can update their models Under these conditions, neutrality is not “false”—it is fragile. A more precise claim Instead of claiming neutrality is impossible, the refined claim is: Neutrality is not a natural property of research; it is an ideal that becomes structurally unstable

when

systems

drift

faster

than

institutions

can

compensate.

This framing is stronger, more defensible, and more aligned with the traditions of Haraway,

Latour,

Jasanoff,

Bowker,

and

Star.

3. Operational Markers of Structural Truth To avoid unfalsifiability, structural truth must be operationalized. Four markers define when

a

structural

explanation

is

warranted:

3.1 Cross ‑ Domain Convergence A pattern appears across unrelated fields (e.g., healthcare, media, economics). 3.2 Incentive ‑ Traceability Outcomes can be mapped to incentive structures rather than stated intentions.

3.3 Infrastructure ‑ Dependence The behavior of the system is shaped by underlying infrastructures rather than surface narratives.

3.4 Drift Sensitivity The explanation accounts for temporal change—how systems evolve faster than institutional

methods.

These markers allow structural truth to be evaluated, challenged, and refined. 4. The Pre ‑ Structured World: Data Is Not Born Neutral Institutions often treat data as if it emerges from a vacuum. In reality: •

economic pressures shape who participates •

administrative burden shapes who is excluded •

platform algorithms shape what is visible •

supply chains shape what is measurable •

social infrastructures shape what is possible By the time data reaches a lab, it has already been filtered, sorted, constrained, biased, shaped,

and

drifted.

Neutrality

becomes

an

asymptotic

ideal—approachable

in

controlled

but

unstable

in

real

5. Methodological Neutrality as a Narrative Stabilizer Methods are often presented as neutral tools. But methods are shaped by: •

what can be funded •

what can be measured •

what can be published •

what fits within institutional timelines •

what aligns with existing narratives A method cannot be fully neutral if the constraints shaping it are not neutral. Neutrality is a

narrative

not

a

structural

6. The Drift Problem: Infrastructures Move Faster Than Methods Modern infrastructures drift in real time: •

algorithms update •

incentives shift •

populations change •

platforms reconfigure •

economic pressures intensify But institutional methods update slowly, require stability, assume fixed conditions, and depend

on

controlled

This

creates

structural

Infrastructures drift faster than institutions can measure. Neutrality destabilizes because the world does not hold still long enough for neutrality to exist.

7. Why Institutions Must Claim Neutrality (Even When It’s Unstable)

Neutrality is essential for public trust, legitimacy, continuity, authority, and coherence. If institutions

Thus:

Neutrality is a narrative requirement, not an empirical condition. Institutions must claim neutrality even when infrastructures make neutrality unstable. This
