Abstract Collapse Governance studies a specific divergence in public systems: institutions can remain

formally

authoritative

and

visibly

operational

while

part

of

the

capacity

required

to

sustain

public

function

is

supplied

by

workers,

households,

unpaid

caregivers,

contractors,

private

staffing,

private

finance,

or

other

compensatory

substrates.

The

field

integrates

institutional

cognition,

effective

capacity,

burden

transfer,

compensation,

visibility,

and

restoration

into

one

empirical

architecture.

A

Canadian

multi-system

test

finds

direct

evidence

of

extraordinary

staffing

compensation

in

hospitals,

large-scale

unpaid

care,

formal-versus-timely

access

divergence

in

primary

care,

widespread

household

financial

difficulty,

and

persistent

credit-card

balance

carrying

that

predicts

later

financial

stress.

These

results

support

burden-transfer

and

compensated-continuity

mechanisms

but

do

not

establish

that

every

private

burden

originates

in

government

failure,

that

compensation

necessarily

precedes

collapse,

or

that

Canada

is

a

collapse

state.

Collapse

Governance

is

therefore

classified

as

an

empirically

grounded

emerging

governance

field,

not

a

prospective

declaration

or

an

established

mature

discipline.

1. Field Definition and Boundary Collapse Governance is not a synonym for political failure, recession, poverty, administrative

inconvenience,

low

trust,

or

dissatisfaction.

Its

unit

is

the

coupled

institution-population

system.

Its

distinctive

object

is

formal

public

continuity

sustained

partly

through

capacity

supplied

outside

the

institution

formally

responsible

for

the

function.

Public

administration,

state-capacity

research,

administrative-burden

scholarship,

welfare-state

research,

social

reproduction,

resilience

theory,

and

household-finance

research

already

explain

many

components.

Collapse

Governance

survives

only

if

the

burden-transfer

architecture

connects

those

components

and

adds

discriminant

or

incremental

explanatory

value.

D

durable

X_t → R_t → Action_t; G_C(t)=Q_t−D_t; if G_C(t)>0 and C_E,t>0, F_t may remain

The architecture is probabilistic and branch-sensitive. Demand, demography, policy design,

Current

3. Effective Capacity and Burden Transfer Effective Institutional Capacity (EIC) is treated as a measurement family rather than a discovered

scalar

law.

At

minimum,

empirical

work

should

retain

separate

measures

of

eligible

requirement,

completed

public

service,

service

delay,

externally

supplied

function,

unresolved

harm,

and

durable

staffing

or

infrastructure.

Institutional

Burden

Transfer

(IBT)

likewise

remains

administrative

time,

unpaid

care,

repair,

search,

direct

cost,

and

service

should

exists.

4. Private Compensation and Buffer Dynamics Private Compensation Load (PCL) is a family of observable compensatory inputs, including

overtime,

additional

work,

credit

use,

savings

depletion,

family

transfers,

unpaid

care,

private

purchased

substitutes,

and

deferred

necessities.

These

inputs

are

not

automatically

pathological.

They

count

as

extraordinary

compensation

only

when

materially

required

to

bridge

an

independently

measured

requirement-capacity

gap.

Buffer

dynamics

can

be

represented

conceptually

as

B_{t+1}=B_t−C_E,t+R_C,t,

where

R_C

is

replenishment

or

recovery.

5. Intensity Regimes Compensated Governance exists when an institutional requirement-capacity gap is bridged

by

compensatory

inputs

without

immediate

loss

of

observable

function.

Erosive

Governance

is

a

hypothesis

for

cases

in

which

compensatory

demand

persistently

exceeds

replenishment

and

produces

measurable

burden

or

buffer

loss.

Exposed

Collapse

Governance

is

reserved

for

cases

in

which

required

compensation

can

no

longer

preserve

the relevant function. No universal scalar Collapse Governance Index is declared in this manuscript.

6. Visibility and Falsification Visibility Lag is the interval between a dated structural change and a dated institutional recognition

or

response.

Current

cross-sectional

evidence

cannot

establish

that

compensation

itself

delays

recognition.

The

stronger

model

is

weakened

if

independently

measured

capacity

gaps

do

not

covary

with

transferred

burden,

if

restoration

does

not

reduce

extraordinary

compensation,

or

if

the

burden-transfer

architecture

adds

no

explanatory

value

beyond

established

public-administration,

state-capacity,

resilience,

and

administrative-burden

models.
