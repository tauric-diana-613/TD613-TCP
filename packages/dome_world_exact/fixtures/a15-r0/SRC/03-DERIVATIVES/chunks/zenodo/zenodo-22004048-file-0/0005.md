Introduction

Debt can be both a bridge and a trap. The distinction depends not merely on whether

a

balance

exists

but

on

what

is

purchased,

whether

repayment

restores

capacity,

and

whether

the

household

can

stop

borrowing.

When

inadequate

residual

income

pushes

groceries,

utilities,

medication,

or

transport

onto

credit,

interest

and

minimum

payments

reduce

the

following

month’s

residual

income.

Borrowing

then

reproduces

the

condition

that

required

borrowing.

The governance importance is conceptual. If stable payment is treated as proof

of

stable

capacity,

the

household’s

private

financing

makes

systemic

insufficiency

disappear

from

the

institutional

view.

Debt Loop and New Measures The structural loop is:

insufficient residual income → necessity credit → interest or statement repayment

→

lower

next-period

residual

income

→

renewed

credit.

Structural Debt Dependence requires recurrence, essential consumption, and low

exit

capacity.

The

Necessity

Credit

Ratio

is

essential

credit

purchases

divided

by

total

credit

purchases.

Income

Pre-Commitment

is

the

next

statement

plus

fixed

obligations

divided

by

expected

next

income.

Debt

Exit

Capacity

is

liquid

resources

available

after

essential

obligations

divided

by

the

amount

required

to

stop

revolving

use.

Credit Float Dependence is the limiting case in which the statement is paid in full,

avoiding

interest,

but

the

payment

consumes

current

cash

and

necessitates

immediate

reuse

for

current

essentials.

Therefore:

Paid in full ≠ financially buffered.

The correct classification depends on the counterfactual: could the household cease

using

credit

for

one

complete

billing

cycle

without

missing

essentials

or

another

obligation?

Typology Convenience users pay in full and possess exit capacity. Transient borrowers carry

a

balance

after

a

bounded

shock

and

restore

capacity.

Structural

carriers

repeatedly

carry

essential

balances

and

cannot

exit

without

deprivation

or

outside

support.

Credit-float

dependents

pay

in

full

but

cannot

complete

a

cycle

without

reusing

credit.

Distressed

borrowers

exhibit

arrears,

over-limit

use,

collections,

or

insolvency.

These

states

should

be

modeled

as

transitions

rather

than

fixed

identities.

Canadian Evidence

Bank of Canada analysis using anonymized credit data found that about 46% of

cardholders,

on

average,

carried

a

balance

for

at

least

two

consecutive

months

in

any

given

month

since

2016;

the

share

varied

from

42%

to

49%.

In

2023,

the

median

carried

balance

was

$1,150.

Carrying

was

more

common

among

mortgagors

(53%)

than

non-mortgagors

(43%),

although

a

larger

share

of

carrying

non-mortgagors

used

at

least

80%

of

available

limits.

Persistence mattered. Among non-mortgagors, carrying a balance for seven to twelve

months

was

associated

with

roughly

seven

times

the

later

financial-stress

risk

of

paying

in

full.

This

supports

revolving

persistence

as

an

early-warning

signal.

It

does

not

establish

why

each

balance

was

incurred,

whether

purchases

were

essential,

or

whether

full

payers

possessed

exit

capacity.

Relationship to Broad Hardship The numerical similarity between balance carrying (roughly 46%), survey paycheck

dependence

(often

in

the

mid-40%

range),

and

official

household

financial

difficulty

(39%)

is

theoretically

important

but

not

proof

of

overlap.

Separate

datasets,

definitions,

sampling

frames,

and

dates

prevent

person-level

inference.

The

correct

next

test

links

transaction

or

bureau

histories

to

expenditure,

income,

savings,

and

hardship

responses

under

privacy-preserving

governance.

Replication and Incremental-Validity Design

Follow households monthly for at least twenty-four months. Collect statement

balance,

payment,

utilization,

essential-category

purchases,

income

deposits,

fixed

obligations,

liquid

savings,

family

transfers,

and

hardship

outcomes.

Pre-register

four

exposure

states:

convenience,

transient,

structural

carrying,

and

credit

float.

Require

at

least

three

recurring

cycles

for

structural

classification

and

conduct

sensitivity

tests

at

two,

six,

and

twelve

cycles.

Primary outcomes are arrears, food insecurity, missed medication, forced borrowing,

insolvency,

buffer

depletion,

and

time

to

exit

dependence.

Hidden

Markov

or

multistate

survival

models

can

estimate

transitions.

A

randomized

or

quasi-experimental

income

interruption

is

not

ethically

required;

natural

payroll

timing

changes,

benefit

changes,

interest-rate

resets,

and

tax

refunds

can

identify

whether

added

liquidity

produces

durable

exit

or

only

temporary

balance

reduction.

Governance Implications Delinquency is a late indicator. Minimum-payment compliance is not resilience.

Full

payment

is

not

necessarily

liquidity.

Banks

and

governments

should

report

persistence,

essential-use

share,

utilization,

exit

capacity,

and

buffer

restoration

separately.

Benefit

design

should

be

evaluated

for

whether

it

permanently

raises

residual

capacity,

not

merely

whether

it

lowers

a

balance

for

one

month.

This prevents debt from being naturalized as a personal preference. Some borrowing

reflects

consumption

choice,

but

repeated

necessity

borrowing

is

also

evidence

about

wages,

prices,

benefit

adequacy,

housing,

care,

and

health

costs.

The

debt

account

is

downstream

of

those

systems.

Limitations and Falsification

The available evidence cannot measure credit float, essential purchase composition,

informal

family

debt,

or

causality.

The

model

would

be

weakened

if

credit-float

households

show

high

liquid

buffers

and

voluntary

rewards

optimization,

if

recurring

balances

do

not

predict

hardship

after

income

and

assets

are

controlled,

or

if

balance

persistence

is

better

explained

by

planned

low-cost

financing

than

necessity.

Results

must

distinguish

correlation

from

mechanism

and

avoid

treating

all

credit

use

as

pathology.

Conclusion The central measurement correction is simple: payment status is not capacity. Structural

debt

dependence

occurs

when

credit

no

longer

bridges

income

through

time

but

becomes

the

machinery

through

which

inadequate

residual

income

is

continuously

reproduced.

A

governance

system

that

sees

only

default recognizes the household after private stabilization has failed. SR measurement

begins

while

the

loop

is

still

keeping

the

system

alive.
