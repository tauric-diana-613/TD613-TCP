FROM HISTORICAL SUBSTRATE TO ALGORITHMIC ALLOCATION

A Cross-Domain Empirical Test of SignalRupture System Inheritance

Redlining · Mortgage Underwriting · Predictive Policing · School Administrative Risk · AI Proxy Recovery

SignalRupture (SR)

2026

Abstract

This paper evaluates the SignalRupture concept of system inheritance across five empirical domains and one declared simulation. System inheritance is defined as the persistence and operational reuse of historically structured classifications, exposures, and institutional records through contemporary geographic, administrative, and algorithmic substrates. The studies analyze 8,626 census tracts across 217 cities in a national redlining linkage; 18,488 California mortgage applications across 989 historically graded tracts and 497 lenders; 133,127 Oakland police stops represented as 2,291 grid-quarter observations; 15,439 schools across 2,268 districts; and 7,231 census tracts in an AI proxy and recursive-feedback audit. Results show strong and repeated evidence of persistence and proxy legibility. A one-standard-deviation increase in historic redlining exposure was associated with higher contemporary racialized composition and adverse health and social-need estimates. Prior enforcement intensity predicted later enforcement intensity in Oakland (β=0.297, clustered SE=0.045), while prior contraband hits and hit rates did not. Mortgage variables reconstructed historically D-like location with AUC=0.704 and Black-or-Hispanic applicant status with AUC=0.831. Nominally neutral tract variables reconstructed non-White-majority geography with AUC=0.968 and D-like geography with AUC=0.712. School administrative actions displayed longitudinal persistence and predicted later chronic absence. A recursive simulation amplified an observed racialized record gap under detection feedback and reduced it under corrective allocation. The combined evidence supports the weaker SR claims that historical structure remains statistically and operationally legible and that institutional records carry forward across domains. It does not yet establish observational proof that deployed AI systems caused recursive amplification, nor does it establish discriminatory intent. The paper therefore validates system inheritance as a measurable cross-domain research program while placing the strongest amplification claim under explicit empirical reservation.

1. Introduction

Artificial intelligence does not enter an empty world. Neither do policies, risk scores, eligibility systems, or administrative reforms. They enter environments already organized by prior classifications, unequal infrastructures, spatial segregation, institutional routines, and accumulated records. Removing an explicit racial variable or historical label does not necessarily remove the structure generated around it. The central empirical question is whether that structure remains recoverable and consequential through variables considered operationally neutral.

SignalRupture calls this process system inheritance. The concept does not require a claim that a contemporary decision-maker intends to reproduce a historical injustice. It asks whether earlier structures persist in present substrates; whether contemporary classifications can reconstruct them; whether institutional actions become inputs into later decisions; and whether recursive deployment increases, preserves, or interrupts unequal exposure.

The present synthesis tests those propositions across housing, lending, policing, education, health-related need, and model-based allocation. It does not pool heterogeneous coefficients into a single effect size. Such a meta-analytic combination would be invalid because the units, outcomes, estimands, time scales, and data-generating processes differ. Instead, the paper uses a structured replication logic: every domain is evaluated against the same sequence—historical or institutional record → contemporary substrate → classification or allocation → later institutional record—and every result is graded against an interpretation ladder.

2. Theory and Hypotheses

2.1 System inheritance

System inheritance occurs when a historically or institutionally produced structure remains encoded in contemporary variables and shapes present classifications, allocations, or outcomes. Inheritance can be geographic, administrative, infrastructural, or statistical. It can operate without the original label being present and without any one actor possessing complete control over the process.

2.2 Amplification

Amplification is a stronger claim than inheritance. Inheritance requires persistence or recoverability. Amplification requires evidence that a later institutional or algorithmic process increases the magnitude, reach, speed, or durability of the inherited disparity relative to a defensible baseline. Observational amplification further requires longitudinal deployment evidence linking model-conditioned action to the data used in subsequent classification.

2.3 Hypotheses

Hypothesis

H1 — Persistence

Present geographic, social, or infrastructural variables carry part of the historical relationship.

H3 — Conditional disparity

Group differences remain after observed legitimate need and context are modeled.

H4 — Proxy inheritance

Variables excluding explicit group and historical labels reconstruct protected or historically classified geography.

H5 — Recursive amplification

Institution-conditioned records increase disparity over repeated decision cycles.

H6 — Interruptibility

Corrective allocation or altered feedback rules reduce recursive disparity.

3. Interpretation Ladder and Evidentiary Rules

The analysis separates six levels of inference. A result can support a lower level without supporting the levels above it.

Difference: unadjusted outcome rates vary across groups or places.

Persistent disparity: differences recur through time or remain after fixed effects and declared controls.

Conditional disparity: observed-minus-expected differences remain within the available measurement model.

Structural inheritance: historical or institutional structure is linked to present substrates or decisions through a measured pathway.

Recursive amplification: outputs alter later inputs and increase disparity across observed cycles.

Intentional racism or unlawful discrimination: requires legal and purpose-specific evidence not inferable from coefficients alone.

Three evidence classes are used. E1 denotes documented source observations or official model-based estimates. E2 denotes derived scores, model predictions, coefficients, ratios, and simulations. E3 denotes the SR interpretation connecting those results to system inheritance. The classification prevents theoretical language from being mistaken for directly observed fact.

4. Data and Methods

4.1 National historical-substrate study

The historical analysis links Mapping Inequality residential Home Owners’ Loan Corporation polygons and a 2020 census-tract crosswalk to contemporary racial composition and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention PLACES 2025 release. The analytic sample contains 8,626 tracts across 217 historical cities. A historic redlining score is calculated as the area-weighted mean of grade values A=1, B=2, C=3, and D=4. Models include historical-city fixed effects and cluster standard errors by city. Contemporary health and social-need outcomes are CDC small-area model estimates, not direct tract censuses; the analysis is therefore cross-sectional association rather than evaluation of local causal effects.

4.2 Mortgage-underwriting study

The lending audit analyzes 18,488 California 2024 HMDA applications located in 989 historically graded tracts and involving 497 lenders. The observed denial rate is 11.4%. Cross-fitted models estimate expected denial using available applicant, loan, tract, and lender variables. Proxy classifiers test whether nominal underwriting and lender variables reconstruct D-like historical geography or Black-or-Hispanic applicant status. HMDA omits or bands important underwriting information, including detailed credit score and debt-to-income or loan-to-value values; residual differences are not causal estimates of unlawful discrimination.

4.3 Predictive-policing and enforcement study

The Oakland audit contains 133,127 stops from 2013–2017, aggregated to 2,291 quarter-by-spatial-cell observations across 203 cells. Fixed-effect models test whether prior-quarter stops, contraband hits, and hit rates predict subsequent enforcement. A held-out model estimates the allocation that would result if later enforcement were predicted from the recorded pattern. The data do not identify which deployments used a predictive-policing algorithm and do not fully separate resident-reported incidents from police-discovered activity.

4.4 School administrative-risk study

The school analysis links the 2015–16 and 2017–18 Civil Rights Data Collection waves. After quality restrictions, 15,439 schools across 2,268 districts are matched. District-clustered models estimate later chronic absence and administrative actions from prior chronic absence, law-enforcement referrals, arrests, suspensions, racial composition, disability composition, enrollment, and state effects. CRDC supports school-level structural analysis; it cannot identify an individual student’s movement through a risk score, sanction, and subsequent relabeling.

4.5 AI inheritance and recursive simulation

The AI audit uses 7,231 complete-case tracts. A five-fold GroupKFold design holds out historical cities. Food-insecurity prevalence is predicted from eight tract variables while explicit race, racial composition, HOLC grade, historic-redlining score, city identity, and geographic identifiers are excluded. Separate classifiers test recovery of non-White-majority and D-like geography. The recursive component is a declared simulation: detection feedback adds λ to selected records; corrective allocation subtracts λ. It tests model behavior under specified rules, not the behavior of a documented agency.

5. Results

5.1 Historical structure remains visible

Within historical cities, a one-standard-deviation increase in the historic redlining score was associated with a 5.10 percentage-point higher Black population share, a 5.15-point higher Latino share, and a 9.64-point higher non-White share in 2020. The same exposure was associated with higher modeled lack of insurance, asthma, diabetes, fair or poor health, food insecurity, housing insecurity, lack of transportation, and utility shutoff. The largest listed association was food insecurity: 5.73 percentage points (clustered SE=0.43, p≈2.1×10⁻²⁸).
