EMPIRICAL POLITICAL CASE STUDY

The Policy Contradiction

How educational recovery policy can propagate the conditions it claims to correct

Systems Propagation Research

Case Study 03  |  Ontario secondary education  |  August 2026

Central finding

Ontario is increasing formal assessment pressure while equalizing instructional capacity is not increasing on the same trajectory. Bill 101 enables mandatory secondary examinations; real per-student provincial operating funding was at a ten-year low in 2024–25; and roughly one-third of tracked English-language mathematics cohorts never met the provincial standard across successive assessment points. When preparation capacity differs across schools and households, a universal examination can measure both learning and unequal access to the conditions that produce learning.

The contradiction is not that examinations are inherently invalid. It is that the state intensifies downstream measurement without first demonstrating that upstream learning capacity is sufficient and equitably distributed.

Abstract

This paper tests whether an educational accountability policy can reproduce the performance problem it is intended to solve. Ontario’s Putting Student Achievement First Act, 2026 received Royal Assent on 7 May 2026 and authorizes province-wide assessment policies, including mandatory written examinations for secondary students. The policy arrives amid persistent mathematics difficulty: in 2024–2025, 51% of Grade 6 and 58% of Grade 9 students in English-language schools met the provincial standard, while cohort data show that 33% never met the standard from Grade 3 to Grade 6 and 34% never met it from Grade 6 to Grade 9. Simultaneously, Ontario’s Financial Accountability Office reports that inflation-adjusted provincial operating funding reached $14,504 per student in 2024–2025, the lowest level in ten years, with a projected decline to $14,111 by 2027–2028. The paper develops and tests a Policy Contradiction Propagation Model. Its central mechanism is burden transfer: when enforcement rises faster than institutional learning capacity, preparation work moves toward schools, teachers, students, and households with unequal time, income, digital access, and educational capital. The available evidence confirms the preconditions for contradiction but cannot yet estimate the causal effect of the new examinations, because implementation is beginning. The paper therefore offers prospective predictions, a falsification design, and measurable indicators for the 2026–2029 period.

1. The political problem

Governments often respond to weak outcomes by increasing measurement. This is politically legible: an examination has a date, a score, a common format, and an accountable student. Capacity is less visible. It is distributed across teacher time, class size, special-education support, school resources, household schedules, tutoring markets, sleep, food security, disability accommodations, and the stability required for sustained attention. A policy can therefore become more standardized at the point of judgment while remaining deeply unequal at the point of preparation.

The research question is:

Does mandatory examination policy improve learning capacity, or does it primarily increase the consequences attached to an already unequal distribution of learning capacity?

The paper does not assume that exams lower achievement. It tests a conditional proposition: examinations become contradictory when enforcement intensity rises without a commensurate and equitable increase in the resources required to meet the standard.

2. The policy event

Bill 101, the Putting Student Achievement First Act, 2026, received Royal Assent on 7 May 2026. The amended Education Act gives the Minister authority to establish policies and guidelines governing assessment. Government statements identify mandatory written examinations on official exam days for Grades 9–12 as an intended use of that authority. Legislative debate described final examinations representing approximately 20% to 25% of the final mark. The Ontario Human Rights Commission cautioned that implementation could affect students protected under the Human Rights Code, including students with learning disabilities, and emphasized accommodation obligations.

This distinction matters: the legislation establishes authority and the government has announced the policy direction, while the exact operational rules are implemented through subsequent policy and regulation. The present study is therefore a baseline and prospective test, not a retrospective estimate of an effect that has already matured.

3. Baseline conditions

3.1 Mathematics performance is improving slowly but remains structurally weak

EQAO’s 2024–2025 results show improvement over the preceding two years, which prevents a simplistic “continuous collapse” claim. Yet the level and cohort patterns remain serious. In English-language schools, 64% of Grade 3 students, 51% of Grade 6 students, and 58% of Grade 9 students met the mathematics standard. The Grade 3-to-6 cohort is more revealing: 33% never met the standard at either point and 15% dropped below it. From Grade 6 to Grade 9, 34% never met the standard, while 16% rose to it.

3.2 Instructional funding and assessment pressure move on different trajectories

The Financial Accountability Office reports that real provincial operating funding to school boards was $14,504 per student in 2024–2025—the lowest value in the ten-year series. In constant 2024–2025 dollars, funding declined from $14,862 in 2015–2016 to $14,504 in 2024–2025, a reduction of $358 or 2.4%. The 2025 budget outlook projects $14,111 by 2027–2028, 5.1% below the 2015–2016 value and 2.7% below 2024–2025.

Funding is not identical to service capacity, and total board spending differs from provincial transfers. Nevertheless, the official trajectory fails to show a large new equalizing investment aligned with the new assessment burden. The FAO further estimates that the ministry’s 2027–2028 plan is $0.9 billion below the amount required to maintain 2024–2025 service levels under its cost-driver projection.

4. The Policy Contradiction Propagation Model

Let examination enforcement be E, effective institutional instructional capacity be C, household-dependent preparation burden be B, and inequality in available household capacity be H. The contradiction pressure at time t is:

PCPₜ = Eₜ × (1 − Cₜ) × Hₜ

All terms are normalized from 0 to 1. The model predicts little contradiction when institutional capacity is high or household capacity is equal. Contradiction rises when consequential assessment intensifies, schools cannot fully absorb preparation needs, and households differ in their ability to compensate.

Burden transfer is represented as:

Bₜ₊₁ = max[0, Eₜ − Cₜ] × (1 − Sₜ)

where S is school-provided compensatory support. Outcome inequality is then expected to change as:

ΔGₜ₊₁ = αBₜHₜ − βSₜ − γAₜ

Here G is the achievement gap and A represents effective accommodation. The model does not predict that every student performs worse. It predicts dispersion: students with abundant compensatory capacity may improve or remain stable, while students without it carry a larger share of the new consequence.

5. Propagation sequence

Weak mathematics outcomes become politically visible through provincial indicators.

The state increases examination standardization and the consequences attached to final performance.

Schools must allocate additional time to exam preparation, administration, accommodation, and remediation.

Where school capacity is insufficient, preparation and recovery work moves into homes or private tutoring.

Households absorb the work unevenly because schedules, income, language, education, disability, housing conditions, and access to quiet space differ.

The common examination converts unequal preparation conditions into apparently comparable scores.

Score gaps are interpreted as student, family, teacher, or school deficits, generating demand for further enforcement rather than upstream capacity repair.

The loop is politically self-reinforcing. Measurement makes the downstream outcome visible while the upstream distribution of preparation capacity remains partially unmeasured. The policy can then reproduce the evidence used to justify its expansion.

6. Empirical support for the burden-transfer mechanism

6.1 Socioeconomic capacity predicts mathematics risk

PISA 2022 reports that socioeconomically disadvantaged students across OECD countries were seven times more likely than advantaged students not to reach basic mathematics proficiency. This does not prove that Ontario examinations will widen gaps, but it establishes that the capacity relevant to mathematics achievement is not equally distributed before the new policy begins.

6.2 Work arrangements change available care time

Statistics Canada reports that parents who teleworked from home spent 35 minutes more per day on childcare than parents who did not telework, and 23 minutes more than on-site teleworkers. It also reports lower work–life balance satisfaction among shift-working parents than among those with regular daytime schedules. These measures do not directly record mathematics tutoring, but they demonstrate that household time capacity varies systematically with work arrangements. A policy that relies implicitly on home compensation therefore has distributional consequences even when no parent is formally ordered to provide it.

6.3 Disability makes formally equal examination rules substantively unequal

The Ontario Human Rights Commission specifically warned that mandatory examinations and assessment standards may affect students with learning disabilities and other protected characteristics. Accommodation can reduce this inequality, but accommodation itself consumes institutional capacity and must be designed, delivered, monitored, and appealed when inadequate. Formal universality does not remove the need for differentiated support.

7. The technology contradiction

Ontario’s technology policy contains a secondary contradiction. The province limits personal mobile-device use to reduce classroom distraction, while the education system retains mandatory online-learning requirements for secondary students and administers major EQAO assessments digitally. These policies are not logically impossible: a school can restrict recreational cellphone use while using computers intentionally. The contradiction arises only when technology is treated as an individual distraction at one moment and as assumed educational infrastructure at another, without measuring the unequal environments in which students develop attention, digital fluency, and off-screen recovery capacity.
