6.3 The closed loop, stated carefully

The evidence does not justify the universal statement that debt exists because wages are inadequate. Canadian debt includes mortgages, investment-linked borrowing, vehicle loans, consumption smoothing, and strategic credit use. It does justify a narrower proposition: for a substantial group, persistent revolving balances are associated with constrained flexibility and future arrears, and essential-cost compression provides a plausible economic condition under which such reliance increases.

7. Alternative Explanations

Post-pandemic normalization raised transportation activity and spending between 2021 and 2023.

Higher mortgage interest rates increased shelter payments for some owners.

Changes in household composition can alter average spending shares across survey waves.

Nominal expenditure growth reflects both prices and quantities.

Credit-card rewards and payment timing can generate temporary balances unrelated to distress.

Households may voluntarily prioritize shelter quality, vehicles, or food quality rather than face pure constraint.

Transfers, savings, informal support, and asset sales are not fully represented by the core-share decomposition.

These alternatives limit causal interpretation but do not erase the measured compositional change. The CES calculation answers how the average consumption dollar was distributed, not why every household made each purchase.

8. Limitations

The 2021 and 2023 spending surveys are repeated cross-sections, not a household panel.

The implied 2021 dollar total is reconstructed from the published 2023 total and 14.3% growth rate.

Shelter, food, and transportation are not universally fixed or equally essential for every household.

RCS includes other necessary expenditures and must not be labeled fully discretionary income.

Debt principal, interest, and shelter concepts differ across the cited systems and are not added into one unsupported composite.

The credit microdata findings come from Bank of Canada models and cannot be independently re-estimated from public account-level data.

The paper establishes descriptive compression and predictive credit risk, not a causal wage-to-debt pathway.

9. Falsification and Future Tests

The margin-compression claim would be weakened if harmonized microdata showed that essential-cost shares did not rise within comparable households; if the residual share recovered after price adjustment; or if persistent balance-carrying did not predict later stress after income, assets, and borrower risk were adequately modeled.

Link household disposable income, spending, debt service, and balance-sheet data at the household level.

Estimate EIM by income quintile, tenure, province, family type, age, and immigration status.

Decompose changes into price, quantity, interest-rate, and composition effects.

Test whether income shocks predict balance-carrying and whether balance-carrying mediates later consumption compression.

Distinguish renters from outright homeowners within the non-mortgagor category.

Compare persistent revolvers with transactors and short-duration balance carriers.

Estimate the threshold at which debt service converts temporary smoothing into a self-reinforcing margin constraint.

10. Policy Implications

A margin perspective changes the policy question from whether average income is growing to whether households can absorb essential commitments without repeatedly converting current shortfalls into future obligations. Relevant interventions may include housing supply and affordability measures, income supports targeted to high marginal propensities to consume, lower-cost credit alternatives, debt-repayment assistance, consumer-credit regulation, and improved measurement of renters and non-mortgagors.

The paper does not prescribe eliminating credit. Credit is a useful intertemporal instrument. The policy concern is a system in which high-cost revolving credit becomes a routine substitute for adequate current margin, especially where the same borrowing then increases future fixed payments.

11. Conclusion

Between 2021 and 2023, the three largest Canadian household consumption categories—shelter, food, and transportation—rose from approximately 61.8% to 63.6% of average consumption. Estimated spending on that bundle increased 17.6%, outpacing total consumption growth by 3.3 percentage points. The lowest income quintile devoted approximately 66.0% to the same three categories in 2023. These results establish core-consumption margin compression.

Independent debt and credit evidence shows why the change matters. Household debt remained high relative to disposable income, balance-carrying was widespread, and persistent or intensive credit-card use predicted later arrears. The findings do not prove that wages alone caused debt or that every borrower is trapped. They show that aggregate income and wealth indicators can conceal a more immediate economic relation: when essential commitments claim a growing share, credit can preserve present consumption by reducing future margin.

Income without margin is therefore not income without value. It is income whose increase does not translate proportionally into flexibility. Measuring that distinction provides a clearer foundation for household economics and for policies concerned with resilience rather than aggregate solvency alone.
