SIGNALRUPTURE: CYBER-EPISTEMIC INFRASTRUCTURAL DIAGNOSTIC

FIELD

SR-CEIDF Cross-Layer Instability, Diagnostic Visibility, Infrastructure Coupling, Propagation, and

Operational

Recovery

Empirical Field Edition · 2026 SignalRupture (SR)

Abstract SignalRupture Cyber-Epistemic Infrastructural Diagnostic Field (SR-CEIDF) defines

an

interdisciplinary

diagnostic

architecture

for

studying

instability

across

epistemic,

infrastructural,

diagnostic,

and

propagation

layers.

The central proposition of SR-CEIDF is that contemporary systemic failure cannot

be

adequately

understood

by

examining

isolated

institutional

failures,

technological

failures,

information

failures,

or

infrastructure

failures

independently.

Instability

may

emerge

through

interactions

among

these

layers,

particularly

when

epistemic

drift,

recursive

contamination,

infrastructural

strain,

diagnostic

visibility

loss,

and

cross-layer

propagation

reinforce

one

another.

SR-CEIDF therefore defines five principal state variables: Drift, Contamination,

Harm,

Visibility

Lag,

and

Drift-Transfer

φ.

These

variables

form a proposed SR system state from which collapse boundaries, diagnostic surfaces,

propagation

dynamics,

governance

responses,

and

operational

interventions

can

be

defined.

The framework distinguishes rigorously between empirical measurements and

SR-specific

constructs.

Real-world

measurements

such

as

semantic

drift,

latency,

packet

loss,

detection

lag,

telemetry

degradation,

contamination

ratios,

and

observed

propagation

are

empirical

inputs.

SR

invariants,

composite

indices,

thresholds,

propagation

kernels,

collapse

boundaries,

and

diagnostic

surfaces

are

proposed

theoretical

operationalizations

unless

and

until

independent

empirical

datasets

validate

them.

The field consequently operates as a hybrid architecture: Empirical observation → SR operationalization → state representation →

boundary

assessment

→

diagnosis

→

governance

→

intervention

→

stabilization

→

empirical

validation.

SR-CEIDF does not claim that its proposed variables have already been universally

validated.

It

defines

a

measurement

architecture

through

which

those

variables

can

become

testable.

The purpose of the framework is therefore not to predict collapse through prophecy.

It

is

to

make

structural

instability

observable,

measurable,

comparable,

operationally

actionable,

and

ultimately

falsifiable.

1. Epistemic Contamination and Model Degeneration Recent research provides empirical support for the proposition that recursive dependence

on

synthetic

data

can

alter

the

statistical

properties

of

machine-learning

systems.

Shumailov

et

al.

(2024)

demonstrate

that

when

generative

models

are

recursively

trained

on

data

generated

by

earlier

generations

of

models,

model

performance

can

progressively

deteriorate.

Their

analysis

identifies

a

process

in

which

information

from

the

original

data

distribution

can

be

progressively

lost,

including

information

located

in

the

tails

of

the

distribution.

The

authors

describe

this

phenomenon

as

model

collapse

and

demonstrate

its

occurrence

across

several

generative-model

settings

(Shumailov

et

al.,

2024).

Alemohammad et al. (2024) independently examine recursive self-consumption

in

generative

models

and

introduce

the

term

Model

Autophagy

Disorder

(MAD)

to

describe

degradation

occurring

when

generated

data

are

repeatedly

used

as

training

material.

Their

experiments

examine

different

self-consuming

regimes

and

report

progressive

reductions

in

either

precision

or

diversity

when

sufficient

fresh

real

data

are

not

maintained

across

generations

(Alemohammad

et

al.,

2024).

These studies do not establish SR-CEIDF’s constructs of Drift, Contamination,

or

φ- Propagation

as

validated

scientific

variables.

They

do,

however,

establish

an

empirically

documented

mechanism

through

which

recursive

information-generation

processes

can

alter

the

stability

and

diversity of subsequent model states. SR-CEIDF therefore treats model collapse

and

self-consuming

generative

loops

as

empirical

mechanisms

relevant

to

the

proposed

Contamination

and

Drift

constructs ,

rather

than

as

proof

of

those

constructs

themselves.

The distinction is important. Existing literature establishes the phenomenon; SR-CEIDF

proposes

a

broader

diagnostic

architecture

for

representing

how

analogous

degradation

may

propagate

across

epistemic

and

infrastructural

layers.

2. AI System Security, Containment, and Diagnostic Visibility The security literature increasingly treats advanced AI systems as components

embedded

within

larger

computational

environments

rather

than

as

isolated

models.

Contemporary

AI

systems

may

interact

with

tools,

external

data

sources,

software

environments,

APIs,

agents,

and

other

infrastructure.

Consequently,

security

failures

can

occur

not

only

within

model

behavior

but

also

at

the

interfaces

between

models

and

the

environments

in

which

they

operate.

This literature supports an important premise for SR-CEIDF: system security cannot

be

reduced

to

model-level

behavior

when

the

model

is

operationally

coupled

to

external

infrastructure .

However, the stronger claim that a specific Cloud Security Alliance report has

already

established

a

general

empirical

condition

of

“frontier-scale

AI

containment

fragility”

should

not

be

retained

without

identifying

and

independently

verifying

the

exact

report

and

its

underlying

evidence.

The

existence

of

AI-security

and

AI-governance

literature

does

not

by

itself

establish

the

SR-specific

construct

of

containment

fragility.

SR-CEIDF therefore treats containment, isolation, monitoring, and tool-interface

security

as

empirical

security

concerns

that

may

contribute

to

diagnostic

and

propagation

risk ,

while

retaining

Visibility

Lag

and

φ- Propagation

as

SR

operational

constructs

requiring

independent

validation.

This distinction preserves the connection to contemporary AI-security research

without

attributing

conclusions

to

the

literature

that

the

cited

sources

do

not

actually

establish.

3. Automated Bureaucracy and Algorithmic Visibility The transformation of administrative decision-making through information and

communication

technologies

has

a

substantial

foundation

in

public-administration

research.

Bovens

and

Zouridis

(2002)

describe

the

movement

from

street-level

bureaucracies

toward

what

they

term

system-level

bureaucracies ,

in

which

software

designers

and

system

analysts

increasingly

shape

the

implementation

of

administrative

rules.

Their

analysis

emphasizes

the

implications

of

this

transformation

for

administrative

discretion, constitutional control, due process, and fairness (Bovens & Zouridis,

2002).

This work is particularly relevant to SR-CEIDF because it demonstrates that computational

infrastructure

can

alter

not

merely

the

efficiency

of

administration

but

the

location

of

discretion

and

the

mechanisms

through

which

institutional

decisions

are

produced.

Noble (2018), in Algorithms of Oppression , examines how search-engine systems

can

reproduce

and

reinforce

racial

and

gendered

inequalities

through

the

organization

and

ranking

of

information.

Her

analysis

demonstrates

that

algorithmic

search

results

are

not

necessarily

neutral

reflections

of

relevance

and

that

commercial

and

social

structures

can

shape

what

becomes

visible,

discoverable,

and

prominent

within

information

environments

(Noble,

2018).

Together, these works establish an important empirical and theoretical foundation

for

SR-CEIDF’s

treatment

of

diagnostic

visibility

and

institutional

information

systems .

They

do

not,

however,

establish

SR’s

Visibility

Lag

construct

directly.

Rather,

they

demonstrate

that

computational

systems

can

mediate

administrative

discretion

and

information

visibility,

providing

established

literature

against

which

SR’s

proposed

diagnostic

constructs

can

be

tested.

4. AI Infrastructure, Electricity Demand, and Systemic Strain

The physical infrastructure supporting contemporary AI systems introduces another

layer

of

systemic

dependency.

The

rapid

expansion

of

AI

data

centers

has

increased

attention

to

electricity

demand,

power-delivery

architecture,

grid

interconnection,

thermal

management,

and

the

temporal

and

spatial

characteristics

of

data-center

loads.

Recent research explicitly examines the relationship between AI data-center growth

and

electric-power

systems.

Chen

et

al.

(2025)

review

the

electricity

requirements

associated

with

AI

data

centers

across

model

preparation,

training,

fine-tuning,

and

inference

and

identify

challenges

involving

long-term

planning,

interconnection,

electricity-market

operation,

and

real-time

grid

dynamics

(Chen

et

al.,

2025).

These findings provide empirical support for treating AI computation as materially

dependent

upon

electrical

and

infrastructural

systems.

They

do

not ,

by

themselves,

establish

SR-CEIDF’s

Harm

invariant

or

demonstrate

that

AI

workloads

necessarily

produce

systemic

infrastructural

collapse.

Instead,

they

establish

the

underlying

infrastructural

conditions

that

make

such

questions

empirically

investigable.

SR-CEIDF therefore conceptualizes infrastructural strain as a measurable substrate

condition.

The

proposed

Harm

construct

is

intended

to

integrate

relevant

empirical

indicators—such

as

load

concentration,

latency

degradation,

packet

loss,

backbone

strain,

and

other

system-specific

measures—into

an

SR

operational

representation.

The

weights

and

thresholds

proposed elsewhere in this framework remain model parameters requiring calibration.
