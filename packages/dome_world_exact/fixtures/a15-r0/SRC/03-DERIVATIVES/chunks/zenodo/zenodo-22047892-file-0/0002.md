1.1 The Domain–Causality Distinction A policy jurisdiction is an administrative boundary. A causal system is not necessarily

bounded

by

that

jurisdiction.

The

distinction

is

Governments

may

component

Agencies

may

without

Public

may

be

whose

or

SR calls this condition infrastructure dependency. The policy actor may remain

formally

sovereign

while

becoming

This creates a structural asymmetry. Authority can be visible at the top of a system

Policy

The SR Policy Substrate treats effective policy agency as a function of formal authority,

A simplified representation is: A_eff(t) = A_f(t) × C(t) × V_A(t) / [1 + Dep(t)]

The equation is not presented as a universal empirical law at this stage. It is a calibration

CHAPTER 2 — WHAT IS A POLICY SUBSTRATE? The term substrate is used deliberately. A substrate is the layer through which higher-order

processes

operate.

In

SR,

policy

is

not

suspended

above

society

as

an

independent

command

layer.

It

operates

through

infrastructure,

institutions,

markets,

administrative

systems,

technologies,

and

human

capacities.

A policy substrate therefore contains the conditions that make some policy actions

possible,

others

costly,

and

others

nearly

impossible.

The framework defines six primary substrate dimensions: •

Infrastructure — physical, financial, digital, logistical, informational, and organizational

systems

required

for

policy

execution.

•

Dependency — concentration of essential functions in actors or systems outside

the

direct

control

of

the

policy

authority.

•

Capacity — the material and administrative ability to execute policy. •

Incentive — the reward and penalty structure influencing actor behavior.

•

Visibility — the degree to which relevant causal conditions are observable to

decision-makers

and

affected

populations.

•

Exposure — the distribution of consequences across populations, institutions,

and

time.

These dimensions interact. Infrastructure creates dependency. Dependency alters

incentives.

Incentives

affect

institutional

behavior.

Institutional

behavior

changes

exposure.

Exposure

generates

outcomes.

Outcomes

become

visible

through

measurement

systems

that

themselves

may

be

incomplete.

The substrate is therefore dynamic rather than static. 2.1 The Substrate as a Causal Layer The policy substrate can be represented as a vector X_s(t) = [I, Dep, C, Inc, V,

E],

where

each

component

may

itself

be

multidimensional.

The

policy

layer

P(t)

sits

above

this

vector,

while

outcomes

Y(t)

emerge

from

their

interaction.

Y(t) = F[P(t), X_s(t), ε( t)] The conventional policy model may estimate Y as a function of P. SR asks whether

omission

of

X_s

produces

systematic

residual

structure.

If residuals are systematically associated with infrastructure concentration, dependency,

capacity

constraints,

incentive

asymmetry,

visibility

lag,

or

exposure

concentration,

then

the

policy-surface

model

is

incomplete.

The framework's empirical task is therefore not to replace existing policy models

but

to

test

whether

adding

substrate

variables

improves

causal

explanation,

prediction,

transportability,

and

intervention

design.

This gives the framework a direct route to falsification. If substrate variables do

not

improve

explanation

across

cases,

the

framework's

stronger

claims

weaken.

If

they

repeatedly

explain

residual

variation

that

policy-surface

variables

cannot,

the

substrate

hypothesis

gains

support.

CHAPTER 3 — SR ONTOLOGY FOR POLICY SR begins from five structural operators: Pressure, Reflex, Drift, Collapse, and

Reorganization.

These

are

not

metaphors.

Within

the

policy

substrate

framework

they

are

treated

as

process

categories

that

can

be

operationalized.

3.1 Pressure Pressure is the accumulation of constraints acting on an institutional or social system.

Pressure

may

arise

from

resource

scarcity,

infrastructure

dependency,

fiscal

constraints,

demographic

change,

technological

concentration,

labor

shortages,

or

accumulated

unmet

demand.

3.2 Reflex Reflex is the system's immediate adaptive response to pressure. Reflex can stabilize

a

system

temporarily

while

simultaneously

moving

pressure

elsewhere.

Examples

include

emergency

funding,

administrative

triage,

temporary

outsourcing,

eligibility

restriction,

queue

management,

and

symbolic

policy

announcements.

3.3 Drift

Drift is cumulative structural movement away from an intended state. Drift can

occur

while

formal

policy

remains

unchanged.

It

is

therefore

possible

for

policy

stability

to

coexist

with

structural

instability.

3.4 Collapse Collapse occurs when accumulated drift and pressure exceed the system's ability

to

absorb

or

transfer

them

without

major

loss

of

function.

3.5 Reorganization Reorganization is the formation of a new structural arrangement after destabilization.

It

may

restore

capacity,

redistribute

dependency,

institutionalize

a

workaround,

or

create

a

new

dependency.

3.6 The SR Policy State The policy system at time t is represented as: S(t) = {D(t), Δ V(t), H(t), Φ( t)} D(t) = structural drift. Δ V(t) = visibility lag, the temporal distance between structural change and reliable

recognition

of

that

change.

H(t) = accumulated structural harm or consequence exposure. Φ( t) = drift transfer, the movement of unresolved pressure from one subsystem

to

another.

A policy system may appear stable while some underlying conditions worsen if recognition

is

delayed

or

measured

at

a

different

layer.

This

is

a

testable

possibility

rather

than

a

general

law.

This is the structural logic of quiet governance: the absence of immediate political

visibility

does

not

imply

the

absence

of

structural

movement.

CHAPTER 4 — CAUSAL SUBSTRATE AND POLICY PROBLEMS A policy problem is conventionally represented as an undesirable condition requiring

collective

action.

SR

adds

a

second

representation:

the

generative

system

that

produces

and

reproduces

the

condition.

Let Y_p denote a policy-visible outcome. Let X_s denote the substrate. Let P denote

the

policy

intervention.

Then:

Y_p(t+1) = F[Y_p(t), P(t), X_s(t)] + ε( t) If X_s is omitted, P may appear to have a direct effect that is actually mediated

by

substrate

conditions.

This is particularly important when policy is compensatory rather than transformative.

A

transfer

can

reduce

measured

hardship

without

changing

the

infrastructure

that

produces

the

hardship.

A

subsidy

can

reduce

immediate

exposure

while

increasing

dependence

on

the

system

being

subsidized.

A

regulation

can

change

behavior

at

one

node

while

shifting

pressure

to

another.

SR calls this substrate displacement. Substrate displacement occurs when an intervention changes the location or expression

of

a

problem

without

changing

its

generative

mechanism.

4.1 The Causal Depth Test

The Causal Depth Test asks whether an identified policy problem has been traced

through

at

least

four

layers:

1. Observed outcome — what is visibly happening? 2. Institutional response — what policy or administrative mechanism is responding? 3.

Operational substrate — what infrastructure, capacity, dependency, or incentive

determines

whether

the

response

can

work?

4. Generative substrate — what deeper structure produces the operational condition?

The test is intentionally recursive. If the explanation stops at the policy instrument,

the

analysis

has

not

yet

established

causal

depth.

A policy analysis may pass conventional evaluation standards while failing the

Causal

Depth

Test.

SR

does

not

treat

that

as

invalid

evaluation;

it

treats

it

as

a

different

level

of

explanation.

CHAPTER 5 — POLICY AGENCY UNDER SUBSTRATE DEPENDENCY The central agency question in the SR Policy Substrate is not simply who has authority.

It

is

who

can

alter

the

conditions

that

generate

the

outcome.

Formal authority can remain intact while effective agency declines. This occurs

when

the

infrastructure

required

to

exercise

authority

is

controlled

elsewhere,

when

viable

alternatives

collapse,

or

when

the

cost

of

intervention

becomes

structurally

prohibitive.

The framework therefore distinguishes four concepts: •

Authority — legal or formal power to act.

•

Capacity — ability to execute an authorized action. •

Autonomy — ability to choose among meaningful alternatives. •

Substrate leverage — ability to alter the conditions that generate the policy problem.

These variables should not be collapsed into one measure. A government can have

high

authority,

moderate

capacity,

low

autonomy,

and

very

low

substrate

leverage.

This distinction is especially important in infrastructure-dependent governance.

5.1 Infrastructural Coercion SR defines infrastructural coercion as the condition in which dependency concentration,

the

collapse

of

viable

alternatives,

mandatory

navigation,

and

consequence

exposure

combine

to

constrain

meaningful

choice.

The Infrastructural Coercion Index is represented as: ICI = (D × M × CE) / VA where D is dependency concentration, M is mandatory navigation, CE is consequence

exposure,

and

VA

is

viable

alternatives.
