CHAPTER 13 — POLICY PROBLEM TAXONOMY The framework identifies several recurring policy-substrate configurations. 13.1 Hidden-Substrate Problem The visible policy problem is well measured, but its generative infrastructure is

poorly

observed.

13.2 Dependency Problem The institution has formal authority but depends on infrastructure it does not control.

13.3 Capacity Mirage

An institution appears capable because authority is formally assigned, but operational

resources

are

insufficient.

13.4 Visibility-Lag Problem Harm accumulates faster than institutional recognition. 13.5 Transfer Problem A policy reduces pressure in one system by transferring it to another. 13.6 Incentive Lock-In The system rewards maintenance of the problem more strongly than transformation

of

its

substrate.

13.7 Insulated Governance Decision-makers or institutions are buffered from the consequences experienced

by

the

populations

affected

by

the

policy

substrate.

13.8 Recursive Policy Failure Each corrective intervention creates conditions that reproduce the original problem

at

a

later

stage.

These configurations can overlap. A single policy problem may simultaneously

exhibit

dependency,

visibility

lag,

transfer,

and

incentive

lock-in.

The most defensible institutional placement is as a specialized policy-science framework

Autonomous

The object remains analytically consequential even without autonomous disciplinary

The SR question is: What structural substrate generates, constrains, delays, transfers, or reproduces

This question is broader than implementation and narrower than general social

14.1 Boundary Conditions SR does not claim that every policy problem is primarily infrastructural. Some

The

The research program should therefore report where the model performs poorly,

CHAPTER 15 — THE POLICY SURFACE–SUBSTRATE GAP The Policy Surface–Substrate Gap is the difference between the causal structure

PSSG = C_required − C_represented A positive gap means the policy model captures less of the causal system than

The concept explains why some policies repeatedly produce disappointing results

The

It

The gap also provides a practical research tool. Analysts can map each policy variable

to

the

substrate

mechanism

expected

to

Unmapped

mechanisms

become

CHAPTER 16 — GOVERNMENT AGENCY UNDER INFRASTRUCTURE DEPENDENCY

The Government Agency Under Infrastructure Dependency framework is a direct

Government agency is traditionally associated with formal authority, institutional

SR

The decomposition is: Agency = Authority + Capacity + Autonomy + Substrate Leverage These dimensions are analytically separable. A government can legislate against a condition while lacking control over the infrastructure

This

The policy substrate framework therefore treats infrastructure dependency as a

16.1 Institutional Insulation Gradient The Institutional Insulation Gradient is defined as: IIG = Institutional Redundancy / Public Redundancy When institutions possess high redundancy while affected populations possess

This asymmetry matters because political feedback depends partly on who experiences

The framework therefore treats exposure distribution as part of policy causality

•

17.1 Cross-Pillar Drift Cross-pillar drift occurs when pressure accumulates in one capacity pillar and transfers

Let D_i represent drift in pillar i. Then:

D_j(t+1) = D_j(t) + Σ φ_ ij D_i(t) The matrix Φ describes transfer pathways. Sparse estimation can identify which

This provides a mathematical basis for the SR claim that policy problems can migrate

CHAPTER 18 — POLICY DRIFT AND TEMPORAL STRUCTURE Policy drift is often understood as failure to update policy as conditions change.

SR

broadens

the

concept

by

distinguishing

policy

drift

from

substrate

drift.

Policy drift: the policy remains relatively stable while the environment changes.

Substrate drift: the underlying causal system changes, potentially without visible

policy

change.

The two can interact. A policy may be stable because its formal parameters remain unchanged. Yet if

infrastructure

concentration,

demographic

conditions,

labor

markets,

technology,

or

exposure

patterns

change,

the

policy's

effective

meaning

can

drift.

This is why historical comparison is indispensable. 18.1 The Drift Differential The Outcome Drift Differential is: ODD = Δ Y_observed − Δ Y_policy_expected

A persistent non-zero ODD indicates that policy-surface expectations systematically

diverge

from

observed

outcomes.

SR predicts that ODD should be partly explained by unmodeled substrate drift.

The empirical test is straightforward: estimate the conventional model, calculate

residual

drift,

and

determine

whether

substrate

variables

explain

the

residual.

CHAPTER 19 — POLICY INTERVENTION TYPOLOGY SR classifies interventions according to where they act. •

Surface intervention — changes the visible outcome without changing the substrate.

•

Buffer intervention — reduces exposure while the substrate remains. •

Transfer intervention — shifts pressure between systems. •

Control intervention — changes an operational substrate condition. •

Transformative intervention — changes the generative substrate. •

Reorganizational intervention — creates a new structural configuration after disruption.

This taxonomy prevents all successful short-term outcomes from being treated

as

equivalent.

A buffer can be valuable. SR does not deny its value. It simply distinguishes relief

from

transformation.

19.1 The Substrate Transformation Ratio STR = Δ X_s / Δ Y_target The ratio should be interpreted alongside outcome improvement. A policy that

improves

outcomes

while

barely

changing

the

substrate

may

be

a

successful

buffer.

A

policy

that

changes

the

substrate

and

produces

durable

improvement

is

closer

to

transformation.

Longitudinal studies should therefore measure both outcome trajectories and substrate

trajectories.
