CHAPTER 22 — NETWORK AND SYSTEM-DYNAMICS FORMALIZATION

Policy substrates are naturally represented as networks. Nodes can represent institutions,

infrastructures,

populations,

or

capacities.

Edges

can

represent

dependency,

information

flow,

resource

flow,

or

exposure.

Let G = (V,E) denote a substrate network. Dependency centrality can be estimated

using

weighted

degree,

betweenness,

eigenvector

centrality,

or

custom

concentration

measures.

A node with high dependency centrality may represent a structural bottleneck.

System dynamics can then model feedback: Pressure → Reflex → Transfer → Accumulated Harm → Visibility → Policy

Response

→

New

Pressure.

The purpose of the network is not visual complexity. It is to expose relationships

that

are

hidden

when

policy

is

represented

as

a

single

decision

22.1 Feedback and Recursive Erosion Recursive erosion occurs when a policy response reduces immediate pressure but

weakens

the

substrate's

future

capacity.

For example, repeated emergency substitution may preserve service continuity

while

reducing

investment

in

durable

capacity.

The

system

becomes

increasingly

dependent

on

emergency

response.

The recursion can be represented as: C(t+1) = C(t) + Investment(t) − Erosion(t)

Investment may be reduced when emergency measures consume fiscal or organizational

capacity.

Erosion

may

therefore

increase

after

repeated

shocks.

SR predicts that systems with persistent emergency reflexes should be examined

for

hidden

capacity

erosion.

CHAPTER 23 — EPISTEMIC SUBSTRATE The policy substrate is also epistemic. What institutions can know depends on

classification

systems,

data

architectures,

reporting

incentives,

administrative

categories,

and

information

SR therefore distinguishes physical substrate from epistemic substrate. Physical substrate determines what can be materially done. Epistemic substrate

determines

what

can

be

institutionally

known

and

recognized.

When epistemic substrate is fragmented, causal visibility can decline even if raw

data

volume

rises.

This is particularly important in algorithmically mediated governance, where ranking,

indexing,

metadata,

and

access

systems

can

alter

which

signals

become

institutionally

salient.

23.1 Structural Ignorance Structural Ignorance is not simply absence of knowledge. It is a condition in which

the

architecture

of

information

production

systematically

prevents

relevant

causal

relationships

from

becoming

visible.

A Structural Ignorance Index can combine missingness, fragmentation, latency,

classification

mismatch,

and

access

asymmetry.

SII = Σ( w_i m_i) where m_i represents normalized ignorance conditions. The index is diagnostic rather than moral. Its purpose is to identify where a policy

system's

knowledge

architecture

is

structurally

incomplete.

CHAPTER 24 — ADMINISTRATIVE ABSTRACTION Administrative systems require categories. Categories simplify reality so institutions

can

But

SR defines Administrative Abstraction as the degree to which administrative representation

AAI = represented complexity / required causal complexity A low ratio indicates a large abstraction gap. This concept connects the policy substrate to the measurement problem. If the

an

CHAPTER 25 — POLICY FEEDBACK RECONSIDERED Policy feedback theory emphasizes how policies reshape politics, interests, identities,

and

institutions.

SR

adds

that

feedback

can

also

reshape

infrastructure

and

dependency.

A policy can create new dependencies. Those dependencies can change incentives.

Incentives

can

change

political

coalitions.

Coalitions

can

defend

the

policy

arrangement.

The

policy

then

becomes

part

of

its

own

substrate.

This is recursive policy construction. The implication is that the policy should not always be treated as an external intervention.

Over

time,

policy

can

become

endogenous

to

the

substrate.

25.1 Endogenization Let X_s(t) be substrate conditions and P(t) policy. Conventional analysis may assume:

P(t) → X_s(t) → Y(t) SR adds feedback: Y(t) → P(t+1) and: P(t) → X_s(t+1) → P(t+1) Policy therefore becomes part of the system it is intended to govern. This is a core reason why static policy evaluation can miss long-run structural effects.

CHAPTER 26 — INSTITUTIONAL COGNITION Institutions do not simply possess information. They possess patterned ways of

recognizing

information.

SR's

institutional

cognition

framework

asks

how

systems

decide

what

counts

as

evidence,

what

counts

as

a

problem,

and

what

counts

as

an

actionable

cause.

A policy substrate can therefore include cognitive routines. When an institution repeatedly classifies a structural problem as an individual failure,

the

classification

becomes

part

of

the

substrate.

When it repeatedly treats infrastructure failure as isolated incidents, fragmentation

becomes

epistemically

stabilized.

The framework calls this institutional cognition because the relevant unit is not

individual

psychology

but

the

patterned

recognition

architecture

of

the

institution.

26.1 Five Institutional Cognition Tests 20. Recognition test — does the institution recognize the relevant condition? 21. Attribution test — does it attribute the condition to the correct causal level? 22. Integration test — can it connect evidence across institutional boundaries? 23. Response test — can recognized causes be translated into action? 24. Learning test — does the institution update after intervention failure? Failure at any stage can generate a policy problem that persists despite apparent

responsiveness.

CHAPTER 27 — COLLAPSE AS A POLICY SUBSTRATE PHENOMENON Collapse in SR is not synonymous with institutional disappearance. A system can

continue

operating

losing

capacities

stable

The policy system may therefore enter a pre-collapse condition characterized by

rising

rising

increasing

A practical collapse indicator is: CRI = f(D, H, Δ V, C_loss) where C_loss represents declining capacity. The important point is temporal. Collapse may be visible only after the system

has

A

substrate

analysis

seeks

earlier

27.1 Pre-Collapse Signature •

Persistent non-zero drift. •

Increasing emergency intervention. •

Growing transfer of pressure between systems. •

Increasing visibility lag. •

Declining effective alternatives. •

Rising exposure concentration. •

Institutional insulation from consequences. •

Increasing mismatch between policy claims and observed outcomes. The combination is more informative than any single indicator. CHAPTER 28 — REORGANIZATION AND POLICY DESIGN When a substrate reaches a destabilized condition, reorganization becomes possible.

Reorganization

may

SR therefore asks policy designers not only how to repair a system but what new

A reform that replaces one dependency with another may reduce immediate harm

Reorganization should therefore be evaluated using three questions: 25. What substrate condition is removed? 26. What new substrate condition is created? 27. CHAPTER 29 — RESEARCH METHODS The framework is methodologically plural but theoretically disciplined. •

Structural equation modeling for latent substrate constructs. •

Panel and longitudinal regression for temporal dynamics. •

Sparse VAR models for drift propagation. •

Network analysis for dependency structures. •

System dynamics for recursive feedback. •

Comparative case studies for mechanism tracing. •

Process tracing for causal sequence reconstruction. •

Interrupted time series and difference-in-differences for intervention effects. •

Synthetic datasets for model validation. •

Sensitivity analysis for weighting and measurement uncertainty.

Method selection follows the causal question. No method is elevated into a universal

SR

method.

A generator can specify known drift matrices Φ, visibility lags, exposure pathways,

and

intervention

effects.

Researchers

recover

the

known

parameters

under

noise,

missingness,

measurement

error,

and

varying

sample

Synthetic validation is especially useful before applying complex indices to real-world

policy

systems.

CHAPTER 30 — WEIGHTING, CALIBRATION, AND UNCERTAINTY Composite indices can become arbitrary if weights are hidden. SR therefore requires

explicit

weighting

rules.

Weights may be derived from theory, empirical concentration, expert elicitation,

entropy-based

methods,

or

estimated

latent

structures.

Each

choice

should

be

disclosed.

Researchers should publish sensitivity intervals rather than a single supposedly

exact

score.

For a composite index Q = Σ w_i x_i, uncertainty should be propagated through

the

weights

and

measurements.

A ranking that changes under plausible weighting schemes should be reported

as

unstable.

30.1 The Rule Against False Precision SR does not claim that a structural index is scientific merely because it contains

decimals.

Numerical precision must correspond to measurement reliability. The appropriate output may be an interval, distribution, ordinal classification, or

posterior

estimate

rather

than

a

single

point.

This rule protects the framework from pseudo-quantification and keeps mathematical

formalization

connected

to

empirical

evidence.
