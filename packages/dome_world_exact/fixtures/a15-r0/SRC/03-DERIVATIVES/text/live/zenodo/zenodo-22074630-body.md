# Infrastructural Political Economy Field: Government Agency Under Infrastructure Dependency

## Zenodo description

Infrastructural Political Economy: Government Agency Under Infrastructure Dependency introduces a new analytical field for understanding how modern states exercise agency within privatized infrastructural environments. The monograph formalizes the GAUID/GAI framework — Government Agency Under Infrastructure Dependency — a structural, mathematical, and empirical model designed to measure how dependency, substitution capacity, regulatory leverage, and operator structural power shape the operational boundaries of contemporary state action.

 Modern governments increasingly rely on infrastructures they do not own, do not fully control, and cannot easily replace. As the manuscript states, “agency is no longer a purely institutional property. It is infrastructural.” Telecommunications networks, cloud platforms, logistics chains, payment systems, and wage‑setting mechanisms have become privately governed socio‑technical systems. Classical theories of autonomy, capacity, and bureaucratic authority cannot account for this condition.

 The GAUID/GAI framework addresses this gap by integrating conceptual theory, non‑linear mathematical formulation, and cross‑sector empirical calibration. The model provides normalized scoring for Infrastructure Dependency, Substitution Capacity, Regulatory Leverage, and Structural Power, along with interaction terms and threshold lock‑in dynamics. A full measurement architecture is included, enabling sector‑level and national‑level Government Agency Index (GAI) computation.

 The monograph includes conceptual foundations, structural logic, mathematical equations, sensitivity analysis, and a complete empirical case study. The Appendix contains the full technical specification required for replication and further research.

 This work establishes Infrastructural Political Economy as a foundational field and provides the first replicable, cross‑national method for evaluating how infrastructural conditions constrain or enable modern state agency.

 Funding/Contribution 
 This monograph contributes three major advances to contemporary political economy, governance studies, and socio‑technical analysis:

 1. Establishes a new field: Infrastructural Political Economy 
 It defines the conceptual and analytical space for studying how infrastructural ownership, governance, and operation shape political outcomes and state behavior. It fills a gap left by classical theories that assume states act through systems they control.

 2. Introduces the GAUID/GAI framework — the first quantitative model of infrastructural agency 
 The work provides a complete mathematical structure for measuring government agency under infrastructural dependency. It includes normalized variables, interaction terms, threshold dynamics, and a clamping‑based normalization function ensuring interpretive consistency.

 3. Provides a replicable empirical architecture 
 The monograph offers a full measurement strategy, sectoral scoring system, weighting model, and national‑level aggregation method. The Appendix includes the complete technical specification, enabling researchers, regulators, and institutions to replicate the model across countries and sectors.

 Together, these contributions make the monograph the foundational text of the Infrastructural Political Economy field and the first operational framework for evaluating how privatized infrastructures shape the boundaries of modern state agency.

Author’s Note

This monograph establishes Infrastructural Political Economy (IPE) as an empirically active SR field and Government Agency Under Infrastructure Dependency (GAUID) as its core causal theory. It does not present GAUID as a validated national scoring standard. The empirical gauntlet removes that overclaim. Every number in this edition is classified as an observed statistic, a transparent transformation, an estimated association, or an unidentified parameter. Researcher-coded demonstration values are retained only where they explain a measurement procedure and are never reported as national facts.

The field’s residual object begins downstream of the established proposition that infrastructures and network concentration matter. IPE asks whether essential-infrastructure configuration changes the executable range of government action and the allocation of adjustment burdens. That claim must survive comparison with state-capacity theory, industrial organization, energy economics, network power, regulatory governance, resilience analysis, and conventional incidence models.

Empirical Standing and Field Boundary

CURRENT SCIENTIFIC STATUS: FULL SR FIELD — EMPIRICALLY ACTIVE, EMPIRICALLY GROUNDED, NOT YET FULLY VALIDATED.

The field survives as a bounded research program because it identifies a distinct dependent variable—effective executable agency—and a distinct downstream mechanism: dependency becomes politically consequential when control is asymmetric, exit is costly, substitution is slow, and adjustment burdens are transferred. It does not survive as a claim that privatization automatically eliminates sovereignty or that a single composite score can establish national agency.

Executive Summary

The gauntlet produces five decisive corrections. First, the manuscript’s earlier GAUID sector numbers were illustrative codings, not empirical estimates; they are removed from substantive conclusions. Second, the earlier text reversed the direction of Substitution Capacity and Regulatory Leverage in several constraint equations. This edition fixes directionality. Third, GAI is reserved for an agency score and PCC for a private-control constraint score; the two are never substituted for each other. Fourth, no universal 0.40, 0.45, 0.60, or 0.70 threshold is presented as fact. Thresholds must be estimated from independent outcomes. Fifth, the European gas shock rejects exposure-only determinism: pre-shock Russian-gas exposure did not robustly predict failure to reduce demand or the magnitude of fiscal buffering in the exploratory tests supplied to this monograph.

Appendix I — Negative Controls and Placebo Tests

Negative-control outcomes should be affected by general political and economic conditions but not by the specific infrastructure pathway. Placebo shock dates should precede the observed disruption. If GAUID variables predict placebo events or unrelated outcomes as strongly as the target, the model is likely capturing generic vulnerability, temporal trend, or researcher degrees of freedom rather than the proposed mechanism. The preregistration must identify both outcome and exposure placebos before estimation.

Required empirical output

The output must be a reconstructable table or model result rather than a narrative classification. It must identify the unit of analysis, source, observation window, denominator, transformation, uncertainty, comparator, and decision rule. A result that cannot be independently reconstructed is excluded from field-level validation.

Failure condition

This component fails if the required information is unavailable, unreliable, non-invariant, circularly defined, or unable to improve the prespecified baseline. Failure is reported as a boundary of the present evidence, not converted into confirmation by conceptual argument.

Appendix J — Missing Data and Uncertainty

Final Methodological Statement

Infrastructural Political Economy survives the empirical gauntlet as a full SR field under active validation. It survives by abandoning the claim that dependence itself is proof of state incapacity. Its scientifically defensible core is narrower: essential-infrastructure configuration may compress effective executable agency and shape the allocation of adjustment burdens when control is asymmetric, exit is costly, and substitution is weak. GAUID is the causal theory; GAI is a construct awaiting direct cross-national identification; neither is a substitute for empirical outcomes.

The first empirical test is adversarial and productive. The European gas shock shows dependency, rupture, costly adaptation, rapid substitution, and partial recovery. Exposure alone does not robustly predict adjustment failure or fiscal buffering. The evidence therefore kills the deterministic model and preserves the conditional model. The next decisive tests must measure the Agency Gap and burden-allocation vector directly and must show incremental held-out performance beyond conventional political economy. If those tests fail across domains, the field contracts. If they survive, Infrastructural Political Economy earns its independent empirical standing.
