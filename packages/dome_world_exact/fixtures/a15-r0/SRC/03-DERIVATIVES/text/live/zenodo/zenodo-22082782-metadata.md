NERC&rsquo;s 2025 Long‑Term Reliability Assessment forecasts more than <strong>224 GW</strong> of additional North American summer peak demand over the next decade &mdash; a <strong>69% increase</strong> over the prior ten‑year forecast &mdash; driven largely by data centers supporting AI and digital‑economy loads. The manuscript shows that SR&rsquo;s grammar can represent this race without exaggeration: rapid demand growth is not collapse; it is a structural condition that can be absorbed through restoration or produce tightening margins depending on the trajectory of dependable additions.</p>\n<p>The manuscript contributes by imposing <strong>strict classification discipline</strong>. It rejects nameplate capacity as a measure of durable capacity, adopting the electricity sector&rsquo;s mature reliability standards. It rejects the temptation to treat normal reserve use, storage dispatch, or routine demand response as extraordinary compensation. It freezes the rule that <strong>C_S is normal resilience</strong>, not evidence of deterioration. Extraordinary compensation begins only when the system moves outside ordinary planning assumptions. This protects SR from misclassifying engineered flexibility as collapse and forces the grammar to respect domain‑specific semantics.</p>\n<p>The paper also contributes by defining the <strong>electricity analogue of compensated continuity</strong>: situations where exceptional substitution preserves reliable delivery under an unresolved margin. It clarifies that this configuration is <strong>possible</strong> and <strong>testable</strong>, but not yet demonstrated continent‑wide. It also defines the electricity analogue of compensatory inversion &mdash; persistent gap plus constrained extraordinary substitution plus reliability deterioration &mdash; and freezes it as a <strong>prospective</strong>test rather than a descriptive claim. This prevents SR from declaring collapse based on intuition or isolated events.</p>\n<p>The manuscript&rsquo;s cross‑domain contribution is demonstrating that the <strong>structural grammar survives translation into a non-human domain</strong>. The mechanisms differ radically, but the relational roles remain stable. This is the form of invariance expected from a structural grammar: <strong>Cross-Domain Invariance</strong> resides in relational structure, not identical physical processes.</p>\n<p>Finally, the manuscript contributes by narrowing SR&rsquo;s scientific claim. The paper therefore strengthens SR&rsquo;s diagnostic identity while clarifying the next burden of proof: <strong>SR must demonstrate predictive value</strong>, not merely structural translation.
