# VIABLE OR RESTORABLE? Testing Structural Restoration Dynamics Against Control and Viability Theory

## Zenodo description

VIABLE OR RESTORABLE? — Control/Viability Theory vs Structural Restoration Dynamics 
 This manuscript is the most mathematically adversarial test in the entire SR canon. It confronts Structural Restoration Dynamics (SRD) with the strongest existing formal frameworks for constrained dynamical systems: viability theory and control theory . These fields already formalize acceptable operating regions, constraint satisfaction, reachability, regulation, capture basins, viability kernels, and long‑horizon survival under changing conditions. The paper’s purpose is to determine whether SRD is a genuinely distinct theory or simply an applied viability formulation with new domain language.

 The manuscript contributes by demonstrating that SRD cannot claim novelty for any of the following:
• staying within acceptable bounds;
• intervening before constraint violation;
• tracking moving requirements;
• reachability before boundary crossing;
• resource‑constrained intervention;
• multi‑constraint trade‑offs;
• long‑horizon survival under uncertainty.

 All of these are already core objects of viability and control theory. This adversarial test removes SRD’s ability to present dynamic constraint satisfaction as an original discovery.

 The manuscript’s strongest contribution is identifying the SRD remainder — the part of SRD that viability theory does not fully absorb. SRD’s surviving core is restoration sufficiency under a moving endogenous burden . It distinguishes between:
• viability — remaining inside acceptable constraints now;
• restorability — rebuilding durable slack, retiring accumulated deficit, and restoring capacity faster than structural restoration requirement grows.

 This distinction matters because systems can remain viable for long periods through depleting compensation , even while restoration deficit accumulates. Hospitals can remain open, grids can remain reliable, and institutions can continue functioning while backlog, maintenance debt, staffing deficit, or affordability gaps grow. This chronic deterioration is not the canonical target of viability theory, which focuses on constraint satisfaction rather than restoration sufficiency.

 The manuscript contributes by formalizing SRD’s key constructs — Restoration Deficit (RD) , Cumulative Restoration Deficit (CRD) , Closing Recovery Window (CW) , and Compensatory Depletion — and showing how each maps onto viability/control analogues. The mapping reveals that SRD’s mathematics can be embedded inside viability theory by augmenting the state vector with deficit variables. This means SRD is not a new mathematical field; its distinctiveness must come from empirical ontology , measurement architecture , and cross‑domain operationalization , not from mathematical exclusivity.

 The manuscript also contributes by clarifying the dangerous absorption risk : if SRD’s restoration variables add no predictive or diagnostic value beyond viability kernels, state constraints, and control effort, SRD collapses into an applied vocabulary. The decisive scientific question becomes whether SRD improves prediction of restoration difficulty, backlog accumulation, recovery timing, or intervention design beyond established viability models.

 The paper defines explicit falsification rules : if viability/control variables fully explain restoration outcomes, SRD must narrow; if CRD does not predict later restoration difficulty, it must be removed; if compensation sustainability can be represented adequately by ordinary resource constraints, SRD’s compensation taxonomy becomes descriptive rather than explanatory; if CW does not improve intervention timing beyond reachability analysis, it becomes a communication tool rather than a scientific construct.

 Finally, the manuscript contributes by reframing SRD’s identity. After this challenge, SRD should not claim to be a new control theory. A defensible position is:
 SRD = empirical science of restoration sufficiency in continuously operating socio‑technical systems ,
built on established dynamic‑systems mathematics but offering a distinct empirical ontology of restoration burden, slack, deficit, and compensatory depletion.

 In unified form, the manuscript establishes that viability and control theory absorb SRD’s mathematical skeleton but do not eliminate SRD’s empirical core: restoration sufficiency under moving requirement, accumulated deficit, and compensatory depletion . It positions SRD as a candidate empirical field whose value must be demonstrated through predictive improvement, not conceptual novelty. It is one of the most important narrowing and clarifying documents in the SR canon.
