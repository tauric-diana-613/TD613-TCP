# DISCIPLINARY VALIDATION SCIENCE: The Evidence Architecture of Scientific Fields, Reciprocal Institutional Accountability, and Unequal Standards of Legitimacy

## Zenodo description

DISCIPLINARY VALIDATION SCIENCE (DVS) 
 Disciplinary Validation Science is a proposed SR field that studies how scientific fields are actually validated , not how institutions claim they are validated. Its core insight is that a field is never validated as one indivisible object; instead, it possesses an evidence architecture — a heterogeneous distribution of supported, unsupported, contested, replicated, unreplicated, and institutionally protected claims. DVS treats a field as a structured population of claims, constructs, instruments, models, replications, contradictions, and institutional decisions rather than a single hypothesis. It introduces a formal grammar for evaluating fields through claim-level evidence vectors , centrality-weighted field profiles , validation coverage , contradiction burden , and institutional–empirical gaps , allowing scientific maturity to be measured independently of institutional recognition.

 DVS contributes by exposing the validation asymmetry that universities routinely apply: established fields retain curricular legitimacy despite unresolved constructs, failed replications, contradictory paradigms, and incomplete causal models, while emerging fields are often required to demonstrate whole-field coherence, unified theory, full replication, and complete validation before receiving recognition. This asymmetry is not treated as rhetorical critique; DVS formalizes it as a measurable operator — Validation Asymmetry — and specifies falsifiers, comparison classes, and burden ratios. The field therefore protects SR not by claiming immunity, but by requiring institutions to apply reciprocal accountability : SR must justify its claims, and institutions must justify their judgments using consistent, transparent, evidence-based criteria.

 DVS contributes a six-layer validation architecture — conceptual, measurement, inferential, reproducibility, cumulative, and institutional — that replaces vague whole-field labels with structured evidence profiles. It introduces claim evidence vectors that track validity, identification, replication, transportability, predictive performance, and uncertainty. It defines field profiles that preserve heterogeneity rather than collapsing thousands of claims into a single verdict. It distinguishes empirical maturity from institutional recognition , allowing DVS to measure when recognition exceeds evidence or when evidence exceeds recognition. This architecture becomes a diagnostic tool for evaluating both SR fields and established university disciplines.

 The manuscript contributes by situating DVS within existing literatures — metascience , philosophy of science , sociology of science , scientometrics , and reproducibility research — while identifying the residual gap they do not fill: none provide a formal, measurable connection between claim-level evidence, field-level maturity, and institutional judgment. DVS fills that gap by defining the unit of analysis (claim → model → program → field → institution), specifying boundary rules, and introducing a comparative empirical test program across fifteen university-recognized fields. The manuscript demonstrates empirically that incomplete validation is ordinary across established disciplines, that whole-field validation is not a scientific unit, and that universities routinely teach fields containing unresolved constructs, failed replications, contradictory theories, and non-invariant instruments without labeling them “partially validated.”

 DVS contributes by making SR an early test case rather than a protected exception. It requires SR to expose its claims, measurements, mechanisms, uncertainties, and falsifiers. It requires SR to accept negative evidence and permit canon revision. At the same time, DVS requires institutions evaluating SR to specify the defect they allege, identify the comparison class, apply matched standards, disclose uncertainty in established curricula, and justify their decisions using evidence rather than prestige or inertia. This reciprocal burden prevents institutions from dismissing SR with vague language and prevents SR from claiming legitimacy without evidence.

 Finally, DVS contributes by establishing a formal architecture for field evaluation — including claim registries, evidence ladders, contradiction diagnostics, dependency concentration, validation coverage, and institutional–empirical gap measures — and by defining the DVS Gauntlet , a falsifiable test sequence that determines whether DVS itself survives as an independent field. If existing disciplines already perform this integration adequately, if DVS constructs fail invariance, or if validation asymmetry disappears under matched controls, DVS must be merged or demoted. This makes DVS scientifically vulnerable rather than self-protective.

 In unified form, DVS provides SR with a scientific shield that is not rhetorical : it shows that incomplete validation is normal across university fields, that whole-field dismissal is not a legitimate scientific operation, and that institutions must apply the same evidentiary standards to themselves that they apply to SR. It transforms “validation” from a vague institutional judgment into a measurable, falsifiable, reciprocal scientific process.
