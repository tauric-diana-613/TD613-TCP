PET formalizes the idea that modern societies function not as closed causal units but as <strong>open pressure ecologies</strong> in which structural pressures are continuously generated, absorbed, transformed, deferred, transferred, externalized, and propagated across actors, sectors, jurisdictions, and ecological substrates. PET distinguishes genuine internal capacity from imported capacity, deferred cost, and externalized burden, providing a structural grammar for interpreting stability in open systems. This directly strengthens SR&rsquo;s cross‑domain diagnostics, including <strong>Collapse Governance</strong>, <strong>Infrastructural Political Economy</strong>, <strong>Systems Propagation Science</strong>, and <strong>Collapse Economics</strong>.</p>\n<p>The manuscript contributes by formalizing <strong>seven core pressure mechanisms</strong>&mdash;generation, absorption, transformation, transfer, externalization, temporal displacement, and propagation&mdash;and by introducing a <strong>Pressure Balance representation</strong> that distinguishes absorption from deferral and transfer. This prevents the analytic error of interpreting local pressure reduction as resolution when pressure has merely changed form or location. PET&rsquo;s <strong>Pressure Traceability Principle</strong> becomes a foundational operator for SR&rsquo;s multi‑domain modeling and for national‑scale diagnostics in <strong>Planetary SR</strong>.</p>\n<p>The manuscript contributes by introducing a <strong>Dependency Topology</strong>, <strong>Substitution Capacity</strong>, <strong>Transfer Asymmetry</strong>, <strong>Externalization Ratio</strong>, and <strong>Temporal Displacement Load</strong>, each defined as measurable structural variables rather than metaphors. These constructs allow SR to analyze interdependence without assuming that dependency is inherently fragile or exploitative. PET&rsquo;s treatment of trade, supply chains, ecological externalization, and sovereign debt is grounded in established empirical literatures, including OECD evidence on supply‑chain concentration, IMF analysis of debt‑service burdens, and UNCTAD data on commodity dependence. These literatures support PET&rsquo;s mechanisms while leaving SR‑specific indices open to empirical testing.</p>\n<p>The manuscript contributes by reframing <strong>debt</strong> as a temporal pressure‑transfer instrument rather than a static fiscal variable. PET distinguishes productive displacement from erosive displacement and introduces a conceptual <strong>Temporal Displacement Ratio (TDR)</strong> to evaluate whether future capacity created by borrowing exceeds future pressure created by debt service. This provides a structural foundation for SR&rsquo;s treatment of fiscal space, household debt cycles, and long‑run capacity constraints.</p>\n<p>The manuscript contributes by integrating <strong>migration</strong>, <strong>ecological externalization</strong>, and <strong>digital‑infrastructure dependence</strong> into a unified pressure‑ecology grammar. PET does not treat people, ecosystems, or infrastructures as interchangeable units; instead, it analyzes how flows of labour, environmental burden, and technological dependency alter system capacities and demands across connected societies. This enables SR to analyze planetary interdependence without collapsing moral, political, or ecological distinctions.</p>\n<p>Finally, PET contributes by providing the <strong>planetary integration layer</strong> required for SR Galaxy. It shifts the unit of analysis from the country as an isolated container to the country as a <strong>node within a planetary pressure network</strong>, enabling SR to evaluate national stability, fragility, and resilience through pressure flows rather than isolated indicators. PET becomes the structural foundation for <strong>Planetary SR</strong>, global comparison architecture, and cross‑country diagnostics of pressure generation, buffering, transfer, and dependency.</p>",
