# THE AUTONOMOUS INSTITUTIONAL SUBSTRATE THEOREM (AIS): A Structural Law of Delegated Institutional Cognition, Machine-Layer Observability, and Computational Propagation

## Zenodo description

THE AUTONOMOUS INSTITUTIONAL SUBSTRATE THEOREM (AIS) 
 The Autonomous Institutional Substrate Theorem (AIS) establishes the second substrate in the SR Substrate Trilogy , positioned between CRI — Persistent Observation and MACS — Network‑Dependent Cognition . AIS formalizes the structural transition in which institutions begin delegating substantively consequential interpretive, classificatory, prioritization, monitoring, allocation, escalation, and decision functions to computational systems. Under AIS conditions, institutional behavior increasingly emerges from hybrid human‑machine processes rather than from human procedure alone.

 The manuscript contributes by distinguishing tool use from cognitive delegation . AIS demonstrates that computational systems can materially structure institutional perception and choice even when final authority remains human. A system that ranks cases, assigns risk scores, flags anomalies, triages queues, or recommends escalation can reorganize institutional cognition by shaping the option set, evidentiary framing, and priority order available to human decision makers. This distinction aligns AIS with contemporary public‑sector policy frameworks, including Canada’s Directive on Automated Decision‑Making, which recognizes consequential delegation even when humans retain formal signatures.

 The manuscript contributes by introducing Delegation Depth (A_D) as a measurable structural variable. AIS rejects binary automated/not‑automated classifications and instead defines levels of computational participation—from clerical assistance to autonomous decision execution. This enables empirical evaluation of how deeply computational systems participate in institutional judgment and provides a foundation for SR’s analysis of hybrid governance environments.

 The manuscript contributes by formalizing machine‑mediated drift (D_M) and coupling distortion (C_HM) . AIS does not assume that machine drift exceeds human drift; instead, it models institutional drift as a hybrid quantity:

 D_AIS = D_H + D_M + κC_HM.

 This formulation captures interactional distortion arising when human behavior adapts to machine outputs, when institutions redesign procedures around computational classifications, or when models retrain on data shaped by earlier machine‑mediated decisions. AIS therefore extends SR’s drift architecture into hybrid institutional systems.

 The manuscript contributes by defining machine‑layer observability (O_M) as a distinct governance variable. AIS shows that visibility does not collapse automatically under automation; it varies with explainability, traceability, grounding, and auditability. Delegation depth and observability interact: risk increases when consequential delegation rises while machine‑layer observability declines. This provides a structural foundation for SR’s analysis of opacity, accountability diffusion, and oversight asymmetry in automated decision systems.

 The manuscript contributes by introducing computational propagation , a mechanism through which automated systems can replicate classifications—and therefore errors or corrections—rapidly across large case volumes. AIS does not claim that computational propagation always increases harm; it demonstrates that automation changes the scale and velocity of institutional action. This extends SR’s Systems Propagation Science into institutional decision environments.

 The manuscript contributes by defining stable and unstable AIS regimes . Stability emerges when grounding, observability, accountability, and human review remain strong; instability emerges when delegation depth rises while oversight, reconciliation, or interpretability weaken. AIS therefore provides a diagnostic vocabulary for evaluating hybrid governance systems without assuming technological determinism.

 The manuscript contributes by integrating literatures on automation, algorithmic governance, algorithmic management, human‑machine interaction, and AI oversight into a unified substrate model. AIS does not claim priority over these literatures; its distinct contribution is to synthesize them with SR variables of drift, visibility, grounding discrepancy, propagation, and harm into a single delegated‑decision architecture .

 Finally, AIS contributes by transforming SR’s institutional diagnostic unit. Under AIS conditions, institutions retain formal authority but may lose epistemic centrality as computational systems increasingly structure perception, prioritization, and decision pathways. AIS therefore becomes the bridge between CRI’s observational substrate and MACS’s network‑dependent substrate , enabling SR to analyze how institutional judgment migrates into coupled computational processes and how governance must adapt to hybrid epistemic environments.
