## Zenodo description

THE CONTINUOUS REALITY INTEGRATION THEOREM (CRI) 
 The Continuous Reality Integration Theorem (CRI) establishes the first substrate in the SR Substrate Trilogy , preceding AIS — Delegated Decision and MACS — Network‑Dependent Cognition . CRI formalizes the structural transition from episodic observation toward persistent environmental computation , in which physical processes become continuously sensed, computationally represented, and algorithmically analyzed. The theorem does not claim perfect visibility or zero‑lag recognition; instead, it defines the conditions under which effective observability increases and recognition latency compresses toward a system‑specific lower bound.

 The manuscript contributes by distinguishing indexing from visibility , and visibility from ground truth . CRI demonstrates that dense measurement alone does not guarantee meaningful observability. Effective observability requires the joint contribution of indexing density, signal quality, grounding correspondence, and interpretive capacity. This multi‑component structure prevents overclaiming and aligns CRI with SR operators such as Ground‑Truth Discrepancy , Silo Exposure Bias , and Zombie Metrics , which can persist or intensify even in highly instrumented environments.

 The manuscript contributes by introducing effective observability (O_e) and a formal model of visibility‑lag compression (Δ_e) . CRI shows that persistent sensing can reduce sensing, transmission, and detection latency, but cannot eliminate interpretive or response delays. Recognition latency therefore approaches a residual floor rather than zero. This formulation is consistent with cyber‑physical systems research, sequential detection theory, and real‑time monitoring literature, grounding CRI in empirically observed precursor systems.

 The manuscript contributes by redefining drift observability . Under episodic observation, drift may accumulate undetected between measurement intervals. Under CRI conditions, a larger portion of drift becomes detectable during emergence. CRI does not claim full drift visibility; it predicts a shift in the probability and timing of detection. This provides a structural foundation for SR’s analysis of Drift , Slow Harm , and early‑warning diagnostics in instrumented environments.

 The manuscript contributes by separating observation from correction . CRI demonstrates that earlier recognition does not guarantee improved outcomes. Continuous observation can support adaptive stabilization, but it can also accelerate misclassification, optimization pressure, and coercive intervention. CRI therefore models harm as a contingent feedback process rather than as an automatically declining function of visibility. This distinction is essential for SR’s governance analysis and for understanding how persistent sensing interacts with institutional power.

 The manuscript contributes by defining three possible CRI trajectories : adaptive micro‑correction, continuous maladaptive correction, and ambient coercion. These trajectories show that persistent observability increases the frequency and speed of feedback, but the normative direction of that feedback depends on grounding, interpretation, governance capacity, and power distribution. CRI thus provides a structural grammar for evaluating both stabilizing and destabilizing effects of continuous sensing.

 The manuscript contributes by integrating precursor literatures—cyber‑physical systems, industrial monitoring, anomaly detection, smart‑city telemetry—into a unified substrate model. CRI does not claim priority over these literatures; its distinct contribution is to synthesize them with SR variables of drift, visibility, grounding discrepancy, and harm into a single temporal‑architecture theorem .

 Finally, CRI contributes by establishing the temporal foundation for the SR Substrate Trilogy. CRI reorganizes the time architecture of recognition; AIS reorganizes the locus of institutional judgment; MACS reorganizes epistemic behavior across coupled networks. CRI therefore becomes the entry point for SR’s future substrate architecture, enabling SR to analyze how persistent observation reshapes drift detection, correction dynamics, and governance in increasingly instrumented environments.
