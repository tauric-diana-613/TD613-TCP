# PLANETARY SR I — CANADA: Public-System Compensation, Unequal Exposure, and the Limits of Institutional Absorption

## Zenodo description

PLANETARY SR I — CANADA 
 Planetary SR I — Canada demonstrates how the SR architecture operates inside a high‑capacity welfare‑state system characterized by public‑system compensation . The paper contributes by showing that Canada’s continuity is not the product of uninterrupted durable capacity, but of extraordinary inputs —overtime, purchased staffing, household debt, unpaid care, intergovernmental transfers, and regional substitution—that bridge requirement–capacity gaps while preserving visible function.

 The manuscript contributes by applying the SR grammar—Pressure → Reflex → Drift → Collapse → Reorganization—to a federated, multi‑jurisdictional system. It shows that Canada’s pressures are absorbed through staffing flexibility, fiscal buffers, household margin, and jurisdictional overlap. This produces compensated continuity , not collapse. The paper demonstrates how Pressure Ecology distinguishes genuine absorption from transfer, externalization, and temporal displacement, revealing how stability can coexist with unequal exposure.

 The paper contributes by identifying surface–substrate divergence : stable headline indicators coexist with impaired access, distributional strain, and compensation dependence. Healthcare staffing, emergency‑department waits, household margin compression, food insecurity, and housing burden illustrate how visible continuity can mask underlying gaps. Negative controls—improved core housing need, stable hospital harm, strong PISA performance—prevent collapse inflation and demonstrate SR’s falsification discipline.

 The manuscript contributes by demonstrating cross‑domain propagation : housing pressure into care and mobility; health‑workforce gaps into overtime and agency substitution; jurisdictional fragmentation into navigation burden; climate exposure into insurance, municipal finance, and household recovery. These pathways are mechanism‑consistent, not universal claims, and follow SR‑CFIF’s requirement for exposure, lag, mechanism, and falsifiers.

 The paper contributes by showing how Canada’s transition from CRI → AIS → MACS is emerging through digital infrastructure, cloud concentration, platform mediation, and administrative automation. These transitions are treated as conditional substrate pathways rather than deterministic futures.

 Finally, the paper contributes by demonstrating SR’s empirical reproducibility: the SR grammar organizes Canadian evidence without collapsing into alarmism or erasing restoration. The conditional forecast—strain first in human and organizational buffers, not generalized collapse—shows SR’s ability to produce bounded, falsifiable predictions.
