# PLANETARY SR XIV — SOUTH KOREA Export-Technological Concentration, Household-Pressure Intensity, Ultra-Low Fertility, and Digital Coordination

## Zenodo description

PLANETARY SR XIV — SOUTH KOREA 
 Description 
 Planetary SR XIV — South Korea provides a structural diagnostic of South Korea as a coupled institutional–population system, using evidence from 2000–2025 with 2026 verification. The paper identifies Korea’s continuity architecture as export‑technological concentration, household‑pressure intensity, ultra‑low fertility, and digital coordination . Korea’s macro profile— “GDP growth around 2.2% and unemployment around 3.0% for 2025” —coexists with structural pressures including “extremely low fertility, one of the strongest long‑lag constraints in the entire 30‑country series” and severe housing and education burdens concentrated in the Seoul capital region. The study distinguishes universal insurance from effective access, showing how household debt, youth competition, labour dualism, ageing, and metropolitan concentration shape continuity beneath high technological and administrative capacity. Korea’s globally concentrated industrial champions and advanced digital infrastructure provide durable capacity, while demographic contraction and household‑pressure intensity remain central conversion tests.

 Contribution 
 This paper expands the Planetary SR comparative series by introducing a new continuity topology distinct from Canada, the United States, Mexico, Germany, France, the United Kingdom, Italy, Spain, Sweden, Norway, Poland, and Japan. Korea’s configuration strengthens SR fields such as Planetary Pressure Ecology , Institutional Reflex Science , Planetary Drift Mechanics , and Systems Propagation Science by demonstrating how export‑technological concentration, ultra‑low fertility, household leverage, and digital coordination interact across 17 metropolitan/provincial governments and Seoul/non‑Seoul labour and housing markets. The document provides high‑resolution evidence for SR’s frozen hypotheses, including compensated continuity, unequal insulation, surface–substrate divergence, and reorganization. It also contributes to the ATLAS architecture by showing how extreme technological capacity can coexist with demographic reproduction constraints generated partly by the same housing, work, and competition systems that support economic performance. As the paper states, Korea’s mechanism is empirically distinguishable through its blend of “technology‑export concentration and high administrative capacity under very low fertility, household/housing pressure and rapid ageing,” thereby adding a new structural configuration to the planetary series.
