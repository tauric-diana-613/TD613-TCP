# ATLAS III SOCIETY AS A PLANETARY PRESSURE ECOLOGY: Value Transfer, Burden Transfer, Absorption, and Restoration Across Borders

## Zenodo description

ATLAS III — Society as a Planetary Pressure Ecology 
 Description 
 ATLAS III defines the world as a planetary pressure ecology : a system where visible stability is maintained not by resolving pressure, but by distributing, absorbing, and externalizing it across households, institutions, infrastructures, ecosystems, and countries. As the manuscript states, “Visible stability can be maintained by distributing, absorbing, and externalizing pressure across households, institutions, infrastructures, ecosystems, and countries.” 

 The paper establishes that international exchange becomes asymmetric pressure transfer when benefits accumulate in one jurisdiction while ecological, labour, fiscal, or institutional burdens accumulate elsewhere. It rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof of lock‑in; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations.

 ATLAS III introduces the Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis , a conditional sequence linking domestic value retention (R), burdens (B), substitution capacity (S), external dependence (D), bargaining agency (A), and renewed externalization (X). It specifies necessary distinctions, migration‑responsibility rules, a dynamic formal pathway, a falsifiable hypothesis family, and a multi‑layer measurement architecture using TiVA, UNCTAD, UNIDO, WDI, and WTO sources.

 The manuscript positions societies as pressure ecologies , where stability emerges from how pressure is transferred, not from whether pressure disappears.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS III provides the planetary‑scale pressure layer required for the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes Society as a Pressure Ecology by showing how international value chains, ecological depletion, labour compensation, fiscal strain, and migration form a coupled system of pressure redistribution.

 It strengthens the Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis by adding cross‑border evidence gates: return‑path exposure, ecological liabilities, care‑chain dependence, and consumption‑side footprints. It deepens Infrastructural Political Economy by demonstrating how external extraction interacts with domestic substitution capacity and bargaining agency.

 The migration‑responsibility rule introduces a structural, non‑moral framework for evaluating how external advantage and domestic erosion interact. The measurement architecture provides the empirical substrate for ATLAS IV and the cross‑country falsification protocol for ATLAS I .

 Ultimately, ATLAS III contributes the planetary pressure‑transfer grammar needed to evaluate whether societies retain gains, absorb burdens fairly, and possess credible restoration capacity.
