# ATLAS IV THE DEVELOPMENTAL LOCK-IN HYPOTHESIS: Unequal Value Retention and the Reproduction of International Dependency

## Zenodo description

ATLAS IV — The Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis 
 Description 
 ATLAS IV formalizes the Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis , the core causal engine of the ATLAS universe. It defines developmental lock‑in as a reproduced causal sequence in which external extraction or policy pressure reduces domestic value retention (R), increases burdens (B), weakens substitution capacity (S), raises external dependence (D), reduces bargaining agency (A), and thereby renews externalization (X). As the manuscript states, “Developmental lock‑in exists when external extraction or policy pressure reduces domestic value retention, burdens local recovery, weakens substitution, increases dependence, reduces bargaining capacity, and thereby renews externalization.” 

 The paper rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations. It introduces necessary distinctions (commodity dependence vs. lock‑in, domestic value added vs. retention, external dependence vs. constraint) and extends the migration‑responsibility rule by showing how external advantage and domestic erosion can interact to shape mobility.

 ATLAS IV specifies the dynamic pathway using R, B, S, D, A, and X; defines a falsifiable hypothesis family (H1–H6); and establishes a rigorous measurement architecture using TiVA, UNCTAD, UNIDO, WDI, and WTO sources. It sets strict empirical design rules, case‑selection criteria, falsification gates, restoration tests, and reproducibility protocols. The manuscript positions developmental lock‑in as a conditional, testable, cross‑country mechanism , not a deterministic label or moral judgment.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS IV provides the mechanism core of the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes Developmental Lock‑In by turning SR’s pressure‑ecology insights into a falsifiable causal chain that can be tested across countries, sectors, and time. It deepens Society as a Pressure Ecology by showing how domestic retention, burdens, substitution, and bargaining interact with global value‑chain structures.

 The hypothesis family (H1–H6) supplies the adjudication architecture needed to distinguish lock‑in from ordinary specialization, temporary commodity cycles, or voluntary global‑value‑chain participation. The measurement architecture provides the empirical substrate for ATLAS III and the cross‑country falsification protocol for ATLAS I .

 ATLAS IV also strengthens SR’s Infrastructural Political Economy by demonstrating how external extraction interacts with domestic substitution capacity and bargaining agency. The restoration section introduces a structural, non‑ideological framework for evaluating whether interventions increase retained capability, reduce burdens, and build credible substitution.

 Ultimately, ATLAS IV contributes the causal grammar required to evaluate whether societies can escape dependency, retain gains, and build durable restoration capacity.
