# ATLAS VI STATE AGENCY UNDER EXTERNAL DEPENDENCY: Infrastructure, Finance, Technology, and Executable Sovereignty

## Zenodo description

ATLAS VI — State Agency Under External Dependency 
 Description 
 ATLAS VI formalizes the agency layer of the ATLAS universe: the diagnostic architecture that distinguishes formal authority from executable agency . As the manuscript states, “Formal authority does not guarantee executable agency. Dependence constrains agency when substitution is low, private or foreign structural power is high, and regulatory leverage cannot be implemented.” 

 The paper rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations. It positions state agency as a conditional capability , shaped by infrastructure dependency, external financing, technology control, supplier concentration, switching costs, and regulatory enforceability.

 ATLAS VI extends the formal pathway (R → B → S → D → A → X) by showing how low substitution capacity (S) and high external dependence (D) reduce executable bargaining and policy agency (A). It introduces a measurement architecture focused on infrastructure dependency, substitution capacity, regulatory leverage, supplier concentration, external financing, currency exposure, and implementation gaps.

 The manuscript establishes that agency loss is not a moral failure or institutional flaw; it is a structural consequence of dependency architectures that limit a government’s ability to execute policy, regulate essential systems, negotiate terms, or protect people who move across borders.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS VI provides the state‑agency foundation of the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes Executable Agency by turning SR’s pressure‑ecology insights into a measurable, falsifiable account of how external dependency constrains state action.

 It deepens Developmental Lock‑In by showing how low retention and high burdens weaken substitution capacity, raise external dependence, and reduce the state’s ability to execute policy. It strengthens Infrastructural Political Economy by demonstrating how infrastructure ownership, technology control, logistics dependency, and external finance shape the limits of regulatory leverage.

 The migration‑agency audit expands ATLAS’s cross‑border incidence analysis by tracing how employer dependence, recruiter power, remittance reliance, credential recognition, and diaspora policy shape executable migration agency.

 ATLAS VI also provides the empirical substrate for evaluating restoration through redundant public capacity, interoperability, open standards, procurement‑based capability formation, and regional substitution arrangements.

 Ultimately, ATLAS VI contributes the agency grammar required to evaluate whether states can retain gains, govern essential systems, absorb shocks fairly, and escape coercive or concentrated dependency.
