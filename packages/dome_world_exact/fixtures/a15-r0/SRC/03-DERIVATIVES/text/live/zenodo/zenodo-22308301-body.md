# ATLAS VII HOUSEHOLDS AS THE LAST ABSORBER: Debt, Unpaid Care, Administrative Burden, and Hidden System Continuity

## Zenodo description

ATLAS VII — Households as the Last Absorber 
 Description 
 ATLAS VII formalizes the household‑absorption layer of the ATLAS universe: the diagnostic architecture showing how institutions and markets preserve surface continuity by shifting unresolved costs into households . As the manuscript states, “Institutions and markets can preserve surface continuity by shifting unresolved costs into household money, time, debt, unpaid care, and self-navigation.” 

 The paper rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations. It positions households as the final buffer in the pressure ecology — the place where unresolved institutional, fiscal, ecological, and administrative burdens accumulate before system failure becomes visible.

 ATLAS VII extends the formal pathway (R → B → S → D → A → X) by showing how low retention (R) and rising burdens (B) weaken substitution capacity (S), increase dependence (D), reduce agency (A), and reproduce externalization (X). It introduces a measurement architecture focused on residual income, unsecured debt, unpaid care hours, administrative abandonment, private substitution spending, and subgroup distribution by income, gender, migration status, disability, and region.

 The manuscript establishes that household burden is not a moral failure or personal weakness; it is a structural consequence of systems that rely on private absorption to maintain visible stability.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS VII provides the household‑buffer foundation of the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes Household Absorption by turning SR’s pressure‑ecology insights into a measurable, falsifiable account of how unresolved costs are transferred into private time, money, care, and debt.

 It deepens Society as a Pressure Ecology by demonstrating how households act as the final absorber before institutional deterioration becomes visible. It strengthens Developmental Lock‑In by showing how household depletion reduces recovery capacity, weakens substitution, and increases dependence on external earnings structures.

 The migration‑household audit expands ATLAS’s cross‑border incidence analysis by tracing how family separation, unpaid transnational care, recruitment debt, remittance dependence, credential loss, and legal insecurity interact with gains in income, safety, education, and autonomy. As the manuscript states, “A household benefit does not by itself prove an equitable system, and hardship does not erase migrant agency.” 

 ATLAS VII also provides the empirical substrate for evaluating restoration through burden reduction, universal substitution capacity, care infrastructure, income adequacy, and debt prevention through cost and wage reform.

 Ultimately, ATLAS VII contributes the absorption grammar required to evaluate whether societies can retain gains, protect households, and escape coercive or concentrated dependency.
