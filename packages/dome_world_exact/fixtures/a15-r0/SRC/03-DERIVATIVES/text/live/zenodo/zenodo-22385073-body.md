# ATLAS XIII ECOLOGICAL BURDEN AND EXTRACTIVE VALUE CHAINS: Who Retains the Gain and Who Finances Restoration

## Zenodo description

ATLAS XIII — Ecological Burden and Extractive Value Chains 
 Description 
 ATLAS XIII formalizes the ecological‑burden layer of the ATLAS universe: the diagnostic architecture showing how exports that appear developmentally positive can conceal depletion, pollution, displacement, and remediation liabilities in the producing territory. As the manuscript states, “Net retained gain must subtract credible ecological and social restoration costs.” 

 The paper rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations. It positions ecological burden not as a moral indictment but as a structural accounting problem : who retains the gain, who bears the cost, and who finances restoration.

 ATLAS XIII extends the formal pathway (R → B → S → D → A → X) by showing how uncompensated ecological burdens reduce retained value (R), increase long‑term liabilities (B), weaken substitution capacity (S), raise external dependence (D), and lower bargaining agency (A). It introduces a measurement architecture focused on resource rents, adjusted net savings, land/water/biodiversity footprints, remediation liabilities, displacement, health burden, revenue allocation, intergenerational funds, and post‑extraction regional recovery.

 The manuscript establishes that extraction is not inherently harmful; it becomes harmful when restoration costs exceed retained gains and when producing regions are left with liabilities they cannot remediate.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS XIII provides the ecological‑restoration foundation of the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes Ecological Burden by turning SR’s pressure‑ecology insights into a measurable, falsifiable account of how extractive value chains distribute gains and costs across territories.

 It deepens Developmental Lock‑In by showing how uncompensated depletion and pollution weaken recovery capacity, reduce retained value, and reproduce dependency. It strengthens Society as a Pressure Ecology by demonstrating how ecological burden acts as a long‑tail transmission channel for externalization.

 The migration‑ecology audit expands ATLAS’s cross‑border incidence analysis by tracing how disasters, slow‑onset livelihood loss, water stress, heat, crop decline, and sea‑level rise shape mobility. As the manuscript states, “Those contributing least to climate change may face the highest mobility pressure, but each corridor still requires pathway evidence.” 

 ATLAS XIII also provides the empirical substrate for evaluating restoration through full‑cost bonds, polluter liability, community revenue participation, ecological thresholds, no‑go rules, and diversification before depletion.

 Ultimately, ATLAS XIII contributes the ecological‑accounting grammar required to evaluate whether societies can retain gains, remediate damage, and escape coercive or concentrated dependency.
