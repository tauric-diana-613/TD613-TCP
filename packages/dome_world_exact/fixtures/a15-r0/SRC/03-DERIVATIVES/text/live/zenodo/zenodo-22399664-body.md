# ATLAS XVII ATLAS OF EUROPE: Integrated Markets, Uneven Value Capture, and the Political Economy of Dependency

## Zenodo description

ATLAS XVII — Atlas of Europe 
 Description 
 ATLAS XVII formalizes the European‑integration layer of the ATLAS universe: a diagnostic of how Europe’s integrated markets can simultaneously reduce some dependencies while reproducing centre–periphery differences in ownership, technology, fiscal space, energy, and labour mobility. As the manuscript states, “European integration can reduce some dependencies while reproducing centre–periphery differences in ownership, technology, fiscal space, energy, and labour mobility.” 

 The paper rejects three shortcuts: commodity dependence is exposure, not proof; domestic value‑added does not guarantee broad retention; and low income or weak institutions cannot be used as circular explanations. It positions Europe not as East vs. West but as a pathway system , where upgrading, dependency, and restoration differ by sector, institutional capacity, and historical trajectory.

 ATLAS XVII extends the formal pathway (R → B → S → D → A → X) by showing how uneven retention (R), uncompensated burdens (B), and limited substitution capacity (S) can raise external dependence (D) and narrow bargaining agency (A) even inside an advanced integrated market. It introduces a measurement architecture focused on intra‑European TiVA, firm ownership, headquarters and R&D location, labour mobility, wage convergence, energy import dependence, fiscal capacity, and regional supplier upgrading.

 The manuscript establishes that European integration is not automatically convergence; convergence requires retention, capability formation, and burden alignment across member states.

 How it Contributes 
 ATLAS XVII provides the continental‑political‑economy foundation of the ATLAS universe. It operationalizes European Dependency Pathways by turning SR’s pressure‑ecology insights into a measurable, falsifiable account of how Europe’s integrated markets distribute gains, burdens, and agency.

 It deepens Developmental Lock‑In by showing how ownership geography, R&D concentration, energy dependence, and fiscal asymmetry reproduce dependency even within high‑income regions. It strengthens Society as a Pressure Ecology by demonstrating how labour, ecological, fiscal, and energy burdens travel across borders and institutional layers.

 The migration audit expands ATLAS’s cross‑border incidence analysis by tracing Europe as origin, transit, destination, return, colonial, and refugee contexts simultaneously. As the manuscript states, “No continent-wide causal attribution is permitted.” 

 ATLAS XVII also provides the empirical substrate for evaluating restoration through cohesion tied to productive capability, energy and digital interoperability, regional innovation networks, supplier upgrading, labour standards, and tax‑base protection.

 Ultimately, ATLAS XVII contributes the European‑agency grammar required to evaluate whether European societies can retain gains, govern essential systems, and escape coercive or concentrated dependency.
