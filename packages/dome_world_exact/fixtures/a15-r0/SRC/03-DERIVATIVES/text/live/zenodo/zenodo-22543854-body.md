# ATLAS XXII ATLAS OF AFRICA: Extraction, Infrastructure, Debt, and the Struggle for Domestic Value Retention

## Zenodo description

ATLAS XXII — ATLAS OF AFRICA 
 Extraction, Infrastructure, Debt, and the Struggle for Domestic Value Retention 

 Description 
 ATLAS XXII — ATLAS OF AFRICA evaluates a bounded and falsifiable proposition: many African economies provide the most direct test of the Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis, but the continent cannot be treated as a single case. Sector, country, ownership, conflict, geography, and institutional variation are analytically decisive. The manuscript does not treat countries as essences, does not equate commodity dependence or foreign participation with extraction, and does not infer constraint from specialization alone. Instead, it specifies the causal, temporal, and distributional conditions under which external pressure, low domestic retention, uncompensated labour and ecological burdens, weakened substitution, and constrained agency can reproduce asymmetric dependence.

 The manuscript formalizes the SR developmental pathway using five core variables: domestically retained and broadly usable value (R); unremediated labour, ecological, fiscal, and institutional burdens (B); substitution and recovery capacity (S); dependence on external markets, finance, technology, or infrastructure (D); and executable bargaining and policy agency (A). These variables are linked to renewed externalization (X) through an explicit dynamic representation. ATLAS XXII establishes exclusion rules against overclaiming, defines rival mechanisms for each hypothesis, and sets evidence grades (E0–E6) that govern permissible inference.

 Measurement architecture is fully specified. The manuscript uses OECD TiVA to distinguish gross trade from value‑added origin; UNIDO industrial statistics to track productive structure; UNCTAD indicators to identify commodity dependence and diversification constraints; and World Bank data to measure structural change. Variables include commodity dependence and export concentration, local content and supplier depth, debt‑linked infrastructure terms, ownership and profit allocation, energy and logistics access, and labour, community, and ecological incidence. Every variable must carry source provenance, definition, unit, coverage, transformation code, uncertainty, and missingness rules. The empirical design compares Nigeria, South Africa, and a stratified set of mineral, agricultural, energy, and diversified economies, with country evidence overriding continental generalization. ATLAS XXII is time‑bounded, mechanism‑driven, and explicitly revisable. It is designed for public‑interest analysis, resilience evaluation, and restoration‑oriented policy assessment.

 How It Contributes 
 1. Cross‑country causal architecture 
ATLAS XXII converts SR’s country‑level diagnostics into a comparable, mechanism‑based analytical system for Africa. It supplies explicit variables, dynamic functions, rival explanations, falsification gates, and evidence grades, enabling SR to move from narrative diagnostics to reproducible comparative science. This establishes the methodological backbone for regional and planetary inference.

 2. Formalization of the Developmental Lock‑In Hypothesis in extractive and infrastructure‑dependent contexts 
The manuscript provides a dynamic, testable representation of developmental lock‑in where extraction, infrastructure dependency, debt arrangements, and external ownership interact. It specifies required temporal order, mediation, and rival mechanisms, preventing deterministic or moralized interpretations. Lock‑in becomes a conditional, falsifiable sequence rather than a label or structural destiny.

 3. Distributional, ecological, and community integration 
ATLAS XXII embeds labour capture, ecological depletion, community burden, and subnational inequality directly into causal evaluation. Rising domestic value added or infrastructure expansion cannot establish restoration unless wages, tax capacity, ecological remediation, community outcomes, and local capability improve. This prevents misclassification of extraction‑led growth as structural recovery and ensures that capability formation, burden remediation, and fair retention remain central.

 4. Measurement and reproducibility protocol 
The manuscript establishes SR’s measurement hygiene: source provenance, transformation code, uncertainty, sensitivity checks, explicit coverage windows, and non‑interchangeability of indicators. It defines reproducibility rules—data manifests, code publication, preregistration, holdout cases, and correction visibility—ensuring that SR claims remain transparent, auditable, and scientifically durable.

 5. Restoration architecture 
ATLAS XXII defines restoration as regional processing and power markets, contract and ownership transparency, local supplier and engineering ecosystems, and revenue, community, and ecological accountability. It provides policy evaluation gates that prevent superficial upgrading narratives and ensure interventions are assessed on capability formation, burden remediation, and real refusal capacity. This anchors SR’s ethical purpose: increasing societies’ ability to retain gains, govern essential systems, absorb shocks fairly, and choose their developmental path.
