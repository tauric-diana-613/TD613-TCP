# Global Logistics & Standards Architecture: An empirical applied branch for diagnosing routes, standards, certification gates and market-access continuity

## Zenodo description

GLSA — Global Logistics & Standards Architecture 
 An Applied SR Branch for Diagnosing Routes, Standards, Certification Gates, and Market‑Access Continuity 

 Description 
 Global Logistics & Standards Architecture (GLSA) evaluates a bounded and falsifiable proposition: nominal participation in trade or production does not guarantee effective market access when logistics routes are concentrated, switching is costly, standards are proprietary, certification is delayed, or redundancy is weak. The manuscript does not treat logistics as a neutral technical layer or standards as mere compliance requirements. Instead, it specifies the causal, temporal, and institutional conditions under which physical movement, recognition, and certification determine whether producers can reach buyers, maintain continuity, and recover from disruption.

 GLSA operationalizes mechanisms imported from Infrastructural Political Economy , Systems Propagation Science , and Infrastructure Dependency . It applies dependency, substitution, regulatory leverage, structural power, transmission, delay, cascade, concentration, and redundancy to logistics networks, standards regimes, certification gates, transport corridors, platforms, and market‑access rules. GLSA remains an applied branch because its mechanisms are explained by parent SR fields; its contribution is an integrated diagnostic and intervention protocol.

 The measurement architecture combines route concentration, switching cost, standards dependence, certification delay, provider concentration, redundancy, and market‑access elasticity. These variables are directional diagnostics rather than universal indices. GLSA uses shipment‑level connectivity, speed, and reliability data from the World Bank LPI 2.0, UNCTAD maritime and liner‑shipping statistics, and WTO trade‑facilitation datasets to test whether concentration, proprietary standards, and certification bottlenecks predict delay, exclusion, and recovery time beyond baseline models.

 GLSA defines effective access as realized continuity through viable routes, recognized standards, timely certification, interoperable systems, and credible substitutes. It distinguishes formal eligibility from realized entry, showing how compliance cost, credential exclusion, and proprietary recognition can block access even when producers meet nominal requirements. The branch evaluates recovery through redundancy: systems with alternate routes, mutual recognition, interoperable standards, and diversified providers restore continuity faster than concentrated or proprietary systems.

 GLSA is an empirically grounded applied branch and ATLAS domain. It does not identify exploitable chokepoints or provide disruption guidance. Its purpose is public‑interest diagnosis, resilience evaluation, and intervention testing.

 How It Contributes 
 1. A unified diagnostic of physical and institutional access 
GLSA integrates logistics routes and standards regimes into a single continuity architecture. It shows that market access depends not only on producing a good but on moving it through viable corridors, satisfying technical and legal standards, obtaining recognition, and maintaining interoperable information and certification systems. This joint diagnosis is absent from route‑only or standards‑only models.

 2. Applied operationalization of SR parent fields 
The branch translates mechanisms from Infrastructural Political Economy , Systems Propagation Science , and Infrastructure Dependency into measurable logistics and standards variables. It applies dependency, substitution, structural power, transmission, delay, and redundancy to real‑world corridors, certification gates, and recognition systems. This makes SR’s theoretical architecture operational for empirical testing and policy evaluation.

 3. Incremental prediction over route‑only and trade‑cost models 
GLSA’s distinct claim is empirical: the coupled model must outperform route‑only, trade‑cost, and income‑based baselines in predicting delay, exclusion, substitution cost, and recovery. It tests whether concentration × switching cost × standards dependence × certification delay explain fragility beyond conventional logistics indicators.

 4. A falsifiable continuity and recovery mechanism 
The branch predicts that disruption costs rise with concentration and low substitution; that formal access fails when compliance cost or credential exclusion binds; and that redundancy and interoperability accelerate recovery. Negative and reversal cases—such as concentrated corridors that remain reliable due to immediate substitution or mutual recognition reducing delay without new capacity—narrow or refine the mechanism.

 5. A resilience‑oriented intervention protocol 
GLSA provides a structured evaluation of route diversification, standards interoperability, certification capacity, customs coordination, and continuity for essential goods. It distinguishes interventions that restore effective access from those that merely increase nominal throughput. This supports public‑interest resilience planning without identifying exploitable chokepoints.

 6. Applied‑science discipline and SR boundary integrity 
The branch maintains strict applied‑science boundaries: preregistered tests, shipment‑level delay distributions, matched exposed/unexposed corridors, transparent normalization and uncertainty, and prohibition against interpreting faster trade as distributionally neutral. GLSA remains an applied branch because its mechanisms are inherited from parent fields; its value lies in integration, diagnosis, and intervention testing rather than autonomous field status.
