# PANDEMIC SYSTEMS SCIENCE THE EMPIRICAL CONSTRUCTION: How Biological Hazards Become Coupled Societal System States

## Zenodo description

Pandemic Systems Science (PSS) 
 The Empirical Construction of How Biological Hazards Become Coupled Societal System States 

 Description 
 Pandemic Systems Science (PSS) evaluates a bounded and falsifiable proposition: an infectious biological hazard becomes a societal systems object only when it produces measurable cross‑domain state changes through registered detection, recognition, coupling, substitution, buffering, and restoration mechanisms—and when those joint mechanisms explain or predict outcomes beyond epidemiological and mature component‑field baselines. The manuscript does not treat pandemics as governance failures, nor biological hazards as deterministic societal events. Instead, it specifies the causal, temporal, and distributional conditions under which biological burden is converted into institutional, infrastructural, economic, educational, informational, and household burden.

 PSS formalizes the irreducible mechanism of cross‑domain compensatory load transfer : the time‑indexed movement of unmet requirements from formal systems into workers, households, local bodies, firms, deferred services, or informal networks. It distinguishes biological hazard from system conversion, detection from recognition, formal capacity from executable capacity, propagation from coupling, continuity from compensation, and reopening from restoration. The field integrates Systems Propagation Science , Planetary Pressure Ecology , Household Absorption Science , World Infrastructure Science , and Planetary Restoration Governance while assigning their component mechanisms to their respective domains.

 Empirical anchors include WHO excess‑mortality estimates, WHO essential‑service disruption surveys, Oxford policy‑timing datasets, UNESCO education‑closure records, ILO labour‑loss estimates, and comparative resilience analyses. These datasets demonstrate cross‑domain conversion but do not establish an autonomous field. PSS survives only if interaction‑aware models—incorporating detection latency, recognition lag, formal‑to‑executable capacity gaps, cross‑system bridges, compensatory transfers, visibility lags, and restoration gates—add stable explanatory or predictive value beyond epidemiology, preparedness, health‑system resilience, and mature SR component fields.

 PSS is a candidate SR field , empirically active but not yet validated. Full autonomy requires passing H8A–H8D: demonstrating a stable empirical remainder beyond established pandemic, resilience, public‑health, and SR component research.

 How It Contributes 
 1. A distinct hazard‑to‑system conversion object 
PSS establishes pandemic system conversion as an empirical object: the transformation of biological hazard into cross‑domain system states. It distinguishes biological burden from societal burden and provides a measurable architecture for how unmet requirements move across systems.

 2. A formal mechanism for compensatory load transfer 
The field supplies a dated, directional model of how unmet requirements in health, logistics, education, labour, governance, or information systems are transferred into households, workers, local bodies, firms, debt, or deferred services. This mechanism is absent from epidemiology, preparedness, and health‑system resilience.

 3. Incremental prediction over epidemiological and preparedness baselines 
PSS’s distinct claim is empirical: interaction‑aware models must outperform epidemiological baselines, preparedness indices, health‑system resilience frameworks, and SR component fields in predicting mortality, service disruption, labour loss, education interruption, household burden, and restoration. This establishes a falsifiable autonomy test.

 4. A measurable formal‑to‑executable capacity gap 
PSS distinguishes documented preparedness from usable response. It measures the gap between formal capacity (plans, authority, stockpiles, workforce) and executable capacity (mobilizable at required speed, scale, geography, and population). This gap is a candidate SR remainder not fully represented in benchmark frameworks.

 5. A cross‑domain propagation and coupling architecture 
The field specifies dated bridges linking biological hazard to downstream domains: clinical overload → deferred care; school closure → caregiver burden; workplace exposure → labour loss; border restrictions → logistics disruption; fiscal support → household margin. It requires temporal order and rival separation rather than inferring cascade from co‑movement.

 6. A visibility‑lag mechanism grounded in compensatory burden 
PSS formalizes visibility lag as the interval between deterioration in compensatory burden and later recognition in conventional indicators. It shows how surface continuity can mask rising household, worker, institutional, or deferred‑care burden.

 7. A restoration gate that includes burden decline 
PSS defines restoration as recovery of function and decline in extraordinary compensatory burden, backlog, and access gaps—plus renewal of future response capacity. Reopening without burden decline is continuity, not restoration.

 8. A rigorous non‑duplication boundary 
The field establishes strict boundaries: PSS does not replace epidemiology, public health, preparedness, health‑system resilience, or SR planetary fields. It enters only when hazard‑specific conversion and compensatory‑transfer mechanisms add explanatory or predictive value beyond those baselines. Failure requires demotion to a research programme.
