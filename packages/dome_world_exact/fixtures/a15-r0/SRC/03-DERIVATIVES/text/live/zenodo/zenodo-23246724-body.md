# SR Street Epistemology: Structural Knowledge from Lived Exposure, Consequence Proximity, and Institutional Blind Spots

## Zenodo description

Institutions do not observe reality from nowhere. They observe through instruments, categories, reporting systems, professional jurisdictions, administrative thresholds, databases, surveys, metrics, models, and authorized forms of evidence. Individuals and communities likewise do not observe reality from nowhere. They encounter systems from specific positions defined by exposure, consequence, recurrence, adaptation, dependence, and proximity to failure.

 This paper defines SR Street Epistemology (SR-SE) as the study of knowledge generated through repeated situated exposure to structural conditions, particularly where those who absorb the consequences of a system occupy observational positions not adequately represented by the system's formal instruments.

 “Street” does not refer literally to a road, neighbourhood, social class, or opposition to expertise. It denotes an epistemic position of consequence proximity: the position of actors who repeatedly encounter, absorb, navigate, compensate for, adapt to, or recover from system behaviour.

 SR-SE does not claim that lived experience is intrinsically true, that marginalized actors possess automatic epistemic superiority, that institutions are systematically wrong, or that local knowledge should replace scientific inquiry. Instead, it proposes that different structural positions generate different observational access.

 The framework distinguishes exposure-derived signal from interpretation, belief, causal attribution, and verified knowledge. It introduces Consequence Proximity, Exposure Recurrence, Compensatory Participation, Institutional Visibility Distance, Street-Institution Divergence, Distributed Anomaly Convergence, Translation Loss, Recognition Lag, and the Street-to-Science Validation Pipeline. It proposes falsifiable hypotheses and empirical designs for determining when lived structural knowledge detects conditions earlier or differently than institutional measurement, when institutional instruments outperform situated observers, and when combining the two produces superior diagnosis.

 The central proposition is:

 Different positions in a system create different access to evidence.

 The strongest SR-SE claim is therefore not: the street knows better than the institution.

 It is:

 The street and the institution may know different parts of the same system.

 Scientific inquiry improves when that difference becomes measurable.
