# Democratic Surfaces and Institutional Substrates: Why Electoral Choice Alone Cannot Guarantee Structural Repair

## Zenodo description

Democratic governance authorizes public action through elections, representation, legislation, and policy. Its material outcomes depend on the institutional and infrastructural arrangements through which that action is executed. This paper develops a SignalRupture account of the relationship between democratic decision surfaces and execution substrates. The central mechanism is the authorization-to-execution gap: a public commitment can remain formally valid while its delivery is blocked by inaccessible resources, incompatible interfaces, delayed information, unresolved authority dependencies, or inadequate time for correction. The analysis integrates implementation research, infrastructural power, policy inheritance, path dependence, policy drift, polycentric governance, and administrative burden within a function-bound decision-to-execution chain. Official audits of water regulation in England and Wales, Canada’s Phoenix pay system, and the Flint water crisis establish bounded examples of failures in investment, readiness, oversight, and correction. Evidence of improved water service and positive long-run effects of democratization on growth defines the account’s boundary conditions. Electoral change can produce structural repair when it changes the relevant operating conditions; changing leadership alone does not establish that such repair has occurred. Three falsifiable hypotheses connect prospectively measured execution constraints, documented constraint removal, and verified public outcomes. The framework distinguishes the political source of a decision, the trigger of a disturbance, the substrate’s vulnerability, and the route of repair. Its empirical validity depends on explanatory and predictive performance beyond established implementation models.
