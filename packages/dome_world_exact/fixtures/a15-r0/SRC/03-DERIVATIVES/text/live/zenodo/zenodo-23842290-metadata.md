    "description": "<p>DESCRIPTION</p>\n<p>The Vanishing First Rung examines a neglected consequence of artificial intelligence adoption: the potential erosion of the labour‑market infrastructure that reproduces experienced human capability. Rather than focusing primarily on unemployment, wages, or task displacement, the paper studies how entry‑level work functions as a capability‑production system through which people acquire supervised experience, judgment, tacit knowledge, credibility, networks, and professional competence. It introduces Labour‑Market Capability Reproduction (LMCR) as the central object of analysis and investigates whether productivity gains can coexist with weakening pathways into experienced work.</p>\n<p>The manuscript develops three bounded candidate mechanisms:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>Experience Acquisition Lock‑In (EALI)</li>\n<li>Capability‑Reproduction Erosion (CRE)</li>\n<li>Care‑Automation Asymmetry (CAA)</li>\n</ul>\n<p>It integrates these mechanisms within the Infrastructural Matrix, distinguishes augmentation from substitution, formal opportunity from executable access, and constraint from coercion, and provides formal models, evidence registries, prospective hypotheses, empirical research designs, restoration pathways, and falsification rules. The paper argues that the primary question is not whether AI eliminates jobs, but whether societies continue to reproduce the human capability required for future economic participation and independence.</p>\n<p>HOW IT CONTRIBUTES TO SR</p>\n<p>1. It introduces Labour‑Market Capability Reproduction as a new SR object</p>\n<p>The paper shifts analysis away from immediate employment outcomes and toward the long‑term reproduction of experienced human capability. It asks whether a labour market can maintain productivity while weakening the infrastructure that converts inexperienced workers into competent professionals.</p>\n<p>2. It identifies Experience Acquisition Lock‑In</p>\n<p>The manuscript formalizes a condition in which employers continue to require demonstrated experience while the routes through which experience is acquired become progressively narrower. This creates a self‑reinforcing barrier to labour‑market entry and progression.</p>\n<p>3. It introduces Capability‑Reproduction Erosion</p>\n<p>The paper demonstrates that jobs produce two outputs simultaneously:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>present output,</li>\n<li>future capability.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Capability‑Reproduction Erosion occurs when organizations optimize current productivity while weakening the future supply of experienced workers.</p>\n<p>4. It introduces Care‑Automation Asymmetry</p>\n<p>The manuscript identifies a mechanism through which automation can transform labour‑market access by disproportionately compressing occupations compatible with existing care responsibilities while the underlying care burden remains unchanged.</p>\n<p>5. It expands the SR Infrastructural Matrix into labour systems</p>\n<p>The paper operationalizes:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>enabling relations,</li>\n<li>allocation relations,</li>\n<li>authorization relations,</li>\n<li>representation relations,</li>\n<li>feedback relations</li>\n</ul>\n<p>inside labour‑market entry systems, demonstrating how capability reproduction depends on relational infrastructure rather than labour demand alone.</p>\n<p>6. It reframes economic independence as an executability problem</p>\n<p>The paper distinguishes formal labour‑market access from executable labour‑market access. A person may possess legal eligibility and qualifications while lacking practical pathways into capability‑producing work.</p>\n<p>7. It provides a restoration architecture rather than a collapse narrative</p>\n<p>The manuscript identifies restoration pathways including:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>AI‑assisted apprenticeships,</li>\n<li>alternative experience pathways,</li>\n<li>portable capability signals,</li>\n<li>expanded care infrastructure,</li>\n<li>capability‑preserving entry redesign.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Its purpose is not to preserve obsolete work but to preserve the learning and capability functions those jobs performed.</p>\n<p>8. It establishes a prospective 2026&ndash;2031 test</p>\n<p>The paper freezes hypotheses before future outcomes occur and defines:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>empirical tests,</li>\n<li>rival explanations,</li>\n<li>restoration conditions,</li>\n<li>falsification criteria.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>This makes the programme prospectively auditable rather than retrospectively adjusted.</p>\n<p>9. It integrates AI, labour systems, care systems, and capability formation</p>\n<p>The manuscript connects technological change to:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>entry pathways,</li>\n<li>workforce reproduction,</li>\n<li>caregiving constraints,</li>\n<li>future skill formation,</li>\n<li>economic agency.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>This creates one of SR's strongest cross‑field integrations between labour, care, infrastructure, and AI.</p>\n<p>10. It extends SR&rsquo;s renewal architecture into labour markets</p>\n<p>At its deepest level, the paper asks whether a society can increase productivity while simultaneously weakening the mechanisms that reproduce future capability. It therefore extends SR's broader concern with:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>renewal,</li>\n<li>reproduction,</li>\n<li>restoration,</li>\n<li>long‑term system viability,</li>\n</ul>\n<p>into the labour‑market domain.</p>\n<p>The central contribution is a new question:</p>\n<p>A labour market may remain productive today.<br>Does it still reproduce the human capability required to remain productive tomorrow?</p>",
