Systemic Erosion Theory: How Systems Deplete Capacity, Stability, and Resilience Over Time Abstract Systemic Erosion Theory explains how systems gradually deplete the capacity, stability, and resilience

of

individuals

and

communities

through

cumulative,

long ‑ term

pressures

embedded

in

institutional

design.

Building

on

Social

Infrastructure

Theory

(SIT),

this

framework

identifies

erosion

as

a

predictable

outcome

of

repeated

exposure

to

instability,

scarcity,

surveillance,

and

bureaucratic

friction

(Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018;

Nixon,

2011).

Unlike

acute

harm,

erosion

is

slow,

distributed,

and

often

misattributed

to

personal

failure

rather

than

system

architecture

(Kleinman

et

al.,

1997).

This

paper

defines

systemic

erosion,

outlines

its

mechanisms,

introduces

a

typology

of

erosion

pathways,

and

situates

the

concept

within

the

broader

SIT

causal

chain:

exposure

→

slow

harm

→

erosion.

By

naming

erosion

as

a

distinct

theoretical

construct,

this

work

provides

a

foundation

for

analyzing

how

systems

wear

people

down

over

time

and

clarifies

the

long ‑ term

consequences

of

infrastructural

design. 1. Introduction Systems do not only harm through direct injury or acute failure. They also harm through erosion—the

gradual

depletion

of

a

person’s

capacity

to

function,

plan,

cope,

or

recover.

Systemic

erosion

refers

to

the

long ‑ term

wearing

down

of

physical,

emotional,

financial,

and

social

resources

as

a

result

of

navigating

harmful

or

unstable

infrastructures

(Galtung,

1969;

Nixon,

2011). This theory extends Social Infrastructure Theory (SIT) by identifying erosion as the long ‑ arc outcome

of

system ‑ generated

exposure

and

slow

harm.

While

Infrastructural

Exposure

Theory

explains

the

conditions

people

are

placed

in,

and

Slow

Harm

Theory

explains

the

cumulative

injuries

produced

by

delay

and

scarcity,

Systemic

Erosion

Theory

explains

what

happens

after

years

of

contact

with

these

conditions:

the

depletion

of

resilience

itself. 2. Literature Gap Existing scholarship addresses cumulative harm in fragments—chronic stress, allostatic load, burnout,

social

suffering

(Kleinman

et

al.,

1997)—but

no

unified

framework

explains

erosion

as

a

system ‑ level

outcome

produced

by

institutional

design.

Systemic

Erosion

Theory

fills

this

gap

by

naming

erosion

as

a

predictable,

measurable,

and

structural

process

rather

than

an

individual

psychological

state.

3. Defining Systemic Erosion Systemic erosion is the long ‑ term depletion of capacity caused by repeated exposure to harmful conditions

embedded

in

social,

bureaucratic,

and

environmental

infrastructures

(Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018;

Rodgers

&

O’Neill,

2012).

Erosion

is

characterized

by: • diminishing resilience • reduced cognitive bandwidth • weakened social ties • financial depletion • emotional exhaustion • declining health • loss of agency Erosion is not a personal failure. It is a system output (Galtung, 1969). 4. Theoretical Positioning Within SIT Systemic erosion is the third mechanism in the SIT causal chain: 1. Infrastructural Exposure — the conditions systems place people in (Rodgers & O’Neill, 2012) 2. Slow Harm — the cumulative injuries produced by delay, scarcity, and friction (Nixon, 2011) 3. Systemic Erosion — the long ‑ term depletion of capacity and resilience (Kleinman et al., 1997) Erosion is the endpoint of system ‑ generated harm. 5. A Typology of Systemic Erosion 5.1 Cognitive Erosion Reduced bandwidth, decision fatigue, impaired planning, and diminished executive function (Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018). 5.2 Emotional Erosion Chronic exhaustion, hopelessness, and the collapse of coping mechanisms (Kleinman et al., 1997).

5.3 Social Erosion Loss of community ties, isolation, and weakened support networks (Truth and Reconciliation Commission

of

Canada,

2015). 5.4 Economic Erosion Resource depletion, debt accumulation, and long ‑ term financial instability (United Nations Human

Rights

Council,

2019). 5.5 Physical Erosion Chronic illness, stress ‑ related conditions, and cumulative health decline (Nixon, 2011). 5.6 Temporal Erosion Loss of future orientation, inability to plan, and collapse of long ‑ term stability (Herd & Moynihan, 2018). 6. Mechanisms of Systemic Erosion 6.1 Repetition of Exposure Erosion emerges from repeated contact with instability, scarcity, surveillance, and friction (Rodgers

&

O’Neill,

2012). 6.2 Accumulation of Slow Harm Delays, denials, and bureaucratic obstacles compound over time, producing cumulative injury (Nixon,

2011). 6.3 Resource Drain Systems require individuals to expend time, money, energy, and emotional labor simply to maintain

basic

stability

(Herd

&

Moynihan,

2018). 6.4 Adaptive Overload People must constantly adapt to unstable infrastructures, exhausting their resilience (Kleinman et

al.,

1997). 6.5 Misattribution of Harm

Systems frame erosion as personal failure, which deepens emotional and social depletion (Galtung,

1969). 7. Boundary Conditions Erosion is moderated by: • access to stable resources • strong social networks • institutional flexibility • community ‑ based supports • protective policy environments These factors determine whether exposure leads to erosion or is buffered (Truth and Reconciliation

Commission

of

Canada,

2015). 8. Causal Model (Text ‑ Based Diagram) Infrastructural Exposure (Rodgers & O’Neill, 2012) ↓ Slow Harm (Nixon, 2011) ↓ Systemic Erosion (Kleinman et al., 1997) ↓ Long ‑ Term Outcomes (health, stability, agency) 9. Signature Example: The Disability Benefits System A disability benefits system often exposes individuals to: • surveillance (eligibility reviews) • scarcity (limited supports) • instability (interrupted payments) • bureaucratic friction (paperwork, appeals)

Over years, these exposures produce slow harm (Herd & Moynihan, 2018): • stress • financial strain • repeated crises Eventually, erosion emerges: • diminished health (Nixon, 2011) • reduced capacity to work or advocate • social withdrawal (Kleinman et al., 1997) • collapse of long ‑ term planning This example demonstrates the full SIT chain: exposure → slow harm → erosion → outcome. 10. Methodological Invitation Systemic Erosion Theory invites researchers to: • measure erosion trajectories • map erosion pathways across systems • analyze long ‑ term depletion patterns • study resilience collapse as a system output • evaluate interventions that reduce erosion This opens pathways for interdisciplinary research across public health, sociology, psychology, and

policy

studies. 11. Contribution to the Literature Systemic Erosion Theory advances the literature by naming erosion as a distinct, system ‑ generated

process

rather

than

an

individual

psychological

state

(Kleinman

et

al.,

1997).

It

unifies

By

SIT

the

clarifies

how

long ‑ term

depletion

emerges

from

exposure

and

slow

offering

for

the

long ‑ arc

consequences

of

social

infrastructures.
