    "description": "<p>Systemic Erosion Theory explains how systems gradually deplete the capacity, stability, and resilience of individuals and communities through long‑term pressures embedded in institutional design. Building on Social Infrastructure Theory (SIT), this framework identifies erosion as the cumulative outcome of repeated exposure to instability, scarcity, surveillance, and bureaucratic friction. Unlike acute harm, erosion unfolds slowly and is often misinterpreted as personal failure rather than a predictable output of system architecture.</p>\n<p>The theory defines systemic erosion as the long‑arc depletion of cognitive, emotional, social, economic, physical, and temporal resources. It introduces a structured typology of erosion pathways and clarifies how erosion emerges from two preceding mechanisms: infrastructural exposure (the conditions systems place people in) and slow harm (the cumulative injuries produced by delay, scarcity, and friction). Systemic Erosion Theory positions erosion as the endpoint of the SIT causal chain: exposure &rarr; slow harm &rarr; erosion.</p>\n<p>By naming erosion as a distinct theoretical construct, this framework unifies fragmented research on chronic stress, burnout, allostatic load, and social suffering into a structural model grounded in institutional design. It provides a method for analyzing how systems wear people down over time, how resilience collapses under chronic infrastructural pressure, and how long‑term depletion becomes normalized within social, bureaucratic, and environmental infrastructures.</p>\n<p>As part of the broader Social Infrastructure lineage&mdash;including Slow Harm Theory, Infrastructural Exposure Theory, and the STAR Framework&mdash;Systemic Erosion Theory offers a foundation for studying long‑term system‑generated harm and for designing infrastructures that do not erode human capacity by default.</p>\n<p>This essay is part of the SignalRupture canon, a body of work examining contemporary systems, infrastructures, and social dynamics through a conceptual and diagnostic lens. Each piece contributes to an ongoing analysis of structural stress, digital environments, governance patterns, and the evolving relationship between individuals and large‑scale systems. The work combines theoretical reflection with infrastructural observation, offering frameworks for understanding systemic erosion, cultural shifts, and emerging forms of social complexity.</p>",
