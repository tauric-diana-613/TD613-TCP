The

“public

$500

$5

Although

contexts. Together, these sources reveal a justice system that is not merely punitive but economically foundational. Methods This analysis uses a mixed ‑ source approach: 1. Verified fiscal anchor Only one figure was returned through direct search: • State governments spent $63.6 billion on prisons in 2023 (USAFacts, 2024). This number is treated as exact. 2. Reliable occupational data

Because the search tool did not return results for other fiscal anchors, this paper draws on: • U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) • American Bar Association (ABA) • Data USA • Statista These sources provide employment counts and salary data for lawyers, police officers, and judges. 3. Author ‑ generated estimates Income ‑ tax estimates are calculated using: • total wage income • conservative effective tax rates (20–25%) • clearly labelled approximations These calculations are not intended as precise fiscal claims but as structural comparisons. Results State Prison Expenditures The verified fiscal anchor is: • $63.6 billion in U.S. state prison spending in 2023 (USAFacts, 2024). This figure serves as the baseline for comparison. Lawyers: High ‑ Earning Fiscal Contributors The legal profession represents one of the highest ‑ earning labour blocs within the justice system. • 731,340 employed lawyers (BLS, 2023)

• Mean annual wage: $176,470 (BLS, 2023) Total wage income: 731{,}340 \times 176{,}470 \approx \$129\text{ billion} Assuming a 25% effective income ‑ tax rate: \$129\text{B} \times 0.25 \approx \$32\text{B} Thus, lawyers alone generate approximately $32 billion in annual income tax. Using the broader ABA population of 1.37 million active lawyers (ABA, 2025): 1{,}370{,}000 \times 176{,}470 \approx \$241\text{B income} Tax at 25%: \$241\text{B} \times 0.25 \approx \$60\text{B} Result: Lawyers generate $32–60 billion in annual income tax, depending on the population frame. Police Officers: Mid ‑ Range Contributors Police officers form a second major labour bloc. • 720,652 full ‑ time sworn officers (Statista, 2025) • Average salary: $82,872 (Data USA, 2025) Using a midpoint estimate of 800,000 officers: 800{,}000 \times 82{,}872 \approx \$66.3\text{B income} Assuming a 20% effective tax rate:

\$66.3\text{B} \times 0.20 \approx \$13.3\text{B} Result: Police officers generate approximately $12–13 billion in annual income tax. Judges: Smaller but Symbolically Significant Judges represent a smaller labour category but occupy a central institutional role. Assuming: • 30,000 judges (approximate) • Mean salary: $180,000 (based on federal and state ranges) Total wage income: 30{,}000 \times 180{,}000 = \$5.4\text{B} Tax at 25%: \$5.4\text{B} \times 0.25 \approx \$1.35\text{B} Result: Judges contribute approximately $1–2 billion in annual income tax. Combined Labour ‑ Generated Revenue When combined: • Lawyers: $32–60B • Police: $12–13B • Judges: $1–2B Total: \$46\text{B} \text{ to } \$74\text{B annually}

This range approaches or exceeds the $63.6B spent on state prisons. Recidivism as Structural Function Rather Than System Failure Public discourse routinely frames recidivism as evidence that the justice system is failing to rehabilitate

individuals.

This

framing

assumes

that

the

purpose

of

incarceration

is

personal

transformation,

behavioural

correction,

or

social

reintegration.

Yet

decades

of

scholarship,

economic

analysis,

and

institutional

behaviour

suggest

a

different

interpretation:

recidivism

is

not

a

malfunction

of

the

system

but

a

structural

feature

of

its

design.

The

justice

system,

as

currently

constituted,

does

not

depend

on

rehabilitation;

it

depends

on

throughput.

It

requires

a

steady,

renewable

flow

of

individuals

to

sustain

its

labour

demands,

justify

its

budgets,

and

stabilise

the

economic

ecosystems

built

around

carceral

institutions. Gilmore (2007) argues that prisons emerged as “geographical solutions to political economic crises,”

absorbing

surplus

labour,

capital,

and

land

during

periods

of

economic

restructuring.

In

this

framework,

the

prison

is

not

a

therapeutic

institution

but

an

economic

one.

Its

purpose

is

not

to

reduce

future

contact

with

the

system

but

to

maintain

the

conditions

under

which

the

system

can

continue

to

operate.

Wacquant

(2009)

extends

this

argument

by

showing

how

carceral

manages

social

by

cycling

marginalised

through

that

reproduce,

rather

resolve,

the

conditions

that

lead

to

Recidivism,

is

not

a

sign

of

institutional

failure;

it

is

a

predictable

outcome

of

a

system

that

manages,

contains,

and

recycles

social

precarity. Empirical research reinforces this structural interpretation. The Sentencing Project (2024) documents

that

individuals

leaving

prison

face

systemic

barriers

to

employment,

housing,

education,

and

social

These

are

the

of

the

Criminal ‑ record

stigma,

and

back

into

the

system.

Alexander

(2010)

“closed

The

produces

the

conditions

that

sustain

its

own

necessity. From an economic perspective, recidivism functions as a renewable source of institutional activity.

Every

It

In

The
