Morality on Trial: How Systems Criminalize Survival Through Trauma, Scarcity, and Structural Neglect By SignalRupture Abstract This expanded essay examines how acts labeled as “crime” emerge from trauma, scarcity, and systemic

neglect

rather

than

moral

failure.

It

argues

that

criminalization

is

a

structural

process:

a

method

of

managing

populations,

enforcing

hierarchy,

and

obscuring

the

conditions

that

produce

harm.

Through

personal

narrative,

historical

analogy,

and

infrastructural

analysis,

the

essay

reframes

morality

as

a

context ‑ dependent

artifact

shaped

by

power,

inequality,

and

intergenerational

trauma.

It

concludes

that

justice,

as

currently

practiced,

functions

less

as

a

pursuit

of

truth

and

more

as

a

mechanism

of

containment. Keywords criminalization of survival; structural violence; trauma and law; moral governance; administrative burden;

inequality;

Indigenous

trauma;

systemic

neglect;

infrastructural

ethics I. The Human Story Behind “Crime” When morality is reduced to a binary — right versus wrong — the human story disappears. Shoplifting,

theft,

and

homelessness

are

framed

as

criminal

acts,

yet

for

many,

they

are

survival

strategies

born

of

trauma,

famine,

and

systemic

abandonment. I remember famine as a child. My father, stressed and desperate, reached out for help. No one answered.

In

that

silence,

I

understood:

if

my

parents

had

stolen

food

to

keep

us

alive,

it

would

not

have

been

greed.

It

would

have

been

love. This is the part the system refuses to see: survival is not a crime. It is a biological imperative. II. Choice vs. Necessity

Some individuals choose criminality for its own sake — a minority. Most carry stories of hunger, neglect,

and

trauma.

To

collapse

these

realities

into

a

single

category

—

“criminal”

—

is

to

erase

the

truth

behind

survival

acts. As I once framed it: the system dangles the carrot, but when survival forces you to reach for it, the

act

is

branded

as

theft.

The

carrot

is

not

temptation

—

it

is

necessity. III. Structural Mechanisms of Criminalization Criminalization is not random. It is produced by specific infrastructural mechanisms documented across

public

policy

and

social

science. 1. Manufactured Scarcity Scarcity is often a policy outcome, not an accident (Davis, 2003). 2. Administrative Burden Administrative burden functions as a gatekeeping mechanism that filters access through exhaustion

(Penal

Reform

International,

2019). 3. Moralization of Poverty Poverty is framed as irresponsibility rather than the predictable output of economic design (Savage,

2023). 4. Selective Enforcement Laws are enforced unevenly across race, class, and geography (Baker & Haberman, 2023). 5. Trauma Loops Intergenerational trauma shapes behavior long before law intervenes (Truth and Reconciliation Commission

of

Canada,

2015). These mechanisms create the conditions for “crime,” then punish the people shaped by them. IV. Indigenous Trauma and Systemic Silence

For Indigenous communities, survival acts are judged as “crime” while the trauma that shaped them

is

erased.

Colonization,

displacement,

and

systemic

violence

ripple

across

generations

(Truth

and

Reconciliation

Commission

of

Canada,

2015). The system punishes the symptom — incarceration — while ignoring the cause: transgenerational

trauma

engineered

by

the

state. V. The Falling Apple: False Agency Newton’s falling apple revealed gravity — an invisible force shaping motion (Royal Society, 2010). Survival acts reveal trauma — an invisible force shaping behavior. What appears to be “choice” is often the pull of forces beyond control. VI. The Empty Hand and the Collapse of Equality The empty ‑ hand metaphor is widely used in political theory to describe negotiation and power (Marks,

2011). Only when both parties come with nothing can dialogue be genuine. But when one hand carries a

knife,

or

a

gun,

the

balance

collapses. Law claims neutrality, yet power distorts the frame. Equality becomes domination. VII. Feather and Ball: A Physics of Inequality Galileo’s principle — demonstrated in the BBC/NASA vacuum ‑ chamber experiment — showed that

a

feather

and

a

bowling

ball

fall

at

the

same

rate

when

air

resistance

is

removed

(BBC,

2014). People are like feathers and balls. Poverty, trauma, and discrimination act as “air resistance,” slowing

some

and

accelerating

others. Remove the barriers, and equality is revealed. The system fears this truth because it exposes inequality

as

engineered,

not

natural.

VIII. Double Standards of Justice Justice is not blind. It is selective. A powerful public figure may face extensive legal scrutiny yet retain political viability, depending on

institutional

rules

and

public

interpretation

(Savage,

2023;

Baker

&

Haberman,

2023). Meanwhile, a homeless person stealing food may face violence or lifelong punishment. The contradiction is stark: the powerful are shielded; the powerless are condemned. IX. Law, Facts, and the Hidden Architecture Law judges based on facts — evidence, testimony, measurable outcomes. But facts alone are fragments.

They

strip

away

the

psychological

burden

that

reveals

the

system’s

architecture. Trauma is acknowledged only as mitigation or aggravation. It is never allowed to expose the architect

—

the

system

that

created

the

conditions

for

harm

(Penal

Reform

International,

2019). Facts can be contained. Trauma cannot. X. The End Justifies the Means The maxim often associated with Machiavelli — “the end justifies the means” — becomes a tool of

governance

(Machiavelli,

1532/2005). • System’s use: Punishment is justified as necessary for order. • Reality: When trauma is the root, punishment perpetuates harm. • Double standard: The powerful have their ends excused; the poor are punished regardless of ends. • Collapse: The maxim fails when the end itself is built on unjust means. XI. Oligarchs at the Feast Elites often profit from the negative experiences of others. Prisons, debt cycles, and commodified

suffering

become

revenue

streams

(Davis,

2003;

Penal

Reform

International,

2019).

Morality is inverted: exploitation is normalized; survival acts are demonized. XII. The Criminalization of Survival This is the core truth: most “crime” is survival. Survival is not deviance. Survival is adaptation to structural harm. The system criminalizes adaptation because it reveals the system’s failure. XIII. Toward an Infrastructural Ethics If morality is shaped by infrastructure, then ethics must shift from individuals to systems. An infrastructural ethics asks: • What conditions produced this behavior? • Who benefits from criminalization? • What forms of harm are hidden behind personal responsibility? • How does the system manufacture the appearance of choice? This reframing exposes the architecture of blame. XIV. SignalRupture’s Integrity Integrity is not compliance. Integrity is refusing to let systems hide behind masks. To expose the architecture of law, trauma, and inequality is not rebellion. It is responsibility. Conclusion Morality cannot be separated from context. The majority who commit survival acts carry stories of

famine,

trauma,

and

systemic

neglect.

The system masks exploitation as justice, claiming the end justifies the means. But the truth is clear: Justice is the mask. Survival is the truth. Exploitation is the feast. Equality is the ground.
