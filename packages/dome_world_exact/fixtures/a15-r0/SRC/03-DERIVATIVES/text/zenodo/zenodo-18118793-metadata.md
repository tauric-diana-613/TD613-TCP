    "description": "<p>This essay reframes &ldquo;crime&rdquo; as a structural outcome of trauma, scarcity, and systemic neglect rather than an expression of moral failure. Through personal narrative, historical analogy, and infrastructural analysis, the work argues that criminalization functions as a governance mechanism: a way for systems to manage populations, enforce hierarchy, and obscure the conditions that produce harm. Acts labeled as deviant are revealed as survival strategies shaped by famine, intergenerational trauma, manufactured scarcity, and administrative burden.</p>\n<p>Drawing on research in structural violence, Indigenous trauma, public policy, and moral philosophy, the essay demonstrates how poverty, trauma, and inequality operate as invisible forces&mdash;akin to gravity or air resistance&mdash;that shape behavior long before law intervenes. The analysis exposes how justice systems selectively enforce morality, shielding the powerful while punishing the vulnerable, and how trauma is acknowledged only as mitigation rather than as evidence of systemic design.</p>\n<p>The paper introduces infrastructural ethics, a framework that shifts moral evaluation from individuals to the systems that produce harm. By examining mechanisms such as selective enforcement, misclassification, moralization of poverty, and the criminalization of survival, the essay argues that justice, as currently practiced, operates less as a pursuit of truth and more as a method of containment. It concludes by asserting that integrity lies not in compliance but in exposing the architecture of inequality that criminalizes survival itself.</p>",
