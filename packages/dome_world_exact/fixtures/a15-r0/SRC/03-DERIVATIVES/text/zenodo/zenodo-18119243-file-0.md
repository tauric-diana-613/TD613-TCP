This

of

contemporary

AI. AI as Infrastructure Infrastructure is defined by durability, embeddedness, and invisibility (Star, 1999). AI increasingly

meets

It

(through

data

(through

and

incentives

(through metrics and optimization targets). As Bratton (2016) argues, computational systems form

“stack”

of

planetary ‑ scale

that

AI’s

infrastructural

means

they

shape

the

conditions

under

which

decisions

are

made. Mechanisms That Produce an AI System State Instrumentation and Telemetry Modern organizations generate continuous data streams through sensors, logs, and digital interactions.

These

This

that

stabilizes

AI’s

presence

in

institutional

workflows. Standardization and Interfaces Shared ontologies, APIs, and model ‑ serving frameworks normalize how problems are represented.

Standardization

makes

reinforcing

its

infrastructural

role

(Mitchell

et

al.,

2019). Automation of Routine Judgment As routine decisions are delegated to models, probabilistic outputs become operational rules. Even

when

“in

the

loop,”

to

deference

to

model

outputs

(Robbins

&

van

Wynsberghe,

2022). Feedback Loops and Path Dependence AI systems frequently generate the data used to retrain them. Ensign et al. (2018) demonstrate how

which

Similar

occur

in

hiring,

credit

scoring,

and

health

triage. Economic Consolidation Platform economics favor centralized AI providers with access to large datasets and compute resources.

This

increases

lock ‑ in

and

reduces

institutional

flexibility

(Crawford,

2021). Governmental Benefits Within the AI System State

Governments are not merely regulators of AI—they are direct beneficiaries of AI’s infrastructural role.

As

AI

state

stabilizes

decision ‑ making,

and

political

and

Automated

triage

These

As

AI

new

state

knowledge

and

Once

Bratton

project

power

through

and

However,

state

Once governments adopt AI ‑ driven infrastructures, they become dependent on them for routine operations.

This

stabilizes

existing

power

structures

(Robbins

&

van

Wynsberghe,

2022). Measuring the AI System State To govern AI as a system state, organizations must measure the state itself, not only individual models.

Key

indicators

include: • Attention distribution: What the system surfaces, prioritizes, or flags. • Feedback strength: The proportion of training data derived from model ‑ generated labels. • Representational coverage: Demographic or contextual gaps in datasets (Gebru et al., 2018). • Decision ‑ authority mapping: How model outputs shift power among workers, managers, and vendors. • Drift and retraining cadence: How often models update and how updates affect downstream decisions. These indicators make the system state legible and governable. Documentation and Provenance as Governance Infrastructure Model Cards Model Cards (Mitchell et al., 2019) provide structured documentation of model purpose, performance,

and

limitations.

They

reduce

misuse

and

clarify

intended

contexts. Datasheets for Datasets Datasheets (Gebru et al., 2018) document dataset provenance, collection methods, and known gaps.

They

help

organizations

detect

representational

bias

and

prevent

inappropriate

reuse. Model Registries Model registries—structured inventories of deployed models with lineage, risk tiering, and incident

logs—are

increasingly

recognized

as

foundational

governance

tools.

Registries

enable

oversight

across

Risks and Failure Modes Metric Fixation AI systems often optimize for measurable proxies (e.g., engagement, throughput), which can crowd

out

unmeasured

public

values

(Hwang,

2020). Entrenched Bias Feedback loops can amplify small representational gaps into systemic inequities (Ensign et al., 2018). Opacity and Accountability Gaps As decision rules migrate into models, responsibility becomes diffuse. Lock ‑ In and Fragility Dependence on single vendors or pipelines increases vulnerability to failures and reduces institutional

adaptability. Policy and Design Recommendations Treat AI as Infrastructure Fund maintenance, interoperability, and redundancy. Require documentation and provenance for

all

production

systems. Instrument Feedback Loops Monitor and limit training on model ‑ generated labels. Require human review for high ‑ impact decisions. Promote Modularity and Plurality Avoid single ‑ vendor lock ‑ in; require modular architectures and multiple models for critical domains. Align Incentives with Public Value Redesign procurement and KPIs to reward long ‑ term outcomes, equity, and resilience.

Institutionalize Adaptive Governance Use continuous audits, red ‑ teaming, participatory oversight, and model registries to monitor systemic

drift. Conclusion Understanding AI as a system state reframes governance from reactive audits to proactive stewardship.
