2.1 Case Selection Cases were chosen because they represent: • clear U.S. involvement • documented resource or geopolitical stakes • strong archival or scholarly evidence • continuity across time 2.2 Theoretical Framework Three SR theories structure the analysis: • Pathologized Infrastructure • Cognitive Manipulation • The Leach Economy 2.3 Sources The essay draws on: • peer ‑ reviewed scholarship • declassified documents • human rights reports • policy analyses • international governance sources 3. Defining the Leach Economy The Leach Economy is a structural model describing how U.S. power expands through a recurring

five ‑ stage

cycle: 1. Crisis Construction or Amplification 2. Salvation Narrative 3. Intervention 4. Extraction 5. Instability or Dependency

This cycle is infrastructural — reproduced across centuries. 4. Pathologized Infrastructure: The Domestic Origin Pattern The U.S. intervention pattern begins domestically. The seizure of Indigenous lands established the foundational logic of expansion (Dunbar ‑ Ortiz, 2014;

Wolfe,

2006). This is Pathologized Infrastructure (SR, Pathologized Infrastructure): violence embedded as governance. 5. Chile 1973: The Prototype of Modern Intervention The overthrow of Salvador Allende and installation of Augusto Pinochet illustrates the modern intervention

architecture: • crisis narrative • moral justification • covert intervention (Kinzer, 2006) • extraction (Harvey, 2005) • instability (Grandin, 2006) Declassified documents confirm direct U.S. involvement (National Security Archive, 2023; Borger,

2013). Chile becomes the prototype for modern regime ‑ change logic. 6. Iraq and 9/11: Crisis as Catalyst 9/11 accelerated U.S. interventionism, enabling: • the War on Terror • invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq • expansion of surveillance • growth of the defense economy

This aligns with Johnson’s (2004) analysis of militarized empire. Here, Cognitive Manipulation (SR, Cognitive Manipulation) becomes central: public perception is

shaped

through

fear

and

narrative

control. Iraq followed the same sequence (Perkins, 2004). 7. Africa: Counterterrorism as Resource Corridor Across Africa, U.S. involvement is framed as stabilization, but structurally aligns with: • mineral access • strategic positioning • competition with China • base expansion Turse (2015) documents how AFRICOM mirrors Cold War patterns. Africa is another corridor in the Leach Economy. 8. Venezuela: The Latest Expression of the Leach Economy Venezuela’s deviation from U.S. economic alignment — nationalization, resource sovereignty, geopolitical

be

reorganized.

10. Limitations • Focuses on U.S. actions, not multipolar dynamics • Does not include internal Venezuelan political complexities • Emphasizes structural patterns over individual agency • Relies on publicly available sources 11. Future Implications The Leach Economy suggests: • multipolar competition may intensify extraction ‑ driven interventions • resource transitions may shift target geographies • algorithmic governance may deepen narrative control • Latin American sovereignty will remain structurally constrained 12. Conclusion The United States does not intervene because crises occur. Crises occur because intervention is the operating system. The Leach Economy is the architecture through which U.S. power sustains itself: • crisis as fuel • narrative as interface • intervention as infrastructure • extraction as metabolism • instability as residue From Indigenous lands to Chile to Iraq to Africa to Venezuela — and now into the electoral dynamics
