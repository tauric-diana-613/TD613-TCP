5.1 Representational Harm Distortion, stereotyping, or mislabeling of identities or events. 5.2 Epistemic Harm Invalidation of knowledge, experience, or credibility. 5.3 Visibility Harm Suppression, erasure, or selective amplification of identities or narratives. 5.4 Categorical Harm Imposition of reductive or inaccurate identity categories. 5.5 Algorithmic Harm Systemic misrepresentation produced by automated ranking, sorting, or metadata systems. This typology provides a structured vocabulary for analyzing symbolic injury across contexts. 6. Scope and Boundaries Symbolic Harm Theory does not claim that: • symbolic harm replaces material harm • symbolic harm requires intent • symbolic harm is always visible • symbolic harm is limited to digital platforms Instead, it asserts that symbolic harm is a foundational layer of injury that shapes and predicts other

harms. 9. Methodological Orientation

Symbolic harm can be studied through: • infrastructural analysis • discourse tracing • platform architecture mapping • metadata pattern observation • interpretive and semiotic analysis • visibility and ranking audits This methodological flexibility allows the theory to be applied across disciplines. 10. Why This Matters Now Symbolic harm has become increasingly urgent due to: • the rise of algorithmic mediation • the collapse of traditional gatekeeping • the scale of digital representation • the infrastructural nature of modern communication • the speed at which symbolic narratives propagate In a world governed by platforms, symbolic harm is no longer peripheral — it is central. 11.
