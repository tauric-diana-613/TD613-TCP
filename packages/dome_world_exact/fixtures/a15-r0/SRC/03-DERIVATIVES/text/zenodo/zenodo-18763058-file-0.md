Keywords human erosion; systemic depletion; infrastructural strain; emotional thinning; rupture conditions

1. Introduction: The Disappearing Human Layer Human Erosion Theory begins with a simple observation: people are not functioning the way systems

expect

them

to.

Engagement is inconsistent. Attention is fragmented. Emotional bandwidth is thin. Interpretive capacity

is

unstable.

This is not a cultural shift. It is a structural condition. Human erosion describes the slow depletion of the human layer under systemic strain.

2. The Three Pillars of Human Erosion

Human erosion emerges from the interaction of: A. Ambient Erosion The background condition of instability, precarity, and noise. B. Cognitive Erosion The thinning of interpretive bandwidth under continuous overload. C. Emotional Erosion The collapse of affective capacity under chronic stress and uncertainty. Together, these produce a subject who is not disengaged but overdrawn.

3. The Systemic Drivers of Human Erosion Human erosion is produced by: ● economic precarity ● institutional instability ● platform acceleration ● informational saturation ● collapsing social infrastructure ● continuous crisis cycles ● boundaryless labor environments These forces extract cognitive and emotional labor faster than it can be replenished. Erosion is not a failure of resilience. It is a predictable outcome of systemic design.

4. The Eroded Human as a Systemic Artifact The eroded human is: ● perceptive but depleted ● informed but unable to act ● connected but unable to engage ● aware but unable to metabolize

This is not a psychological profile. It is an infrastructural artifact. The eroded human is shaped by the same forces that destabilize institutions and platforms.

5. Erosion as a Precondition for Rupture Human erosion is the slow collapse. Rupture is the moment of exposure. When erosion reaches a threshold, the subject can no longer maintain coherence within the system.

Rupture

is

not

a

it

the

system

Human Erosion Theory positions rupture as the logical endpoint of sustained depletion.
