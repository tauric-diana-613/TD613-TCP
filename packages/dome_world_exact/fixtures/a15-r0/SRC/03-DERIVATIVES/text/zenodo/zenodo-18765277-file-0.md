1. Survival as a Full ‑ Time Occupation The modern cost of living has transformed survival into a job. Rent consumes half a paycheck, groceries

drain

the

rest,

and

transportation,

childcare,

and

healthcare

push

families

into

debt.

Under

autonomy

becomes

A person who spends every waking hour working, commuting, budgeting, and worrying is not free.

They

are

contained

by

the

economics

of

survival.

The system does not need to restrict autonomy explicitly — it simply ensures that autonomy is unaffordable.

2. Housing: The First Price of Captivity Housing is the foundation of stability, yet it has become the primary mechanism of economic control.

When rent rises faster than wages, people lose: ● mobility ● bargaining power ● the ability to leave toxic jobs ● the capacity to plan for the future Housing scarcity forces people to accept whatever conditions they can afford. The system calls this

a

market.

Lived

experience

calls

it

captivity.

3. Food: The Price of Health and the Cost of

Harm

Food is essential for survival, yet the system prices nutrition as a luxury and harm as a bargain. ● Healthy food is expensive. ● Junk food is cheap and omnipresent. ● Time to cook is scarce. ● Stress drives consumption patterns. People are told to "choose better," but the economics of food make health a privilege. The system

profits

twice:

once

from

selling

harmful

food,

and

again

from

treating

the

illnesses

it

causes.

4. Transportation: The Cost of Access Transportation determines access to work, education, healthcare, and community. Yet it is one of

the

most

expensive

components

of

survival.

Car dependency forces people into: ● loans ● insurance payments ● fuel costs ● maintenance cycles Public transit, where it exists, is often unreliable or unsafe. The system monetizes movement, turning

access

into

a

commodity.

5. Healthcare: The Price of Staying Alive Healthcare is framed as a personal responsibility, yet it is priced as a luxury. People must choose between: ● medication or rent ● treatment or groceries ● preventative care or debt The system profits from illness, not health. Autonomy collapses when staying alive becomes a financial

burden.

6. Childcare: The Cost of Participation Childcare determines whether parents can work, study, or rest. Yet it is priced at levels that rival rent.

When childcare consumes most of a paycheck, autonomy disappears. Parents are forced into: ● unstable work ● unsafe arrangements ● chronic exhaustion The system relies on unpaid labor while penalizing those who cannot provide it.

7. Time: The Most Expensive Resource Time is the foundation of autonomy, yet it is the resource most aggressively extracted. Low ‑ wage workers lose time through: ● long hours ● multiple jobs ● unpredictable schedules ● long commutes ● caregiving responsibilities Time poverty destroys the capacity for rest, reflection, planning, or resistance. A person without time

cannot

be

autonomous.

8. The System Prices Freedom Out of Reach

Autonomy requires: ● financial stability ● access to essentials ● time to think and act ● the ability to say no The system ensures that these conditions remain inaccessible for most people. It prices autonomy

at

a

level

only

the

privileged

can

afford.

The result is a society where freedom exists in theory but not in practice.
