Mapping the Collapse of Modern Society Abstract Modern society is undergoing a multi-layered collapse across mental, social, institutional, and infrastructural

domains.

This

essay

maps

the

genealogy

of

this

collapse

from

the

origins

of

modernity

to

the

present

post-web

era,

showing

how

industrialization,

institutional

rigidity,

neoliberal

erosion,

digital

acceleration,

and

infrastructural

drift

have

produced

a

society

that

is

no

longer

compatible

with

human

life.

Collapse

is

not

a

sudden

event

but

a

cumulative,

recursive

process

in

which

systems

exceed

their

capacity,

institutions

lose

coherence,

individuals

internalize

systemic

failures,

and

the

environment

becomes

cognitively

uninhabitable.

This

framework

synthesizes

historical,

sociological,

and

infrastructural

dynamics

into

a

unified

model

of

contemporary

breakdown.

Keywords Collapse; Modernity; Institutional Decay; Systemic Drift; Social Fragmentation; Cognitive Overload;

Digital

Acceleration;

Governance;

SignalRupture.

1. Origins of Modern Society Modern society emerged from the industrial revolution, which replaced local community structures

with

mass

society

and

mechanized

labor.

Early

sociologists

identified

the

fractures

this

created:

alienation,

anomie,

and

bureaucratic

rationalization.

These

foundational

tensions

never

resolved;

they

became

the

architecture

of

modern

life.

2. The Institutional Era The 20th century built large-scale institutions—states, corporations, schools, hospitals—to stabilize

industrial

chaos.

These

institutions

produced

order

through

hierarchy,

surveillance,

and

conformity.

Stability

was

achieved,

but

it

was

rigid

and

dependent

on

suppressing

dissent

and

pathologizing

deviation.

3. The Neoliberal Turn From the 1970s onward, deregulation and privatization hollowed out institutional capacity. Markets

replaced

communities,

and

individuals

were

burdened

with

responsibilities

once

held

by

collective

structures.

This

shift

initiated

the

long

erosion

of

social

and

institutional

resilience.

4. The Digital Era The rise of the internet destabilized shared reality. Information became abundant but fragmented,

attention

became

monetized,

and

identity

became

algorithmic.

People

experienced

burnout,

loneliness,

and

cognitive

overload

as

digital

infrastructures

reshaped

social

and

mental

life.

5. The Post-Web Era Today’s environment is characterized by systemic drift: infrastructures degrade slowly until failure

becomes

the

system’s

default

state.

Institutions

lose

legitimacy,

data

becomes

unreliable,

and

governance

becomes

symbolic.

Individuals

internalize

systemic

dysfunction

as

personal

failure,

leading

to

widespread

mental

strain.

6. Mechanisms of Collapse Collapse manifests through structural overload, institutional decay, cognitive fragmentation, and social

disintegration.

Systems

exceed

human

cognitive

limits,

institutions

cannot

process

complexity,

and

communities

dissolve.

The

environment

becomes

incompatible

with

human

needs.

7. Exposure Through Failure In advanced collapse, systems reveal their architecture through their failures. Institutional attacks,

omissions,

and

defensive

reflexes

become

evidence

of

deeper

dysfunction.

8. Acceleration Mismatch and Governance Failure AI entered society in less than five years, accelerating far beyond anything bureaucratic or governmental

systems

were

built

to

handle.

Platforms

now

move

faster

than

the

state,

and

the

state

is

reduced

to

chasing

technologies

it

cannot

regulate,

understand,

or

anticipate.

Bureaucracy—designed

for

slowness,

stability,

and

procedural

certainty—becomes

a

barrier

rather

than

a

safeguard,

exposing

the

deeper

truth:

institutional

frameworks

were

never

engineered

to

foresee

failures

at

this

scale

or

speed.

Their

rapid

adoption

of

AI

reveals

this

contradiction.

They

implement

technologies

they

cannot

predict,

while

remaining

blind

to

the

crises

those

technologies

will

generate.

The

result

is

a

governance

architecture

that

learns

only

through

collapse,

reacts

only

after

damage,

and

leaves

society

in

a

chaos

it

cannot

explain.

This

is

the

dysfunctional

paradox

of

modern

institutions:

they

pursue

stability

through

processes

that

guarantee

they

will

always

be

too

late,

and

they

discover

the

consequences

of

their

decisions

only

by

accident.

have

exceeded

human

capacity,

producing

a

world

that

is

no

compatible

with

human

life.

Collapse

is

not

but

cumulative,

emerging

from

industrial

foundations,

institutional

rigidity,

neoliberal

erosion,

and

digital

acceleration.

As

systems

drift

and

institutions

decay,

individuals

internalize

systemic

failures,

leading

to

mental,

social,

and
