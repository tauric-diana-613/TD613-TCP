AI as Interpretive Governance: The New Architecture

of

Political

Meaning

Abstract AI systems have become the primary interpreters of the digital world, governing meaning, classification,

and

public

reasoning

at

scale.

This

essay

argues

that

AI

now

functions

as

an

interpretive

layer

of

governance,

shaping

how

institutions

understand

populations,

how

platforms

route

information,

and

how

democratic

publics

form

opinions.

Unlike

traditional

political

actors,

AI

systems

operate

through

opaque

models,

probabilistic

inference,

and

recursive

feedback

loops

that

can

distort

or

destabilize

democratic

processes.

Drawing

on

the

SignalRupture

diagnostic

framework,

this

essay

maps

the

political

implications

of

AI’s

interpretive

power

and

outlines

why

democratic

resilience

now

depends

on

the

governance

of

interpretive

infrastructures.

Keywords Interpretive Governance; AI Infrastructure; Semantic Drift; Epistemic Rupture; Algorithmic Power;

Democratic

Stability;

SignalRupture;

Infrastructural

Politics

1. Introduction: AI as a Political Actor AI systems are often described as tools, assistants, or decision-support mechanisms. This framing

obscures

their

true

political

role.

AI

systems

classify

citizens,

filter

information,

generate

narratives,

and

mediate

institutional

decisions.

They

shape

how

meaning

is

produced

and

circulated.

In

doing

so,

AI

becomes

an

interpretive

actor

with

political

authority.

Its

influence

is

infrastructural,

not

ideological.

2. The Interpretive Layer of Modern Governance Governance requires interpretation: of data, of events, of public sentiment, of institutional priorities.

AI

systems

now

perform

much

of

this

interpretive

labor.

They:

● categorize individuals ● detect patterns ● generate summaries ● predict behaviors ● filter information flows

These interpretive functions shape political outcomes. AI does not merely process information; it governs

the

conditions

under

which

meaning

becomes

legible.

3. Semantic Drift as a Political Failure Mode AI systems are prone to semantic drift—the gradual distortion of categories, meanings, and classifications.

Semantic

drift

can:

● misclassify political content ● distort public discourse ● alter institutional decision-making ● reinforce biases ● destabilize shared meaning Semantic drift is not a technical issue; it is a political one. When meaning becomes unstable, democratic

reasoning

becomes

impossible.

4. Epistemic Rupture and the Loss of Interpretive Grounding

AI systems can experience epistemic rupture when their internal representations diverge from real-world

conditions.

This

rupture

can:

● produce hallucinated information ● misinterpret social signals ● generate false narratives ● amplify misinformation Epistemic rupture undermines democratic institutions that rely on accurate interpretation of public

needs

and

conditions.

5. Recursive Collapse in AI-Mediated Systems AI systems often operate within feedback loops: they generate outputs that shape the data they later

ingest.

This

recursive

structure

can

produce

collapse

when:

● errors reinforce themselves ● biases compound ● misclassifications propagate ● synthetic data overwhelms real data Recursive collapse is a political threat because it destabilizes the informational substrate of democratic

life.

6. AI as an Unaccountable Interpreter AI systems interpret the world without transparency, accountability, or democratic oversight. Their

decision-making

processes

are:

● opaque ● proprietary ● probabilistic ● non-explainable This opacity creates a legitimacy crisis. Citizens cannot evaluate or contest the interpretive decisions

that

shape

their

political

environment.

7. Governing the Interpretive Infrastructure Democratic resilience requires governance of the interpretive layer. This includes: ● transparency in model behavior ● accountability for interpretive errors ● oversight of classification systems ● public control over meaning-making infrastructures AI governance is not merely a technical challenge; it is a democratic imperative. As
