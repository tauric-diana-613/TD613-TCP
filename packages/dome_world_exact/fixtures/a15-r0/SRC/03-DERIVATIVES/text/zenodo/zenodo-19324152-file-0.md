1. Introduction: Institutional Stability and Epistemic Drift Institutions present their epistemic tools—standardized testing,

quantitative

metrics,

and

experimental

methods—as

neutral

mechanisms

for

knowledge

production.

However,

extensive

scholarship

demonstrates

that

these

tools

are

socially

constructed,

historically

contingent,

and

shaped

by

governance

needs

rather

than

purely

epistemic

goals

(Hacking,

1983;

Porter,

1995;

Daston

&

Galison,

2007).

This paper advances a central claim: institutional

epistemic

failure

is

not

accidental

but

emerges

from

selection

pressures

that

privilege

stability

over

accuracy.

Rather than requiring intentional design, institutional obsolescence

can

arise

through:

● incentive structures that reward legible outputs, ● governance systems that prioritize comparability,

● and disciplinary fragmentation that prevents systemic critique

integration.

Over time, these dynamics produce epistemic drift —a divergence

between

what

institutional

methods

measure

and

the

phenomena

they

claim

to

represent.

2. Infrastructural Hygiene and the Problem of Non-Maintenance

This paper introduces infrastructural hygiene as a measurable

property

of

epistemic

systems.

Infrastructural

hygiene

refers

to

the

extent

to

which

knowledge-producing

systems:

● incorporate known confounding variables, ● update models in response to critique, ● integrate cross-disciplinary findings, and ● remain robust under re-specification and replication. Failure of infrastructural hygiene results in methodological persistence

without

validity .

Despite decades of critique, institutions have maintained: ● standardized testing systems shown to narrow cognitive engagement

(Anyon,

1980;

Eisner,

2002), ● quantification practices that construct rather than merely describe

social

reality

(Espeland

&

Stevens,

1998;

Porter,

1995),

● and experimental methods that shape the phenomena they

measure

(Hacking,

1983;

Pickering,

1995).

This persistence reflects not epistemic success, but infrastructural

inertia.

3. The Scattered Critique Problem A central puzzle is why extensive critique did not produce institutional

change.

Prior scholarship across STS, sociology, and education identified:

● classification bias (Bowker & Star, 1999), ● state simplification and legibility constraints (Scott, 1998), ● disciplinary power structures (Foucault, 1977/1975), ● and paradigm resistance (Kuhn, 1962). However, these critiques remained institutionally non-binding

due

to

fragmentation

across

disciplines.

This paper defines this as the Scattered Critique Problem : When structurally aligned critiques are distributed across disciplinary

silos,

institutions

can

absorb

them

as

theoretical

discourse

without

operational

consequences.

Because these critiques: ● did not produce standardized metrics,

● did not integrate into policy frameworks, ● and did not directly challenge model outputs, they failed to exert systemic pressure despite their accuracy.

4. Mechanisms of Epistemic Inflation Institutional knowledge production is shaped by structural constraints

that

systematically

inflate

effect

sizes

and

overstate

causal

claims.

A key mechanism is Omitted Variable Bias, in which excluded structural

variables

are

absorbed

into

estimated

coefficients,

inflating

their

magnitude.

In parallel, reliance on p-value thresholds as indicators of truth encourages

overinterpretation

of

statistically

significant

but

weak

or

unstable

effects.

These dynamics contribute to broader concerns associated with

the

Replication

Crisis,

in

which

many

published

findings

fail

to

replicate

under

varying

conditions.

As John Ioannidis (2005) argues, a substantial proportion of published

findings

may

be

false

due

to

bias,

flexibility

in

analysis,

and

structural

incentives

within

research

systems.

This paper extends that insight: inflation

is

not

merely

a

statistical

artifact

but

an

infrastructural

outcome.

5. Conceptual Re-Specification: The SignalRupture (SR) Framework

SignalRupture (SR) is introduced as a diagnostic framework for

auditing

epistemic

models.

5.1 Baseline Institutional Model Institutional models often take the form: Y= β0+β1 P+ ε Y= β0 +β1 P+ ε Where: ● PP represents a psychological or behavioral variable, ● structural conditions are excluded. Under these conditions, omitted variables are absorbed into β1β1 ,

producing

inflated

estimates.

5.2 Structural Re-Specification SR extends the model: Y= β0+β1 P+ β2 S+ β3 R+ β4 C+ ε Y= β0 +β1 P+ β2 S+ β3 R+ β4 C+ ε Where: ● SS = socioeconomic conditions ● RR = environmental reliability

● CC = contextual constraints 5.3 Expected Effects of Re-Specification When structural variables are included: ● estimated coefficients for PP typically decrease, ● standard errors increase, ● test statistics decline, ● and p-values rise above conventional thresholds. These shifts do not invalidate the original findings outright but reveal

their

dependence

on

model

specification .

6. Case Analyses: Reinterpreting Canonical Findings 6.1 Obedience Studies Research by Stanley Milgram (1963) is widely interpreted as demonstrating

a

general

human

tendency

toward

obedience.

However, the experimental setup includes: ● strong institutional cues, ● hierarchical authority structures, ● and constrained participant roles. These conditions suggest the study measures context-dependent

compliance

rather

than

a

stable

trait.

6.2 Delay of Gratification The Stanford Marshmallow Experiment (Mischel et al., 1972) is

often

interpreted

as

measuring

self-control.

Subsequent research indicates that: ● environmental reliability and trust significantly influence behavior.

Thus, observed outcomes reflect adaptive responses to context ,

not

purely

internal

traits.

6.3 Growth Mindset The Growth Mindset framework (Yeager & Dweck, 2012) links beliefs

about

intelligence

to

academic

performance.

However: ● effect sizes are often small, ● replication results are mixed, ● and measurements rely heavily on self-report. Structural factors such as: ● school resources, ● teacher stability, ● and socioeconomic conditions may account for a substantial portion of observed variance.

7. Institutional Persistence and Structural Selection Why do these models persist despite known limitations? Institutional systems reward: ● clarity over complexity, ● stability over revision, ● and actionable outputs over epistemic uncertainty. As argued by Thomas Kuhn (1962), paradigms persist not because

they

are

correct,

but

because

they

are

institutionally

stabilized .

Similarly, Michel Foucault (1975/1977) demonstrates that knowledge

systems

are

inseparable

from

power

structures

that

regulate

what

counts

as

truth.

This paper extends these insights: Institutional epistemic systems are shaped by selection pressures

that

favor

methods

producing

administratively

useful

outputs—even

when

those

outputs

are

epistemically

fragile.

8. AI as an Epistemic Stress Test Artificial intelligence systems introduce a new dynamic into epistemic

evaluation.

Because AI systems: ● synthesize across domains, ● detect inconsistencies, ● and operate without institutional attachment, they function as stress tests for epistemic coherence. Rather than introducing epistemic instability, AI reveals: ● model inconsistencies, ● construct ambiguity, ● and dependence on hidden assumptions. Thus, AI does not produce institutional obsolescence—it exposes

it .

9. Conclusion: From Critique to Reconstruction This paper has argued that institutional epistemic failure emerges

from:

● infrastructural non-maintenance, ● disciplinary fragmentation, ● and structural selection pressures. SignalRupture (SR) provides a framework for moving beyond critique

by:

● auditing model specification, ● reintegrating structural variables,

● and redefining epistemic credibility in operational terms. Institutional reform does not require abandoning quantitative methods

or

experimentation.

It

requires:

● expanding model scope, ● integrating existing critique, ● and maintaining epistemic infrastructures capable of adapting

to

complexity.

Obsolescence, in this sense, is not a failure of knowledge—but

a

failure

of

maintenance.
