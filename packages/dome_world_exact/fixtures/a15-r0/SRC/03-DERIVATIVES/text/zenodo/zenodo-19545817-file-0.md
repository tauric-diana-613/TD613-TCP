Abstract Institutions rarely fail through abrupt collapse; they drift. Over time, governance systems accumulate

(AI)

The paper introduces a drift–capacity model in which institutional systems operate through hidden

1. Introduction Modern governance systems operate in environments characterized by rapidly increasing informational

and

administrative

complexity.

Institutions

must

process

legal,

social,

technological,

and

behavioral

inputs

simultaneously,

often

across

decentralized

digital

infrastructures

(Tufekci,

2015).

Over

time,

a

structural

mismatch

emerges

between

system

complexity

and

institutional

processing

capacity.

This paper argues that artificial intelligence systems increasingly function as compensatory mechanisms

within

strained

governance

environments.

Rather

than

representing

a

linear

progression

of

technological

improvement,

AI

adoption

reflects

underlying

structural

pressures

in

institutional

systems

(Zuboff,

2019).

2. Institutional Drift 2.1 Definition Institutional drift refers to the cumulative divergence between an institution’s formal function and its

actual

operational

capacity

under

real-world

conditions.

This condition has been partially observed in theories of bounded rationality (Simon, 1971) and early

cybernetic

limits

to

information

processing

(Wiener,

1950).

However,

these

frameworks

capture

isolated

constraints

rather

than

modeling

drift

as

a

systemic,

accumulative

process

across

governance

layers.

In structural terms, drift is not inefficiency—it is a progressive misalignment between environmental

complexity

and

institutional

processing

capacity.

Drift accumulates through:

● administrative expansion ● policy layering ● informational fragmentation ● coordination overhead

These processes do not immediately produce failure. They are absorbed by institutional buffers until

those

buffers

can

no

longer

sustain

the

mismatch.

3. Over-Extraction and Buffer Collapse Institutions maintain functional stability through hidden buffers: ● surplus labor capacity ● informal coordination ● discretionary compliance ● temporal elasticity

Over-extraction occurs when these buffers are continuously consumed to preserve system outputs.

Once depleted, the system does not become dysfunctional—it becomes visible. Over-Extraction Visibility Law: Systems do not become dysfunctional; they become visible when the buffers masking their limitations

are

exhausted.

At this stage: ● delays stabilize rather than resolve

● errors become structural rather than exceptional ● responsiveness declines irreversibly

Earlier research has documented capacity strain and administrative overload, but has not formalized

the

transition

point

at

which

dysfunction

becomes

externally

observable

as

a

structural

condition.

4. Compensatory Governance and AI Emergence 4.1 Definition Compensatory governance refers to the introduction of external systems designed to stabilize institutional

performance

under

conditions

of

capacity

strain.

Artificial intelligence increasingly fulfills this role.

4.2 Mechanism of Emergence Early cybernetic and organizational research identified limits to human information processing (Wiener,

1950;

Beer,

1972).

These

accounts

establish

boundary

conditions

but

do

not

model

the

systemic

transition

from

human

governance

to

machine-supported

stabilization.

In structural terms: AI Emergence Law: When institutional processing capacity falls below environmental complexity thresholds, AI emerges

as

compensatory

infrastructure

to

preserve

operational

continuity.

Under these conditions, AI is deployed as: ● triage infrastructure ● automation layer ● decision compression system

● workload redistribution mechanism

AI adoption concentrates in environments where: ● decision volume exceeds human processing capacity ● administrative delay becomes politically or legally costly ● platform-scale systems generate continuous governance demands ● error rates become structurally embedded

AI does not replace governance. It stabilizes systems operating beyond their capacity limits.

5. Historical Development of Computational Governance Early computational and cybernetic frameworks identified constraints in human decision-making capacity.

Wiener

(1950)

These accounts identify critical limits but remain localized. They do not integrate these constraints

This paper positions those observations within a broader structural sequence: drift accumulation →

5.1 Early Government Investment in AI (1950s–1970s) Government investment in computational governance began far earlier than contemporary discourse

suggests.

In

RAND

“the

(IPTO)

to

automated

These early programs framed computation as necessary infrastructure for managing emerging system

complexity

rather

as

5.2 The Strategic Computing Program (1983–1993) The Strategic Computing Program (SCP) represented the first large-scale federal AI initiative. Its program

documents

explicitly

cited

the

need

to

augment

human

decision-making

in

where

exceeded

operator

SCP

funded

early

in

machine

autonomous

as

to

stabilize

performance

in

high-demand

operational

This period marks the first formal articulation of AI as compensatory governance infrastructure.

5.3 Administrative Automation and Early Algorithmic Systems

(1990s–2000s)

By the 1990s, governments began deploying automated systems for welfare eligibility, immigration

risk

fraud

These

modernization

The shift toward automated classification and prediction reflected growing strain within public-sector

institutions.

5.4 Drift Visibility and Acceleration (2010s–2023) The 2010s saw the convergence of platform-scale complexity, algorithmic governance failures, and

public-sector

capacity

National

By the early 2020s, AI procurement accelerated across healthcare, immigration, courts, and social

AI

5.5 Evidence of 1990s Automation Failure and Structural Continuity

Early administrative automation in the 1990s is often remembered as a transitional phase between

However,

Rather

Zajko (2023) demonstrates that the harms attributed to “AI” today—opaque decision ‑ making, punitive

These

Their

Similarly, Kannen (2025) documents how welfare automation initiatives consistently produced wrongful

These

Institutions

Historical reviews reinforce this pattern. Oravec (2018) notes that early AI and automation projects

The

Carney

inability

Across these accounts, a consistent pattern emerges: automation was deployed not because it

worked,

but

because

lacked

to

manage

When

This

The 1990s therefore represent not a primitive precursor to modern AI governance but an early expression

The

6. Downward Regulation Under Capacity Strain When institutions lose the ability to regulate complex system layers, governance shifts toward the

only

layer

that

remains

directly

accessible:

individuals.

Existing analyses of platform power and institutional asymmetry (Zuboff, 2019) identify aspects of

this

imbalance

but

do

not

formalize

the

structural

constraint

driving

it.

In structural terms: Downward Regulation Principle: When system-layer regulation becomes inaccessible, institutions regulate the individual layer regardless

of

causal

relevance.

This produces: ● substitution of behavioral control for structural intervention ● compliance-based policy responses ● symbolic enforcement mechanisms

This shift is not primarily ideological. It is a constraint-driven response to system-layer opacity.

7. Compensatory Governance Principle Compensatory Governance Principle: When institutional processing capacity falls below the complexity demands of its environment,

governance

systems

introduce

compensatory

infrastructures

to

maintain

functional

continuity

without

resolving

the

underlying

structural

mismatch.

This principle explains: ● the expansion of AI in public administration ● the persistence of automated decision systems despite known failures ● increasing reliance on intermediated governance structures ● the coexistence of technological scaling and institutional strain

8. Canonical Synthesis Institutions do not collapse; they drift. As environmental complexity exceeds processing capacity,

hidden

buffers

are

progressively

extracted

to

maintain

the

appearance

functionality.

When

these

At

compensatory

stabilizing

underlying

structural

Simultaneously,

system-layer

AI

system

they

governance

persists

has

lost

the

ability

to

govern

complexity

directly.

9. Conclusion The emergence of AI in governance systems correlates strongly with increasing institutional complexity

Rather

Understanding
