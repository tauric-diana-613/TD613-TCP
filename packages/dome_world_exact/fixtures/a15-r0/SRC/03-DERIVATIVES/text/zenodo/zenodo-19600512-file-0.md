**From Occupation Tech to Canadian Streets:

How Military ‑ Grade AI Recreates Carding Through Biometric Surveillance** SignalRupture

(2026)

Abstract This paper argues that contemporary biometric surveillance does not introduce a new form of policing—it

automates

an

existing

one.

Military ‑ grade

facial ‑ recognition

systems,

developed

in

asymmetric

conflict

zones,

are

now

deployed

in

Canadian

and

American

cities,

where

they

reproduce

the

structural

logic

of

carding

without

requiring

interaction

(Amnesty

International,

2023;

Citizen

Lab,

2024).

The result is a shift from discretionary identity checks to continuous, ambient identification. While

constitutional

and

Charter

protections

remain

formally

intact,

the

infrastructural

conditions

that

once

triggered

those

protections

are

systematically

bypassed

(Office

of

the

Privacy

Commissioner

of

Canada,

2021).

This

produces

a

rights ‑ neutralizing

environment

in

which

surveillance

operates

without

suspicion,

consent,

or

awareness.

Biometric surveillance is not an escalation of policing—it is the disappearance of the moment where

policing

becomes

visible.

1. Introduction — The Disappearance of the

Stop

Carding was dismantled because it was visible. It required an officer, a stop, a demand, and a record—each

a

point

of

friction

that

could

be

challenged

(Ontario

Human

Rights

Commission,

2018).

Facial recognition removes the stop.

There is no interaction. No

demand.

No

refusal.

No

moment

that

can

be

contested.

Identification no longer occurs as an event. It

becomes

an

environment.

Biometric surveillance does not replace carding—it completes it.

2. Origin — Surveillance Built for Asymmetry

Systems such as those developed by Corsight AI were designed for environments where identification

must

be

instantaneous,

consent

irrelevant,

and

refusal

impossible

(Amnesty

International,

2023).

These systems were deployed in contexts of population control, where one group is fully visible and

the

other

holds

total

informational

power.

This is surveillance optimized for asymmetry.

3. Migration — From Conflict Zone to Civilian

Street

When these systems enter Canadian and American policing, they do not change their logic—only

their

setting.

The Edmonton Police Service pilot demonstrated this shift: real ‑ time facial recognition via body cameras,

passive

scanning

in

public

space,

and

automated

comparison

against

centralized

watchlists

(Citizen

Lab,

2024).

This is not an investigative tool. It

is

an

ambient

identification

system.

Governance does not precede deployment. It

follows

it—if

at

all.

4. Carding — The Original Infrastructure Carding functioned through selective visibility. Police decided who became legible. It disproportionately targeted Black, Indigenous, and racialized communities (Toronto Star, 2017; Cole,

2019).

Its power came from discretion. Its

limitation

came

from

friction:

interaction

could

be

challenged.

5. Structural Continuity — From Discretion to

Automation

Facial recognition removes discretion at the point of interaction and embeds it upstream in the system.

Carding: “Identify yourself.” Biometric

surveillance:

“You

are

already

identified.”

Both systems: ● produce identity without cause ● disproportionately affect racialized populations ● generate databases of non ‑ criminal individuals ● expand surveillance beyond legal thresholds The difference is mechanical, not ethical. Carding required a decision. Biometric

surveillance

requires

only

presence.

This is not reform. This

is

completion.

6. Rights Without Triggers — The Mechanism

of

Circumvention

Legal protections are event ‑ based. They activate when something happens: a search, a detention,

a

demand.

Biometric surveillance avoids events. No stop → no detention No

detention

→

no

rights

triggered

No

trigger

→

no

violation

to

contest

Rights are not removed—they are never activated. Canadian Charter (Supreme Court of Canada, 2004; 2009; 2014) ● Section 8 (Search): no recognized “search event.” ● Section 9 (Detention): no formal detention. ● Section 10 (Counsel): no trigger point. ● Section 15 (Equality): bias embedded upstream. ● Section 2(b), 2(d): chilled before exercised. U.S. Constitution (Carpenter v. United States, 2018; Katz v. United States, 1967)

● Fourth Amendment: no warrant ‑ triggering event. ● First Amendment: participation becomes traceable. ● Fourteenth Amendment: bias encoded in systems. ● Fifth Amendment: no visible process to challenge. The architecture does not violate rights. It

renders

them

procedurally

irrelevant.

8. Governance Hypocrisy — Importing the Logic

Western governments publicly reject occupation practices and population ‑ control technologies, yet

systems

in

2023).

The contradiction is structural: The same systems deemed unacceptable abroad become normalized at home once reframed

This is not policy drift. It

9. AI Governance — Regulating the Future While

Failing

the

Present

Institutions focus on hypothetical AI risks while ignoring deployed systems that already erode rights

(EFF,

2023).

They regulate: ● generative AI ● platform visibility ● speculative harms While neglecting: ● biometric surveillance ● police adoption ● the erosion of anonymity This is a governance inversion: future

risks

are

present

are

Children are framed as needing protection from digital platforms while growing up inside environments

They are not entering a risky future. They

10. Conclusion — The End of Refusal Carding allowed one final act of agency: refusal. Biometric surveillance removes even that. You cannot refuse what does not ask. You

cannot

what

does

not

You

rights

that

This is the final shift: from contested identification to irreversible recognition. Public space becomes a place where you are continuously resolved into data. Not occasionally stopped. Not

But
