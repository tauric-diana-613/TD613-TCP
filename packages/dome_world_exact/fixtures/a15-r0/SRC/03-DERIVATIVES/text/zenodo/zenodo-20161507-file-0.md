6. Methods 6.1 Data Sources The analysis draws on institutional datasets including: ● Statistics Canada ● United States Census Bureau ● Bureau of Labor Statistics ● Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation ● Canadian Institute for Health Information ● OECD ● Federal Reserve Board These datasets were selected because they measure structural conditions rather than subjective

attitudes.

6.2 Computational Framework Three primary computational constructs were used: 1. Agency Realization Ratio (ARR) 2. Quiet Governance Friction Index (QGFI) 3. Deviation Capacity (DC) Each construct was normalized to a 0–1 interpretive scale to enable cross-domain comparison.

6.3 Household Modeling Household archetypes were constructed using median values derived from national datasets. Each archetype was evaluated across: ● housing burden, ● healthcare access, ● debt load, ● time scarcity, ● mobility constraints, ● and cognitive load indicators. ARR and QGFI were then computed to estimate executable agency retention.

6.4 Population-Level Estimation Population-level agency estimates were derived by: 1. segmenting households into income bands, 2. applying ARR thresholds, 3. weighting by population share, 4. and aggregating nationally. The resulting approximation suggests that only approximately 35–40% of individuals retain stable

executable

agency

conditions.

6.5 Limitations

The framework intentionally prioritizes structural determinism over individual-level psychological variation.

The model does not claim: ● exact predictive precision, ● universal cultural applicability, ● or deterministic behavioral outcomes. Rather, SignalRupture provides a heuristic systems framework for modeling directional relationships

between

friction,

adaptive

capacity,

and

recursive

degradation.

7. Policy Implications 7.1 Access Without Bandwidth Is Not Agency Most Western policy frameworks measure service access rather than executable usability. SignalRupture argues that: access without bandwidth is not agency. Policy must therefore evaluate: ● time cost, ● cognitive load, ● procedural friction, ● administrative burden, ● and degradation rates.

7.2 Reduce Friction, Not Only Spending Increasing funding without reducing friction frequently produces administrative inflation rather than

adaptive

improvement.

High-impact friction reductions include: ● automatic enrollment, ● simplified eligibility systems,

● integrated service portals, ● reduced verification redundancy, ● and single-touch applications.

7.3 Time Is a Policy Variable Temporal scarcity is among the strongest predictors of agency compression. Policy interventions should therefore prioritize: ● predictable scheduling, ● reduced commuting burdens, ● streamlined institutional navigation, ● and recovery time preservation.

7.4 Housing as the Primary Agency Determinant Housing burden appears to function as the strongest structural predictor of ARR degradation. Policy responses may include: ● vacancy stabilization, ● non-market housing expansion, ● rent-to-income limitations, ● and anti-speculation measures. Housing policy therefore functions as agency policy.

7.5 Measuring Quiet Governance Governments rarely measure friction directly despite friction functioning as a primary mechanism

of

institutional

control.

SignalRupture proposes: ● national QGFI measurement, ● friction audits, ● and annual friction-reduction targets.

7.6 Agency as a National Metric GDP, inflation, and unemployment rates do not adequately measure whether populations retain executable

adaptive

capacity.

SignalRupture therefore proposes a national Agency Index integrating: ● ARR, ● QGFI, ● deviation capacity, ● and structural burden indicators.
