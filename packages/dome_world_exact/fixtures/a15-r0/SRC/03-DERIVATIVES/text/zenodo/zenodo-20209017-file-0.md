1. Introduction: Silos as an Institutional Experiment Disciplinary silos are often treated as natural categories—physics, psychology, sociology, biology,

economics.

In

reality,

they

are

institutional

constructs

that

emerged

to

manage

the

expansion

of

knowledge

in

the

19th

and

20th

centuries.

They

function

as

an

experiment

in

complexity

management:

divide

knowledge

into

smaller

units,

assign

experts

to

each

unit,

and

assume

coherence

will

emerge

from

the

sum

of

the

parts.

Evidence

from

peer ‑ review

failures

and

publishing

practices

shows

that

this

experiment

has

produced

fragmentation

rather

than

integration

(The

Conversation,

2025).

2. How Silos Produce Fragmentation

Silos create fragmentation by enforcing boundaries around methods, vocabularies, and acceptable

research

questions.

Peer

review

reinforces

these

boundaries

by

selecting

reviewers from narrow subfields, who then evaluate work based on discipline ‑ specific norms (Phys.org, 2025). This produces: ● incompatible conceptual frameworks ● methodological isolation ● resistance to cross ‑ domain work ● epistemic drift within each silo Fragmentation is not a side effect—it is the structural output of the silo experiment. 3. Publishing as a Silo ‑ Reinforcement Mechanism Academic publishing amplifies silos by organizing journals around disciplines and subdisciplines.

Manuscripts

that

cross

boundaries

are

often

rejected

as

“out

of

scope,”

regardless

of

their

coherence

or

empirical

value

(Canadian

Journal

of

Cardiology,

2025).

This

creates

a

feedback

loop:

● disciplines define journals ● journals define legitimacy ● legitimacy defines careers ● careers reinforce disciplines Publishing does not merely reflect silos—it produces and stabilizes them. 4. Silos and Epistemic Drift Epistemic drift occurs when evaluation criteria diverge from the phenomena they are meant to explain.

Silos

accelerate

drift

by

narrowing

the

criteria

used

to

judge

research.

The

Independent

Institute

documents

how

retractions

often

stem

from

methodological

failures

that

arise

within

isolated

subfields

(Independent

Institute,

2026).

When

each

silo

develops

its

own

standards,

the

system

loses

the

ability

to

evaluate

coherence

across

domains.

5. Silos as Barriers to Integration Modern problems—climate change, AI governance, public health, economic instability—are cross ‑ domain

by

nature.

Silos

prevent

institutions

from

integrating

knowledge

across

fields.

Springer’s

analysis

of

post ‑ publication

review

platforms

shows

that

even

error

is

siloed,

with

limited

for

cross ‑ disciplinary

scrutiny

(Springer,

2026).

Institutions

cannot

integrate

because

their

architecture

is

built

on

fragmentation.

6. The Illusion of Expertise Silos create the illusion that expertise is stable and domain ‑ bounded. In reality, expertise becomes

increasingly

narrow

as

fields

specialize.

This

produces:

● experts who cannot see beyond their subfield ● incompatible interpretations of shared phenomena ● institutional paralysis when problems cross boundaries The Conversation highlights how reviewer selection reinforces this illusion by privileging narrow expertise

over

integrative

capacity

(The

Conversation,

2025).

7. Silos as Legitimacy Structures Silos function as legitimacy structures: they define what counts as knowledge, who counts as an expert,

and

which

methods

are

acceptable.

Publishing,

peer

review,

and

institutional

hierarchies

all

depend

on

these

boundaries.

But

legitimacy

is

not

coherence.

Silos

produce

legitimacy

through

compliance,

not

through

structural

integration.

8. The Cascade: How Exposing Silos Exposes the Next Layer

Once the silo experiment is exposed, the next layers become visible: ● Indexing systems inherit silo categories and reproduce fragmentation. ● Citation economies reward silo ‑ specific prestige rather than cross ‑ domain coherence. ● Evidence ‑ based policy inherits incompatible bodies of knowledge. ● Institutional legitimacy inherits the illusion that silos map onto reality. Silos are not neutral—they are the architecture of institutional incoherence.
