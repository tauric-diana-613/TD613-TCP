The Indexing Experiment: How Retrieval Systems

Became

the

Architecture

of

Visibility

Abstract Indexing systems—Google Scholar, PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus—are widely treated as neutral

infrastructures

for

and

Yet

built

on

the

assumptions

of

peer

review,

academic

publishing,

and

disciplinary

silos.

This

essay

indexing

systems

inherit

the

of

the

layers

beneath

them:

epistemic

drift,

category

lock ‑ in,

prestige

bias,

and

fragmentation.

Evidence

from

retraction

patterns,

compromised

peer ‑ review

processes,

and

the

economics

of

publishing

shows

that

indexing

systems

reproduce

flawed

knowledge

at

scale

while

obscuring

structural

incoherence.

By

examining

indexing

as

an

experiment

in

visibility

governance,

we

reveal

how

it

shapes

what

becomes

thinkable,

citable,

and

institutionally

real.

Exposing

this

layer

reveals

the

fragility

of

citation

economies,

evidence ‑ based

policy,

and

1. Introduction: Indexing as Visibility Governance Indexing systems are often framed as neutral tools for organizing scientific literature. In reality, they

are

visibility

infrastructures—systems

that

determine

what

knowledge

becomes

discoverable,

citable,

and

institutionally

real.

Indexing

is

built

on

the

outputs

of

peer

review

and

academic

publishing,

meaning

it

inherits

their

structural

weaknesses.

When

peer

review

fails

or

publishing

distributes

flawed

work,

indexing

systems

replicate

those

failures

at

scale

(The

Conversation,

2025).

Indexing is not a mirror of knowledge. Indexing is a constructor of visibility.

2. The Architecture of Inheritance: How Indexing Absorbs Upstream

Failures

Indexing systems depend on peer ‑ reviewed journals, publisher metadata, disciplinary categories,

and

citation

structures.

Because

these

inputs

are

structurally

fragile,

indexing

becomes

fragile

by

design.

Compromised

peer

review

allows

flawed

papers

into

the

literature (Phys.org, 2025). Once published, indexing systems ingest them automatically, granting them visibility and legitimacy. Indexing does not evaluate truth. Indexing evaluates metadata. 3. Category Lock ‑ In: When Indexing Freezes Institutional Ontologies

Indexing systems rely on disciplinary categories to classify knowledge. These categories are inherited

from

publishing

and

peer

review,

which

means

they

reflect

institutional

convenience

rather

than

empirical

reality.

Post ‑ publication

review

analyses

show

that

even

error

correction

is

constrained

by

siloed

categories

and

metadata

structures

(Springer,

2026).

This produces rigid taxonomies, outdated ontologies, conceptual blind spots, and structural invisibility

for

cross ‑ domain

work.

Indexing

does

not

merely

reflect

silos—it

hardens

them.

4. The Illusion of Comprehensiveness Indexing systems create the illusion that they capture the totality of scientific knowledge. In practice,

they

capture

what

publishers

index,

what

journals

accept,

what

silos

define

as

legitimate,

and

what

metadata

systems

can

classify.

Retraction

data

shows

that

indexing

systems

to

surface

retracted

or

compromised

long

their

legitimacy

has

collapsed

(Independent

Institute,

2026).

Indexing is not comprehensive—it is selectively incomplete. 5. Prestige Bias Embedded in Retrieval Algorithms Indexing systems privilege high ‑ impact journals, frequently cited papers, established authors, and

institutional

affiliations.

This

reproduces

the

prestige

economy

of

publishing.

Prestige

and

profit

distort

evaluation

and

visibility

(Canadian

Journal

of

Cardiology,

2025).

Retrieval

these

distortions

by

based

on

citation

counts,

journal

impact,

and

historical

visibility.

Prestige becomes a retrieval signal.

6. Indexing as a Legitimacy Multiplier Once a paper is indexed, it becomes citable, discoverable, institutionally recognized, and part of the

scientific

record.

This

means

indexing

multiplies

the

legitimacy

of

whatever

Thousands

retracted

(The

Conversation,

2025).

Indexing does not correct errors. Indexing propagates them. 7. The Failure of Post ‑ Publication Correction Even when papers are retracted, indexing systems often fail to update metadata, continue to surface

retracted

work,

leave

citation

trails

intact,

and

allow

flawed

research

to

downstream

Post ‑ publication

review

show

that

correction

slow,

silo ‑ bound

(Springer,

2026).

Indexing systems are structurally incapable of maintaining epistemic integrity. 8. The Cascade: How Exposing Indexing Exposes the Next

Layer

Once the indexing layer is exposed, the next layers become visible: citation economies inherit indexing’s

prestige

inherits

institutional

visibility

Indexing is not neutral—it is the architecture of institutional visibility.
