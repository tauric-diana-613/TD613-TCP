1. Introduction Modern institutions operate fundamentally as information systems. They receive behavioural input

from

human

populations,

translate

those

inputs

into

administratively

recognizable

categories,

and

generate

outputs

capable

of

sustaining

governance

continuity.

Within this process, substantial transformation occurs. Human behaviour is inherently high-dimensional. Individuals exist within layered environmental, psychological,

economic,

medical,

temporal,

and

social

conditions

that

interact

dynamically

across

time.

Yet

institutional

systems

cannot

operationally

process

unlimited

behavioural

complexity.

Institutions

therefore

reduce

complexity

into

simplified

administrative

representations

capable

of

fitting

procedural

frameworks.

This reduction is not incidental. It is necessary for institutional scalability. Emergency departments require triage codes. Welfare systems require eligibility categories. Corrections

systems

require

behavioural

classifications.

Schools

require

disciplinary

codification.

Police

systems

require

incident

summaries.

Psychiatry

requires

diagnostic

abstraction.

Across domains, institutional survival depends on converting unstable human complexity into administratively

stable

representation.

The SignalRupture framework conceptualizes this transformation as a measurable process rather

than

an

anecdotal

observation.

SR proposes that institutions systematically transform behavioural reality through three major governance

stages:

1. Behavioural complexity generation (GA 1) 2. Administrative compression and abstraction (GA 3) 3. Governance stabilization and oversight normalization (GA 4) The present paper examines whether this transformation pattern appears consistently across multiple

unrelated

institutional

domains.

The core hypothesis is not merely that institutions occasionally misunderstand individuals. Rather,

the

hypothesis

is

that

institutional

architectures

systematically

compress

behavioural

reality

as

an

operational

property

of

governance

itself.

2. Methods This study applies the SignalRupture framework across ten institutional domains using publicly accessible

reports,

tribunal

decisions,

oversight

investigations,

coroner

reviews,

emergency

system

and

administrative

governance

The study design is comparative and cross-domainal. Cases were selected to maximize institutional

diversity

while

preserving

a

common

informational

structure:

● frontline behavioural signal ● institutional interpretation ● oversight review or administrative outcome Each case therefore contains: ● F-text (frontline behavioural narrative; Governance Altitude 1) ● I-text (administrative summary; Governance Altitude 3) ● oversight or governance interpretation (Governance Altitude 4) ● F′ behavioural coding ● I′ administrative coding ● metric computation The analysis focuses not on moral judgment but on structural transformation. The study does not attempt to determine whether institutions are ethically good or bad. Instead, it

measures

how

behavioural

complexity

changes

as

it

moves

through

governance

architectures.

The objective was to determine whether SR distortion metrics remain stable across unrelated governance

environments.

2.1 Operationalization Rules To improve methodological consistency, SR metrics were operationalized through structured coding

procedures.

F-text units were segmented into behavioural signal units representing: ● contextual detail ● behavioural continuity ● environmental constraint ● emotional state ● crisis indicators ● temporal sequencing ● social conditions ● survival behaviour ● adaptive response patterns I-text units were segmented into administratively legible categories including: ● eligibility determinations ● compliance descriptors ● procedural outcomes ● risk classifications ● disposition summaries ● behavioural labels ● diagnostic abstraction ● institutional status categories Signal Compression Ratio (SCR) was computed as: SCR = \frac{\text{F-text signal units}}{\text{I-text administrative units}} Administrative Abstraction Index (AAI) measured the proportional dominance of procedural abstraction

over

contextual

behavioural

detail

within

institutional

text

layers.

Ground-Truth Discrepancy Index (GTDI) was computed as: GTDI = 1 - \frac{I'}{F'} Higher GTDI values indicate greater divergence between behavioural complexity and institutional

representation.

The SR framework does not claim direct access to objective reality. Rather, it measures divergence

between

frontline

behavioural

complexity

and

institutional

representation

within

administratively

mediated

systems.

2.2 Governance Altitude Model

The Governance Altitude (GA) framework conceptualizes institutions as layered interpretive systems.

GA 1 — Frontline Behavioural Reality This layer contains: ● direct behavioural narratives ● lived conditions ● crisis interactions ● environmental instability ● contextual complexity GA 1 represents maximum signal density.

GA 2 — Operational Mediation This layer includes: ● frontline staff interpretation ● case management filtering ● incident documentation ● triage coding ● institutional intake processing

GA 3 — Administrative Compression At GA 3, behavioural complexity is transformed into: ● categories ● procedural summaries ● eligibility outcomes ● risk classifications ● audit-compatible representations This layer produces the primary institutional abstraction.

GA 4 — Oversight Stabilization Oversight bodies partially restore behavioural context while stabilizing institutional legitimacy. Examples include: ● ombudsman investigations ● coroner reviews ● tribunal analysis ● inspector reports ● external audits Importantly, oversight systems rarely dismantle compression structures themselves. Instead, they

frame

distortions

as

procedural

irregularities

or

documentation

deficiencies

while

preserving

overall

governance

architecture.

5. Cross-Domain Analysis 5.1 Signal Compression (SCR) SCR values range from 4.11 to 6.19 across all institutional domains. This indicates that frontline behavioural narratives consistently contain approximately four to six times

more

informational

density

than

institutional

summaries.

The pattern demonstrates several structural characteristics: ● behavioural continuity is truncated ● environmental context is minimized ● temporal complexity is reduced ● adaptive survival behaviour is compressed ● institutional summaries prioritize procedural relevance Importantly, this pattern remains stable despite radical differences between domains. The same compression structure appears in: ● homelessness governance ● emergency medicine ● corrections

● education ● welfare systems ● psychiatric systems This suggests that signal compression is not domain-specific but architecturally recurrent. Institutions compress complexity because throughput systems cannot fully process high-dimensional

behavioural

information

at

scale.

5.2 Administrative Abstraction (AAI) AAI values cluster between 2.0 and 3.0 across all cases. This indicates heavy procedural abstraction throughout institutional systems. High abstraction environments are characterized by: ● eligibility logic ● procedural coding ● diagnostic categorization ● behavioural labeling ● compliance frameworks ● risk scoring ● throughput optimization As AAI increases: ● contextual richness declines ● administrative language density increases ● behavioural nuance becomes secondary to institutional legibility The highest AAI values appear in: ● welfare eligibility systems ● inpatient psychiatry ● overdose response governance These systems rely heavily on formal categorization structures and procedural standardization. The data suggest that abstraction functions as a stabilizing mechanism for large governance systems

operating

under

scarcity

conditions.

5.3 Ground-Truth Discrepancy (GTDI) GTDI values range from 0.53 to 0.62. These values indicate substantial divergence between behavioural complexity and institutional representation.

High GTDI systems demonstrate: ● loss of contextual continuity ● reduction of behavioural dimensionality ● conversion of crisis behaviour into administrative categories ● substitution of procedural representation for lived reality The highest GTDI values appear in: ● inpatient psychiatry ● corrections ● overdose governance These systems process highly unstable behavioural conditions under elevated throughput pressure

and

liability

constraints.

The consistency of GTDI values across domains strongly suggests that Ground-Truth Discrepancy

is

a

structural

feature

of

governance

architectures

rather

than

an

isolated

institutional

anomaly.

6. Meta-Governance Interpretation Across all ten cases, the same structural chain appears repeatedly: 1. Behavioural complexity generation (GA 1) 2. → Administrative mediation and compression (GA 2–3) 3. → Governance stabilization and normalization (GA 4) This chain appears regardless of institutional purpose. Whether the system concerns: ● housing ● medicine ● policing

● psychiatry ● corrections ● education ● welfare ● addiction response the architecture remains remarkably similar. Institutions do not primarily preserve behavioural reality. They preserve governance continuity. To accomplish this, institutions must transform behavioural instability into administratively manageable

representation.

Oversight systems partially restore behavioural context but rarely destabilize the compression architecture

itself.

Instead, oversight systems frequently frame distortion as: ● documentation failure ● procedural inconsistency ● communication breakdown ● isolated operational deficiency rather than as structural properties of governance design. The SR framework therefore interprets institutional distortion not as accidental malfunction but as

emergent

behaviour

within

large

administrative

systems

operating

under

constraint.

7. Canonical SR Interpretation Across ten institutional domains, SR metrics reveal a structurally consistent pattern: Behavioural reality enters governance systems as high-dimensional, context-laden signal and is progressively

transformed

into

Institutions do not simply misunderstand individuals. They reformat behavioural reality into governance-compatible forms.

The reproducibility of SCR, AAI, and GTDI across unrelated public systems demonstrates that institutional

Oversight systems partially restore complexity while simultaneously stabilizing institutional legitimacy

The SR framework therefore proposes that Ground-Truth Discrepancy is not merely a social phenomenon

8. Limitations The present study has several limitations. First, SR coding procedures remain partially manual and interpretive. Although operationalization

rules

were

standardized,

future

work

should

incorporate

inter-rater

reliability

testing

and

automated

linguistic

analysis.

Second, the study relies on selective public-domain institutional documents. These materials vary

in

granularity,

completeness,

and

reporting

style

across

domains.

Third, institutional reporting systems themselves may already contain compression effects prior to

SR

analysis.

Consequently,

measured

distortion

likely

represents

downstream

institutional

abstraction

rather

than

total

behavioural

divergence.

Fourth, the SR framework does not claim direct access to objective reality. Instead, it measures divergence

between

behavioural

complexity

and

institutional

representation

within

administratively

mediated

systems.

Finally, the present study is cross-sectional rather than longitudinal. Future SR work should examine:

● temporal drift accumulation ● institutional degradation trajectories ● comparative international governance systems ● automated text compression analysis ● large-scale narrative mapping ● predictive institutional strain modeling These limitations do not invalidate the framework but identify the next stage of methodological development.

9. Conclusion The collapse of ten SR empirical case studies into a unified comparative analysis demonstrates a

highly

consistent

institutional

pattern

across

multiple

governance

systems.

Across all domains examined: ● behavioural complexity is compressed ● administrative abstraction increases ● contextual fidelity declines ● governance stabilization overrides representational completeness The consistency of SR metrics across unrelated institutional environments suggests that Ground-Truth

Discrepancy

is

not

exceptional

but

structural.

The findings support the interpretation that modern public institutions function as compression architectures

optimized

for:

● throughput management ● procedural standardization ● audit stability ● governance continuity ● administrative legibility rather than full preservation of behavioural complexity. The SR framework therefore contributes not merely a critique of institutional systems but a generalized

theory

of

institutional

signal

processing.

Future work should focus on methodological hardening, expanded datasets, automated coding architectures,

and

longitudinal

institutional

analysis.

The central implication of SR is ultimately systemic: Institutional distortion is not primarily a failure of individual actors. It is an emergent property of governance

architectures

attempting

to

stabilize

complexity

under

conditions

of

finite

administrative

capacity.
