INTRODUCTION: RELIGION MEETS INFRASTRUCTURAL CAUSATION

Religion emerged within environments where suffering was more readily interpreted through interpersonal

action,

divine

intervention,

environmental

uncertainty,

or

visible

authority.

Its explanatory grammar evolved around categories of: ● responsibility, ● virtue, ● temptation, ● obedience, ● justice, ● redemption, ● moral consequence. These categories remain powerful. But modern infrastructures introduce forms of harm that increasingly exceed person-centered causation.

SignalRupture proposes that contemporary societies operate through infrastructures whose defaults,

incentives,

and

interdependencies

shape

outcomes

more

consistently

than

visible

authority.

The Society Blueprint summarizes this shift: “Modern societies are increasingly organized through infrastructures whose outputs structure social

possibility.”

Whether one accepts that statement fully or partially, it introduces an explanatory challenge. If suffering increasingly emerges through architecture rather than intention, then moral interpretation

alone

becomes

insufficient.

This paper therefore asks a narrower question than theology itself: Can agency-centered religious explanations maintain causal explanatory coverage under conditions

of

distributed

infrastructural

harm?

This question should not be interpreted as anti-religious. Religion may continue to provide: ● existential meaning, ● ethical orientation, ● community formation, ● symbolic interpretation, ● practices of endurance. The issue under examination is causal precision. A framework may remain spiritually meaningful while becoming diagnostically incomplete.

SignalRupture’s position is therefore not that religion becomes false. It proposes something narrower: As infrastructures become more dominant producers of outcomes, explanatory systems calibrated

primarily

toward

agency

become

progressively

less

complete

for

explaining

harm.

This introduces the central concept of the paper: Theological Blindness Theological Blindness is defined as: the structural reduction in explanatory coverage that occurs when agency-centered interpretive

systems

encounter

harms

generated

predominantly

through

distributed

infrastructures.

This concept does not replace theology. It establishes its scope conditions. The sections that follow examine whether contemporary harm remains primarily moral—or whether

architecture

has

become

the

more

predictive

explanatory

The

The central question is: When harm emerges primarily through infrastructures rather than identifiable actors, which

The analysis proceeds through five methodological layers: ● structural analysis, ● diagnostic analysis, ● integrative analysis, ● post-institutional analysis,

● recursive analysis. These layers do not replace religious interpretation; they operate as comparative instruments for identifying

where

explanatory

expands

3.1 Structural Analysis Structural analysis asks: What arrangement produced the outcome? Rather than beginning with individual decisions, structural analysis begins with environments, constraints,

and

architectures.

Key questions include: ● What incentive structure operated? ● What procedural constraints existed? ● What dependencies shaped behavior? ● Which outputs became predictable? Religious interpretation frequently asks: ● Who acted? ● Who deviated? ● Who fulfilled obligations? ● Who failed? Structural analysis asks instead: What environment made those outcomes more likely? These questions may coexist, but they prioritize different levels of explanation.

3.2 Diagnostic Analysis Diagnostic analysis evaluates mechanisms. Its objective is causal tracing rather than moral evaluation.

Illustrative infrastructural mechanisms include: ● procedural burden, ● pricing architecture, ● algorithmic filtering, ● administrative density,

● supply-chain fragility, ● information overload, ● incentive misalignment. Illustrative religious mechanisms include: ● temptation, ● obligation, ● moral choice, ● ethical failure, ● spiritual interpretation. The purpose is not to invalidate moral categories but to determine whether those categories identify

the

operating

mechanism.

Meaning is not equivalent to causation. A moral explanation may preserve meaning while underdescribing mechanism.

3.3 Integrative Analysis Modern harms increasingly cross institutional boundaries: ● A housing problem becomes a health problem. ● A health problem becomes a labor problem. ● A labor problem becomes a financial problem. ● A financial problem becomes a psychological problem. Traditional institutional analysis often fragments these domains. SR proposes integration across systems. Illustrative domains include: ● food systems, ● education, ● healthcare, ● governance, ● law, ● banking, ● media, ● digital infrastructure, ● human capacity.

Religious explanatory systems frequently integrate meaning across domains. SR attempts to integrate

causation

across

domains.

These are distinct forms of integration.

3.4 Post-Institutional Analysis Post-institutional analysis begins from the assumption that many outcomes exceed the direct control

of

any

single

institution.

Outcomes increasingly emerge from: ● coordination effects, ● procedural accumulation, ● automated decisions, ● infrastructure interaction. Under these conditions, responsibility becomes layered rather than singular. The analytical focus shifts from: Who governs? toward: What produces?

3.5 Recursive Analysis Recursive analysis examines feedback loops. Human erosion is treated simultaneously as: ● output of conditions, ● input into future outcomes. Examples include: ● administrative exhaustion reducing participation, ● reduced participation limiting access, ● limited access increasing instability, ● instability producing further exhaustion. Within SR, individuals are neither fully autonomous nor fully determined. They operate within recursive

environments.

This distinction becomes critical for understanding agency.

5. THE SR–RELIGION TENSION: AGENCY UNDER CONDITIONS

OF

CONSTRAINT

The deepest difference between SR and many religious explanatory systems concerns agency. Many religious traditions assume that moral life requires meaningful human action. SR does not reject agency; it proposes that agency is conditional. Agency becomes constrained by: ● time, ● access, ● resources, ● information, ● institutional friction, ● environmental load. The Agency Myth introduces a distinction between: Formal Agency: theoretical ability to choose. Practical Agency: real capacity to realize choices. This distinction changes interpretation. Deviation from ideals may no longer indicate unwillingness; it may indicate constraint. A person may fail to: ● follow recommendations, ● access treatment, ● maintain routines, ● participate fully, not because of moral deficiency— but because required conditions are absent. This creates explanatory divergence. Religious interpretation often emphasizes choice . SR emphasizes available action space . The claim is not that agency disappears. The claim is that agency becomes environmentally dependent.

5.1 The Agency Realization Ratio (ARR) SR proposes the Agency Realization Ratio (ARR) as a conceptual heuristic. ARR is defined as: Practical outcomes achievable / formally available choices. ARR is not presented as an empirically validated universal metric; it is a diagnostic construct. Its purpose is to measure divergence between theoretical freedom and executable freedom. As ARR declines: ● responsibility becomes harder to isolate, ● intervention shifts upstream, ● moral interpretation loses precision. This introduces the next section: whether responsibility itself changes under infrastructural causation.

7. ACCOUNTABILITY UNDER DISTRIBUTED CAUSATION Traditional accountability models assume four conditions: ● identifiable actor, ● visible action, ● observable consequence, ● assignable responsibility. Infrastructural environments increasingly violate these assumptions. No single participant may: ● control the outcome, ● understand the system, ● possess corrective authority. Yet harm still occurs. This creates a paradox: Responsibility exists. Control fragments. SR describes this as Distributed Responsibility . Distributed Responsibility does not eliminate ethics. It expands the object of responsibility. Questions

Who caused this? toward: What arrangement repeatedly produces this? This shift changes intervention. Moral repair often emphasizes: ● repentance, ● accountability, ● behavior change. Structural repair emphasizes: ● redesign, ● simplification, ● resilience, ● transparency, ● incentive correction.

The approaches are complementary but not interchangeable.

8. CASE ANALYSIS: MEDICATION SCARCITY Consider a patient unable to obtain prescribed treatment. The question is not: Who failed? The question is: What produced the shortage? A person-centered model may emphasize:

● responsibility, ● compliance, ● individual decision, ● provider behavior. These may matter, but they rarely explain the full event. An infrastructural model asks: ● Were reimbursement structures distorted? ● Did procurement incentives change? ● Were administrative barriers introduced? ● Did supply dependencies accumulate? ● Were coordination mechanisms fragile? Each answer alters intervention. The harm may emerge despite: ● competent clinicians, ● responsible patients, ● ethical intentions. No actor intends deprivation. Yet deprivation appears. The system produces scarcity. This distinction demonstrates the paper’s central claim: causal precision increasingly requires

stability

through

architectural

causation;

contemporary

harm

emerges

through

distributed,

algorithmic,

and

administrative

architectures

that

theological

frameworks

cannot

fully

diagnose.

11. CONCLUSION: FROM MORAL SUFFERING TO ARCHITECTURAL

SUFFERING

The central argument of this paper is not that religion becomes obsolete. It is that explanatory environments

change.

Religion

emerged

within

conditions

where

suffering

could

be

interpreted

through

visible

action,

moral

choice,

and

legible

responsibility.

Under

those

historical

conditions,

religious

systems—including

Anglicanism—often

functioned

as

infrastructures

of

social

order,

producing

stability

through

moral

regulation,

identity

formation,

and

governance

architecture

(Thompson,

1963;

Asad,

2003;

Hempton,

1996).

Modern infrastructures operate differently. Contemporary harm increasingly emerges through distributed

causation,

recursive

constraints,

procedural

accumulation,

and

socio ‑ technical

interdependence.

These

environments

generate

outcomes

that

exceed

individual

intention

and

resist

person ‑ centered

explanation.

Under

such

conditions,

agency ‑ centered

interpretation

becomes

less

complete.

Religion continues to provide meaning, hope, ethics, and endurance. These functions remain vital.

But

infrastructure

analysis

increasingly

explains

mechanism,

prediction,

distribution,

and

the reproduction of harm. These explanatory layers need not compete, but they should not be confused.

Modern suffering increasingly exceeds categories centered on sin, intention, or personal deviation.

Understanding

contemporary

harm

requires

analyzing

architecture

alongside

interpreting

persons.

This

is

the

Infrastructural

Incompatibility

Thesis:

as

infrastructural

causation

becomes

dominant,

frameworks

calibrated

primarily

toward

agency

lose

causal

coverage

over

system ‑ generated

harm.
