In the modern infrastructural environment, this assumption no longer holds. 2. State Capacity and Institutional Strength Contemporary political economy expanded Weberian theory by emphasizing state capacity , defined

● Extraction capacity — ability to raise revenue ● Coercive capacity — ability to enforce rules ● Implementation capacity — ability to deliver programs These models treat capacity as an internal property of the state. They assume that the state’s ability

3. Autonomy and Embeddedness Theories of state autonomy —from Skocpol to Evans—argue that states possess independent preferences

● Autonomous states — capable of independent action ● Embedded states — integrated with societal actors These frameworks focus on interest groups, political coalitions, and institutional design. They do not

Infrastructure Dependency (ID) Infrastructure Dependency refers to the degree to which government operations rely on infrastructures

owned

or

controlled

by

external

operators.

Dependency

is

not

merely

technical;

it

is

operational,

institutional,

and

strategic.

A

government

is

dependent

when:

● essential services require external infrastructures ● continuity of operations relies on private systems ● crisis response is mediated by operator cooperation ● substitution is costly, slow, or infeasible High dependency reduces agency because the state cannot act without the cooperation or stability

of

external

systems.

Substitution Capacity (SC) Substitution Capacity measures the ability of a government to replace, bypass, or reconfigure infrastructures

when

operators

fail,

resist,

or

impose

constraints.

Substitution

can

take

multiple

forms:

● technical substitution (alternative networks or platforms) ● operational substitution (manual or parallel systems) ● institutional substitution (public alternatives or emergency powers) Low substitution capacity amplifies dependency. High substitution capacity mitigates it.

Regulatory Leverage (RL) Regulatory Leverage captures the degree of influence government can exert over infrastructural operators

through:

● legislation ● oversight ● compliance mechanisms ● enforcement powers ● licensing and sectoral regulation Regulatory leverage is not equivalent to ownership. A government may regulate an operator without

being

able

to

compel

operational

cooperation

during

crisis

or

conflict.

Leverage

reduces

dependency

but

does

not

eliminate

it.

Structural Power (SP) Structural Power refers to the ability of infrastructural operators to shape, constrain, or condition state

action

through

their

control

of

essential

systems.

Structural

power

is

not

political

lobbying

or

negotiation;

it

is

operational.

Operators

possess

structural

power

when:

● government cannot act without their systems ● substitution is infeasible ● regulatory leverage is insufficient ● dependency is high Structural power is the inverse of agency. As structural power increases, government agency decreases.

Interdependence of the Four Components The four components interact to determine the GAI (Government Agency Index). Their relationships

are

non ‑ linear

and

context ‑ dependent:

● High ID + Low SC → severe constraint ● High ID + High RL → moderated constraint ● High SP + Low RL → operator dominance ● High SC + High RL → strong agency These interactions form the conceptual logic of the GAUID/GAI framework. They explain why governments

with

strong

institutions

may

still

experience

infrastructural

constraint

and

why

infrastructural

operators

can

possess

operational

veto

power

even

without

political

conflict.

Chapter 5 — Mathematical Formulation of the

GAUID/GAI

The conceptual architecture introduced in Chapter 4 becomes analytically operational only when translated

The mathematical formulation is built around the four core components: ● Infrastructure Dependency ● Substitution Capacity ● Regulatory Leverage ● Structural Power

Each component is represented as a normalized value between 0 and 1, allowing cross ‑ sector and

cross ‑ national

comparison.

1. Core Variables Let: ● ID ∈ [0,1] = Infrastructure Dependency ● SC ∈ [0,1] = Substitution Capacity ● RL ∈ [0,1] = Regulatory Leverage ● SP ∈ [0,1] = Structural Power ● GAI = f(ID, SC, RL, SP) Agency is represented as: [ GAI = f(ID, SC, RL, SP) ] The function ( f ) is non ‑ linear because infrastructural constraint is non ‑ linear.

2. Base Interaction Structure The foundational relationship is: GAI = (1 − ID) + SC + RL − SP This expresses the intuitive logic: ● Higher ID reduces agency ● Higher SC increases agency ● Higher RL increases agency ● Higher SP reduces agency But this linear form is insufficient. It does not capture threshold effects, compounding constraints,

or

operator

veto

power.

Thus, the model introduces interaction terms .

3. Interaction Terms

Dependency × Substitution Interaction I_DS = ID · (1 − SC) High dependency combined with low substitution produces exponential constraint . Dependency × Structural Power Interaction I_DP = ID · SP This captures the condition where operators gain veto power because the state cannot act without

them.

Regulatory Leverage × Structural Power Interaction I_LP = SP · (1 − RL) This expresses the idea that structural power is most dangerous when regulatory leverage is weak.

4. Threshold Dynamics The model includes a threshold function: T = {1 if ID > 0.7 and SC < 0.3; 0 otherwise} When triggered, ( T = 1 ) represents infrastructural lock ‑ in , a condition where agency collapses

regardless

of

regulatory

leverage.

This reflects real ‑ world cases such as: ● Cloud platform lock ‑ in ● Telecommunications monopoly regions ● Logistics chokepoints ● Payment system dominance

5. Full GAUID/GAI Equation The complete model integrates all components: GAI = (1 − ID) + SC + RL − SP − I_DS − I_DP − I_LP − T

Where: ● I_DS = ID(1−SC), I_DP = ID·SP, I_LP = SP(1−RL), T = threshold lock-in. The result is normalized to: GAI ∈ [0,1] A value of: ● 0.8–1.0 = strong agency ● 0.5–0.79 = moderate agency ● 0.3–0.49 = constrained agency ● 0–0.29 = severe infrastructural dependency

6. Interpretation The mathematical model reveals several non ‑ obvious insights: ● High regulatory leverage cannot compensate for low substitution capacity. ● Structural power amplifies dependency more than dependency amplifies structural power. ● Threshold lock ‑ in is the most dangerous condition because it collapses agency even when

to

either

capacity

or

autonomy.

3.2 Infrastructure Dependency (ID) Infrastructure Dependency measures the extent to which critical governmental functions

rely

on

systems

that

the

government

does

not

fully

control

or

readily

replace.

Dependency

is

sectoral

because

different

infrastructures

have

different

ownership

structures,

concentration

levels,

switching

costs,

and

public

alternatives.

3.3 Substitution Capacity (SC) Substitution Capacity measures the availability, speed, feasibility, and scalability of alternatives

when

an

incumbent

infrastructure

provider

becomes

unavailable

or

non-cooperative.

Public

is

one

of

substitution

but

is

not

the

only

interoperability,

multiple

private

providers,

geographic

redundancy,

stockpiles,

and

interoperable

emergency

arrangements

can

increase

3.4 Regulatory Leverage (RL) Regulatory Leverage measures the government's demonstrated ability to secure compliance,

impose

sanctions,

enforce

decisions,

and

maintain

cooperation

when

private

actors

have

incentives

to

resist.

RL

is

therefore

broader

than

the

number

of

enforcement

actions.

3.5 Structural Power (SP)

Structural Power captures power generated by the relationship between dependency and

control.

It

is

divided

into

operational

and

structural

components. SP = α SP_operational + β SP_structural, α + β = 1 Operational structural power arises from control over infrastructure the government requires.

Structural

political

power

arises

from

the

ability

to

shape

rules,

standards,

expectations,

lobbying

environments,

and

institutional

arrangements.

3.6 The central mechanism High ID + Low SC + Low RL → High SP → Lower Effective Agency The mechanism is conditional rather than deterministic. High dependency can be offset

by

high

substitution

capacity

or

high

regulatory

leverage.

The

universally.

a

formal

institutional

property.

The

framework

begins

from

a

simple

observation:

governments

execute

authority

through

infrastructures,

and

the

degree

of

control

over

those

infrastructures

affects

the

range

of

feasible

governmental

action. The revised mathematical architecture corrects the central directionality problem in the model.

Infrastructure

Dependency

reduces

agency,

while

Substitution

Capacity

and

Regulatory

Leverage

increase

it.

All

primary

variables

are

normalized

to

a

common

interval,

and

Structural

Power

is

modeled

separately

as

an

emergent

mechanism

rather

than

as

an

unstructured

additive

component. The Canadian application demonstrates how the model can be populated from sectoral evidence

while

explicitly

distinguishing

observed

data

from

researcher-assigned

parameters.

The

provisional

Canada

calculation

produces

a

baseline

GAI

of

approximately

22.3/100

under

equal

weights.

That

result

is

not

presented

as

a

final

national

estimate;

it

is

a

reproducible

demonstration

awaiting

empirical

calibration

of

weights,

regulatory

leverage,

structural

power,

and

outcome

relationships.

The framework also specifies nonlinear interactions, threshold effects, crisis coding, sectoral

wage-setting,

comparative

validation,

and

explicit

falsification

conditions.

These

additions

transform

GAUID

from

a

conceptual

proposition

into

a

testable

research

program. The central proposition can therefore be stated precisely: formal governmental authority

can

remain

intact

while

operational

agency

declines

when

critical

infrastructures

are

externally

controlled,

difficult

to

substitute,

and

difficult

to

regulate.

Conversely,

strong

substitution

capacity

and

regulatory

leverage

can

buffer

dependency

and

preserve

agency

(Evans,

1995;

Farrell

&

Newman,

2019). GAUID's contribution is therefore not the claim that private actors influence states—a proposition

already

well

established

in

the

literature.

Rather,

it

provides

a

formal

architecture

for

measuring

when

infrastructural

dependence

becomes

an

operational

constraint

on

government

agency,

how

that

constraint

changes

over

time,

and

under

what

conditions

it

can

produce

drift,

vulnerability,

or

collapse.

X.7 Integration Guide

•

Replace any legacy additive GAI with the normalized directionally consistent specification.

•

Normalize all component variables to [0,1] before aggregation. •

Replace enforcement-count ratios with a multidimensional RL construct. •

Treat wage-setting as a conditional functional subsystem rather than an automatic infrastructure

Estimate interaction terms and thresholds from independent outcome data. •
