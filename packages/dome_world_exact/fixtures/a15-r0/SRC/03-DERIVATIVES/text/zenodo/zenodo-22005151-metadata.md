    "description": "<p><strong>Policy Contradiction</strong> is a SignalRupture political‑systems operator that identifies when a government increases downstream enforcement or measurement without first increasing the upstream capacity required to meet the new demand. In this case, Ontario&rsquo;s mandatory secondary examinations (enabled by Bill 101) intensify assessment pressure while real per‑student instructional capacity is historically constrained and household preparation capacity is unequal. The contradiction does not claim that examinations are invalid; it shows that a universal exam can measure both learning and unequal access to the conditions that produce learning. When enforcement (E) rises faster than institutional capacity (C), the residual preparation load transfers to households (B), where socioeconomic, disability, time, and digital‑access inequalities (H) determine who absorbs the burden. The result is a structural risk: the policy may propagate the very performance gaps it is intended to correct.</p>\n<p>This case contributes to <strong>Systems Propagation Research</strong> by demonstrating the political form of propagation: <strong>policy load &rarr; institutional absorption &rarr; burden transfer &rarr; unequal exposure &rarr; downstream measurement &rarr; upstream justification</strong>. It extends SR&rsquo;s propagation architecture beyond physical systems (e.g., Suez backlog) into institutional and household systems. The paper introduces four operational concepts &mdash; <strong>policy load</strong>, <strong>absorption capacity</strong>, <strong>burden transfer</strong>, and <strong>contradiction closure</strong> &mdash; which formalize how enforcement can become self‑reinforcing when upstream capacity is insufficient. It also provides a falsifiable <strong>Policy Contradiction Propagation Model</strong>, showing how examination enforcement interacts with school capacity, household inequality, and accommodation systems. The contribution is not a prediction of failure but a conditional structural law: <strong>when policy enforcement grows faster than institutional absorption capacity, residual policy load propagates downstream, and where downstream capacity is unequal, universal policy produces unequal exposure to consequences.</strong> This case therefore strengthens SR&rsquo;s political field by offering measurable indicators, prospective tests, and a unified mechanism linking <strong>Policy Contradiction</strong> to <strong>Burden Transfer</strong>, <strong>EAO</strong>, and <strong>SSO</strong> across the education system.</p>",
