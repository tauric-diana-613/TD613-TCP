EMPIRICAL EPISTEMIC CASE STUDY The Student Missing from the Model Exposure-Silo Bias in explanations of PISA 2022 mathematics

performance

Collapse Psychology × Systems Propagation Research Case Study 04 | August 2026 Central finding PISA 2022 does not reveal one isolated mathematics problem. It reveals a student

simultaneously

situated

inside

socioeconomic

inequality,

digital

distraction,

bullying,

food

insecurity,

classroom

disorder,

anxiety,

and

unequal

support.

When

any

one

exposure

is

reported

as

the

explanation,

the

dataset

is

epistemically

narrowed

even

if

the

statistical

estimate

is

technically

correct.

The

resulting

bias

lies

in

the

transition

from

a

partial

estimate

to

a

whole-person

explanation.

The exact SR/Collapse Psychology term is Exposure-Silo Bias (ESB): distortion or

incompleteness

produced

when

structurally

co-occurring

exposures

are

modeled

separately

and

single-domain

estimates

are

interpreted

as

independent

or

sufficient.

Abstract This case study tests Exposure-Silo Bias using OECD PISA 2022 mathematics evidence.

Public

reporting

often

isolates

digital

distraction,

bullying,

socioeconomic

disadvantage,

food

insecurity,

school

climate,

or

pandemic

disruption

as

distinct

explanations.

Yet

these

exposures

occur

within

the

same

student

population

and

may

overlap,

interact,

mediate

one

another,

or

share

upstream causes. OECD summaries report that 30% of students experience digital

distraction,

20%

are

bullied

at

least

a

few

times

per

month,

and

8%

experience

food

insecurity.

Socioeconomically

advantaged

students

score

93

mathematics

points

above

disadvantaged

students

on

average,

and

disadvantaged

students

are

seven

times

more

likely

not

to

reach

basic

proficiency.

Students

reporting

frequent

distraction

by

other

students’

digital-device

use

score

15

points

lower

even

after

adjustment

for

student

and

school

socioeconomic

profile.

Using

probability

bounds,

this

paper

estimates

that

at

least

30%

and

at

most

58%

of

the

OECD

student

population

experienced

one

or

more

of

the

three

reported

exposures,

absent

information

about

overlap.

This

bounded

compound-exposure

window

is

valid;

adding

prevalence

rates

or

effect

sizes

as

if

independent

is

not.

The

case

confirms

an

epistemic

failure

mode:

accurate

silo

estimates

can

produce

an

inaccurate

account

of

the

person

when

omitted

co-exposures

are

treated

as

nonexistent.

1. Construct definition Exposure-Silo Bias is the distortion or incompleteness that occurs when structurally

co-occurring

exposures

are

separated

into

analytic

silos

and

the

estimate

from

one

silo

is

interpreted

as

an

independent,

sufficient,

or

person-level

explanation.

ESB

does

not

mean

that

single-variable

studies

are

invalid.

Isolation

is

often

necessary

for

identification.

The

bias

begins

when

the

boundary

of

the

model

is

forgotten

during

interpretation.

The distinction is: A partial estimate may be statistically valid within its model and epistemically

incomplete

as

an

account

of

the

student.

In Collapse Psychology, the unit of analysis is the person nested within an institutional

and

material

environment

over

time.

In

Systems

Propagation

Research,

the

question

is

how

exposures

move,

accumulate,

and

change

form

across

connected

domains.

ESB

is

the

failure

that

appears

when

those

connections

are

removed

from

the

explanatory

surface.

2. Why PISA 2022 is a suitable case PISA 2022 assessed mathematics during the COVID-19 period and collected extensive

student,

school,

and

contextual

information.

The

OECD

database

contains

individual

student

responses,

school-principal

responses,

plausible

achievement

values,

survey

weights,

and

indices

covering

socioeconomic

status,

school

climate,

digital

practices,

belonging,

anxiety,

bullying,

food

insecurity,

learning

time,

and

home

support.

It

therefore

contains

both

the

temptation

to

isolate

exposures

and

the

architecture

required

to

study

them

jointly.

•

Shared population: multiple exposure domains were measured within one international

assessment

programme.

•

Common outcome: mathematics performance provides a consistent dependent

variable.

•

Temporal boundary: the 2022 administration occurred after extensive pandemic

disruption

but

cannot

attribute

all

decline

to

school

closures.

•

Public salience: technology, bullying, poverty, and COVID are frequently converted

into

competing

explanations.

•

Replicability: OECD provides public-use data, codebooks, weights, and technical

documentation.

4. The epistemic error 4.1 Silo estimate A silo model asks whether mathematics performance differs with digital distraction

after

selected

controls.

This

is

a

legitimate

question.

OECD

reports

a

15-point

difference

associated

with

frequent

distraction

by

other

students’

digital-device

use

after

accounting

for

student

and

school

socioeconomic

profile.

The

estimate

is

more

careful

than

a

raw

correlation.

4.2 Silo interpretation The error occurs when the estimate becomes “technology caused the mathematics

decline”

or

when

a

device

ban

is

treated

as

a

sufficient

recovery

strategy.

Adjustment

for

socioeconomic

profile

does

not

reconstruct

food

insecurity,

bullying,

sleep,

anxiety,

school

disorder,

parental

availability,

prior

achievement,

platform

exposure

outside

school,

or

interactions

among

them.

A

control

variable

can

reduce

confounding

without

restoring

the

whole

student.

4.3 System interpretation A system model asks how digital distraction is produced and what it co-occurs with.

Peer-device

distraction

may

be

embedded

in

classroom

disorder;

night-time

use

may

influence

sleep;

bullying

may

occur

through

devices;

household

stress

may

change

supervision;

anxiety

may

increase

compulsive

checking;

school

enforcement

capacity

may

moderate

every

link.

Technology

remains

a

real

exposure,

but

it

is

repositioned

inside

a

compound

system.

5. Compound Exposure Measurement Window The OECD headline prevalences permit a bounded inference even without overlap

tables.

Let

D

be

digital

distraction

(0.30),

B

bullying

(0.20),

and

F

food

insecurity (0.08). The prevalence of exposure to at least one condition is bounded

by

the

Fréchet

inequalities:

max(P(D), P(B), P(F)) ≤ P(D ∪ B ∪ F) ≤ min[1, P(D)+P(B)+P(F)] 0.30 ≤ P(D ∪ B ∪ F) ≤ 0.58 Therefore, based on these three reported indicators alone, between 30% and 58%

of

students

experienced

at

least

one

exposure.

The

lower

bound

assumes

maximal

nesting:

every

bullied

and

food-insecure

student

is

already

inside

the

digitally

distracted

group.

The

upper

bound

assumes

no

overlap.

Reality

lies

between

them.

6. What cannot be calculated The 93-point socioeconomic gap, 15-point distraction difference, bullying prevalence,

and

food-insecurity

prevalence

cannot

be

added

into

a

“total

harm

score.”

They

use

different

scales,

contrasts,

conditioning

sets,

and

causal

positions.

Socioeconomic

disadvantage

may

precede

or

moderate

other

exposures;

food

insecurity

may

mediate

economic

effects;

bullying

and

distraction

may

interact;

classroom

disorder

may

confound

both.

Summing

them

would

manufacture

precision

and

double-count

shared

pathways.

Likewise, the 30%–58% union window cannot tell us how many students experienced

all

exposures,

whether

effects

were

synergistic,

or

whether

the

same

students

produced

each

performance

difference.

The

window

is

a

disciplined statement of what the marginals permit—not a substitute for joint analysis.

7. Testing Exposure-Silo Bias directly ESB becomes empirically testable by comparing nested models and changes in interpretation

rather

than

merely

listing

omitted

variables.

•

Model S1 — single exposure: mathematics performance predicted by digital distraction.

•

Model S2 — adjusted silo: add socioeconomic profile and basic demographics.

•

Model S3 — compound exposure: add bullying, food insecurity, classroom climate,

anxiety,

belonging,

learning

time,

and

home

support.

•

Model S4 — system model: add pre-specified interactions, school random effects,

and

pathways

consistent

with

temporal

ordering.

•

Model S5 — longitudinal or quasi-experimental extension: test whether exposure

change

precedes

within-person

or

cohort

change

in

performance. Define the Exposure-Silo Distortion for exposure j as the change in its standardized

coefficient

when

moving

from

the

adjusted

silo

to

the

compound

model:

ESD  = |β  ,S2 − β  ,S3| / max(| β  ,S2|, ε) A large ESD indicates that the apparent exposure effect is sensitive to restoring co-exposures.

A

small

ESD

does

not

eliminate

ESB

if

the

interpretation

still

treats

the

exposure

as

sufficient.

Statistical

stability

and

epistemic

sufficiency

are

separate

tests.

8. Propagation architecture The case also shows why ESB matters to Systems Propagation Research. Exposures

do

not

merely

accumulate;

they

can

transform

one

another:

Economic strain → food insecurity and reduced supervision → sleep/attention

disruption

→

classroom

difficulty

→

anxiety

or

withdrawal

→

lower

performance

→

intensified

monitoring

This is a hypothesized pathway, not a causal result from the published marginals. Its

value

is

to

specify

what

must

be

measured.

If

research

observes

only

the

final

device

use

or

final

test

score,

upstream

transformations

disappear

and

downstream

regulation

targets

the

most

visible

node.

9. Falsification The ESB interpretation would be weakened if a preregistered compound model showed

that

single-exposure

estimates

remained

stable

across

plausible

co-exposure

specifications;

interactions

were

negligible;

exposure

clustering

was

minimal;

joint

models

did

not

improve

out-of-sample

prediction

or

explanatory

adequacy;

and

policy

effects

targeted

at

one

exposure

generalized

across

socioeconomic,

disability,

and

school-capacity

groups.

ESB

is

not

protected

from

evidence

by

claiming

that

everything

is

connected.

10. Findings •

Confirmed: PISA reports several prevalent, performance-relevant exposures

within

the

same

assessment

population.

•

Confirmed: single-exposure narratives omit measured co-exposures and their

potential

pathways.

•

Confirmed: marginal prevalences support a 30%–58% window for exposure to

at

least

one

of

digital

distraction,

bullying,

or

food

insecurity.

•

Not confirmed: the amount of overlap, causal interaction, or combined performance

effect.

•

Not confirmed: technology, bullying, food insecurity, or COVID as a singular cause

of

mathematics

decline.

•

Field contribution: ESB separates validity inside a model from sufficiency of the

model

as

an

account

of

the

person.

11. Conclusion PISA 2022 demonstrates how an epistemic distortion can be built from accurate statistics.

Digital

distraction

is

real.

Bullying

is

real.

Food

insecurity

is

real.

Socioeconomic

inequality

is

real.

Each

can

be

estimated

separately,

yet

no

student

experiences

them

as

separate

journal

sections.

Exposure-Silo

Bias

begins

when

analytic

separation

is

converted

into

ontological

separation—when

what

the

model

did

not

include

is

treated

as

absent

from

the

person.

The bounded result is already meaningful: between 30% and 58% of students experienced

at

least

one

of

three

widely

reported

exposures,

while

the

extent

of

co-exposure

remains

unidentified

from

published

marginals.

The

correct

response

is

neither

to

add

incompatible

effects

nor

abandon

identification.

It

is

to

preserve

the

boundary

of

each

estimate,

measure

overlap

directly,

compare

silo

and

system

models,

and

keep

the

person

inside

the

explanation.
