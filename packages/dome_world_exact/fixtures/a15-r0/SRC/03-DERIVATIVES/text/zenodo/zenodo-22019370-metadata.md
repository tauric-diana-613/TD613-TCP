    "description": "<h1><strong>The Student Missing from the Model: Empirical Epistemic Case Study </strong></h1>\n<p>**PISA 2022 does not reveal one mathematics problem; it reveals a student living inside multiple simultaneous exposures &mdash; socioeconomic inequality, digital distraction, bullying, food insecurity, classroom disorder, anxiety, and unequal support. Exposure‑Silo Bias (ESB) is the SR operator that detects the epistemic distortion created when these structurally co‑occurring exposures are separated into analytic silos and any single‑domain estimate is interpreted as a sufficient explanation of the whole person. The case shows that OECD&rsquo;s statistically valid silo estimates (e.g., a 15‑point mathematics difference associated with peer digital distraction, a 93‑point socioeconomic gap, 20% bullying prevalence, 8% food insecurity) become epistemically incomplete when converted into singular causal narratives. Using Fr&eacute;chet bounds, the study establishes a disciplined compound‑exposure window: between <strong>30% and 58%</strong> of students experienced at least one of digital distraction, bullying, or food insecurity &mdash; without assuming independence or fabricating overlap. What cannot be calculated is equally important: the amount of co‑exposure, causal interaction, or combined effect cannot be inferred from marginals, and silo estimates cannot be added into a &ldquo;total harm score.&rdquo; ESB becomes empirically testable by comparing nested models (single‑exposure &rarr; adjusted silo &rarr; compound exposure &rarr; system model &rarr; longitudinal extension) and computing Exposure‑Silo Distortion (ESD) to detect coefficient sensitivity when co‑exposures are restored. The case demonstrates that digital distraction, bullying, food insecurity, and socioeconomic disadvantage are real exposures, but none is structurally sufficient; each is embedded in a propagation architecture where upstream pressures (economic strain, household stress, sleep disruption, classroom disorder, anxiety) transform downstream exposures and outcomes. ESB is falsified only if compound models show stable coefficients, minimal clustering, negligible interactions, bounded interpretation, and system‑level intervention effects. The central SR finding is that <strong>accurate statistics can produce inaccurate explanations</strong> when analytic separation becomes ontological separation. The correct response is not to abandon identification or fabricate compound effects, but to preserve model boundaries, measure overlap directly, compare silo and system models, and keep the student &mdash; the full exposure architecture &mdash; inside the explanation.</p>",
