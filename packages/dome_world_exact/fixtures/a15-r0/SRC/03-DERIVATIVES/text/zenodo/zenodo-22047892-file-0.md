CHAPTER 2 — WHAT IS A POLICY SUBSTRATE? The term substrate is used deliberately. A substrate is the layer through which higher-order

processes

operate.

In

SR,

is

not

above

as

It

operates

through

infrastructure,

institutions,

markets,

administrative

and

A policy substrate therefore contains the conditions that make some policy actions

possible,

others

and

others

The framework defines six primary substrate dimensions: •

Infrastructure — physical, financial, digital, logistical, informational, and organizational

for

•

Dependency — concentration of essential functions in actors or systems outside

the

direct

of

the

policy

•

Capacity — the material and administrative ability to execute policy. •

Incentive — the reward and penalty structure influencing actor behavior.

•

Visibility — the degree to which relevant causal conditions are observable to

decision-makers

and

affected

populations.

•

Exposure — the distribution of consequences across populations, institutions,

and

time.

These dimensions interact. Infrastructure creates dependency. Dependency alters

incentives.

Incentives

affect

institutional

Institutional

behavior

changes

Exposure

generates

outcomes.

Outcomes

become

visible

through

measurement

systems

that

themselves

may

be

incomplete.

The substrate is therefore dynamic rather than static. 2.1 The Substrate as a Causal Layer The policy substrate can be represented as a vector X_s(t) = [I, Dep, C, Inc, V,

E],

where

The

Y(t) = F[P(t), X_s(t), ε( t)] The conventional policy model may estimate Y as a function of P. SR asks whether

If residuals are systematically associated with infrastructure concentration, dependency,

The framework's empirical task is therefore not to replace existing policy models

4.1 The Causal Depth Test

The Causal Depth Test asks whether an identified policy problem has been traced

through

four

1. Observed outcome — what is visibly happening? 2. Institutional response — what policy or administrative mechanism is responding? 3.

Operational substrate — what infrastructure, capacity, dependency, or incentive

determines

can

4. Generative substrate — what deeper structure produces the operational condition?

The test is intentionally recursive. If the explanation stops at the policy instrument,

the

analysis

has

not

yet

established

depth.

A policy analysis may pass conventional evaluation standards while failing the

Causal

Depth

Test.

SR

does

invalid

it

a

of

explanation.

CHAPTER 5 — POLICY AGENCY UNDER SUBSTRATE DEPENDENCY The central agency question in the SR Policy Substrate is not simply who has authority.

It

who

can

alter

the

conditions

that

generate

the

outcome.

Formal authority can remain intact while effective agency declines. This occurs

when

The framework therefore distinguishes four concepts: •

Authority — legal or formal power to act.

•

Capacity — ability to execute an authorized action. •

Autonomy — ability to choose among meaningful alternatives. •

Substrate leverage — ability to alter the conditions that generate the policy problem.

These variables should not be collapsed into one measure. A government can have

high

authority,

moderate

capacity,

low

autonomy,

and

very

low

substrate

This distinction is especially important in infrastructure-dependent governance.

The index increases when essential infrastructure is concentrated, navigation through

25.1 Endogenization Let X_s(t) be substrate conditions and P(t) policy. Conventional analysis may assume:
